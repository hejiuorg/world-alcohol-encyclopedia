# Egon Müller

Egon Müller - Scharzhof stands in Wiltingen in Germany's Saar valley, at a property called the Scharzhof. Its name is almost the same as the Scharzhofberg: a south-facing slope of blue slate and old vines, with very low yields, whose Rieslings are reference points on many wine lists. Unlike estates with visitor centres, the Scharzhof feels like a private property — the official page gives a place name and an email, and arrival has to be arranged beforehand.![Egon Müller image](/media/photos/place-egon-muller-1c0adf7b.jpg)

Figure: Egon Müller. Image: KarleHorn, CC BY 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons ([source page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Egon_Mueller_2018.jpg)).

## The Scharzhof: an estate in the Saar valley

The Scharzhof lies at the foot of the slope outside Wiltingen, in the narrow valley cut by Saar tributaries. The Saar is a tributary of the Mosel and cooler than the Mittelmosel, with a later ripening season; its Rieslings are sharper, more closed in fruit and tighter in body. The property itself is not large, with the buildings grouped around the old house and cellar, and its low-key appearance contrasts with the reputation of its sweet wines.

The official contact page reads "Weingut Egon Müller - Scharzhof, Wiltingen / Saar" and gives egon@egon-mueller.de; there is no street number and no fixed visiting time. The postal code 54459 in the address field comes from public geographic data, and navigation should use "Scharzhof, Wiltingen". Coordinates should be taken from official channels.

## One family since 1797

The home page carries only one line: "Since 1797, one family", and that is the estate's self-introduction. The family has held the Scharzhof since 1797 through several generations, and the business still appears under the Müller family name. Compared with historic estates bought by large groups, this continuity is part of the style: cellar decisions do not have to answer to an external brand portfolio, and vintage differences can be preserved.

Continuity also means caution. The estate does not chase new styles, the labels and packaging stay plain, communication is minimal, and prices are driven by the market and auctions. Do not expect a "guided experience" here: it is a working family business rather than a site designed for visitors.

For visitors this caution has a practical meaning: there is no laid-out tour and no gift-shop entrance. What you see is the house, cellar and stores, and which areas you may enter depends on your host; that is why an email should state the purpose, buying, tasting or a professional call, since the arrangement differs.

## The Scharzhofberg: a south-facing slope of blue slate

The Scharzhofberg is one of the Saar's most famous vineyards. It faces south and catches the maximum sun; its soil is mainly blue slate, weathered into thin broken stone that drains and warms quickly and forces roots down. The steepness keeps machinery out, so picking and carrying depend on hand labour, and yields are held very low. Old vines here are not a marketing phrase but the basis of stable flavour.

The same hill varies: higher up, the slope is steeper and the soil thinner, giving a tighter wine; lower down, deeper soil gives more open fruit. The estate's Scharzhofberg range is usually sorted by Prädikat rather than split into small parcels, unlike some Mittelmosel estates, which makes the hill itself a single brand.

## Sugar and acidity in Riesling: from Kabinett to TBA

Egon Müller's range covers the full sequence from Kabinett to Trockenbeerenauslese (TBA). Kabinett is light with bright acidity and suits early drinking; Spätlese is riper, with more sweetness and body; Auslese starts selecting fruit, and the botrytis share depends on the vintage; Beerenauslese and TBA rely on botrytis and berry-by-berry selection, with tiny yields, and often reach the market through auctions.

The point of the sequence is not "sweeter is better" but balance between sugar and acidity. The Saar's cool climate leaves Riesling with high acidity, so even a TBA with very high sugar keeps a clear skeleton; without that acidity the wine would feel cloying. When tasting TBA, nose the honey, dried apricot and citrus peel first, then check whether acidity holds the sweetness in the mouth.

## The range, vintages and auctions

The estate works mainly with Riesling, separating sites such as the Scharzhofberg and Wiltinger Braune Kupp, then sorting by Prädikat. Vintage differences are obvious on the Saar: cool years give high acidity and a light body, ripe years give more fruit and easier botrytis. Collectors watch auction lots, because TBA and some Auslese wines are sold only at auction or by allocation and rarely appear in ordinary retail.

Check provenance and storage when buying. Sweet wines are sensitive to temperature and light, and a bottle after a long journey may differ from the one tasted at the estate; old TBA can darken in colour and change in aroma, which is not automatically a fault but calls for careful judgement. For an entry point, buy Kabinett or Spätlese rather than chasing TBA.

## Arranging a visit

The address is Scharzhof, 54459 Wiltingen, Germany; the official page gives only an email, with no booking entry. To visit, write ahead with the number of guests, dates, purpose and whether you are driving, then wait for a reply; do not assume the estate takes walk-ins. Harvest, auction time and weekends are busier, and replies may be slower.

Even with an invitation, keep the tasting small and have anyone driving spit throughout. Saar valley roads are narrow and winding with limited lighting at night, and returning to Trier or Luxembourg takes time. Say in advance if you do not plan to buy, so the visit does not turn into sales pressure. Reception capacity is limited, so contact early in high season and give notice if plans change; that is the basic courtesy for a family estate.
