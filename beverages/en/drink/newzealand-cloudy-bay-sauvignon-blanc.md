# Cloudy Bay Sauvignon Blanc

Cloudy Bay Sauvignon Blanc is the flagship dry white from Cloudy Bay in Marlborough, New Zealand, first made in 1985 and commonly 750 mL at about 13% ABV. It is known for citrus, passion fruit and green-grass aromas and is one of the wines that brought New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc to international attention.

## Definition and position

Separate the levels first: white wine is the broad category, Sauvignon Blanc is the grape variety, Marlborough is the region, Cloudy Bay is the estate and brand, and this Sauvignon Blanc is the specific product. Keeping the four apart clarifies the difference between varietal character, regional style and estate craft.

It is a dry wine within [white wine](ref:white-wine), neither sweet nor sparkling. The name Cloudy Bay comes from the bay near the estate and is a brand name rather than a legal appellation grade. New Zealand wine labelling centres on region and variety and has no Old World-style hierarchy.

## Producer and brand

The estate was founded by David Hohnen in 1985 and now belongs to the [LVMH](ref:lvmh) group. Sauvignon Blanc is its core product, alongside Chardonnay, Pinot Noir, a barrel-fermented Sauvignon Blanc and a sparkling wine, forming a range united by the Marlborough setting.

Estate, group and product are three levels: the group answers ownership, the estate answers winemaking and brand, and the product answers variety, vintage and format. The exact acquisition year and ownership changes should be checked against corporate public material; brand history cannot replace the vintage and batch on the label.

## Region and ingredients

Marlborough lies at the northern end of New Zealand's South Island and is a major region in [New Zealand](ref:new-zealand). Long sunshine hours and a wide day-night temperature range during ripening keep acidity while building aroma. Gravel, silt and clay soils across the Wairau Valley and neighbouring valleys drain differently and affect water supply and ripening pace.

The raw material is [grape](ref:grape), here the [Sauvignon Blanc](ref:sauvignon-blanc) variety. It is an aromatic white grape whose skin precursors convert during winemaking into citrus, passion fruit, green grass and green pepper notes. [Water](ref:water) does not enter the finished wine, but irrigation and rainfall affect dilution and ripeness.

## Production

After picking, the grapes are pressed, the juice is settled cold and clarified, and yeast carries out [fermentation](ref:fermentation). Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc is usually fermented cool in stainless steel to preserve varietal aroma and fresh acidity; some batches use oak or lees contact to add texture and complexity.

The estate also makes a barrel-fermented Sauvignon Blanc with a different craft aim from the stainless-steel line. Fermentation temperature, yeast choice and whether malolactic fermentation occurs all change the final aroma and texture. These details belong to estate craft information and are not always public, so this entry gives no single set of parameters.

## Specification and label

The common volume is 750 mL with [alcohol by volume](ref:abv) around 13% ABV, varying slightly by vintage and batch. The label should be checked for estate name, variety, vintage, region, ABV, net content, bottling information and importer; New Zealand labels usually state region and variety as basic identity checks.

Vintage in wine means the harvest year, which differs from the minimum-age statement used for whisky. Aroma intensity and acidity vary from year to year at the same estate because of weather, and this is not a formal quality ranking; the actual performance still needs tasting and review.

## Flavour and tasting

Typical aromas are grapefruit, lime, passion fruit, gooseberry and green grass, with elderflower and mineral notes in some batches. The palate has clear acidity, concentrated fruit, a light to medium body and a clean finish. Aroma intensity balanced by acidity is the core feature, and warmer vintages lean toward tropical fruit.

Chill well to about 8–10 degrees Celsius and use a tulip-shaped or white-wine glass to gather the aromas. Too cold suppresses aroma, while too warm pushes alcohol forward. Check for excessive green pepper or oxidation notes, the latter possibly related to storage conditions.

## Drinking and pairing

Seafood, shellfish, steamed fish, salads and goat cheese pair well; acidity and citrus cut through fat and lift savoury flavours. Avoid rich red-meat sauces or very spicy dishes, which can mask the aroma or amplify the alcohol.

After opening, keep it chilled and finish within a few days, since the aroma fades with air contact. It is not intended for long cellaring, and most bottles are best within a few years of harvest; the exact window depends on vintage and estate craft. Excessive drinking is harmful to health, and minors should not drink.

## Buying and storage

Buy through regular channels and check vintage, region, importer and anti-counterfeit information. Prices move with vintage, channel and batch, so this entry gives no figures. Different lines from the same estate, such as the barrel-fermented version, should be judged separately and not merged under one label.

Keep unopened bottles in a dark, cool place, lying down or standing, ideally at 10–13 degrees Celsius, away from heat and sharp temperature swings. Bottles closed with cork need humidity and are often stored lying down; after opening, refrigerate and drink soon rather than warming and cooling repeatedly.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating a brand name as a legal appellation grade. New Zealand has no Old World-style hierarchy.
Second, treating the vintage as time in bottle. The vintage is the harvest year, not a cellar-age figure.
Third, assuming Sauvignon Blanc must be drunk young. Most are best early, but cellar suitability depends on estate and craft.
Fourth, equating aroma intensity with quality. Strong aroma does not mean balanced structure.
Fifth, judging authenticity by price or packaging. Channel, label and anti-counterfeit information are more reliable.
