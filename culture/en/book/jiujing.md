# Beishan Jiujing (Classic of Wine)

The Classic of Wine is a brewing treatise by Zhu Gong of the Northern Song, transmitted in three volumes and also known as Beishan Jiujing. Unlike agricultural books that attach brewing to farming, it takes wine itself as its subject: first the history and role of wine, then qu-making, then the brewing process. Reading it shows both how Song writers turned experience into steps that could be copied and which stages still depended on observation and feel.![Beishan Jiujing (Classic of Wine) thematic illustration](/media/photos/theme-wine-spirits.jpg)

## Book information

Zhu Gong, courtesy name Yizhong, lived in the Northern Song. Few details of his life survive, but the structure of the Classic of Wine is clear: the upper volume is a general survey of the history of wine, the meaning of drinking and the principles of brewing; the middle volume is devoted to qu-making and records 13 methods in three groups, yanqu, fengqu and fuqu; the lower volume sets out the brewing process, from preparing raw materials to heating and storing the wine, with several “immortal wine” recipes appended.

The titles Jiujing and Beishan Jiujing are two names for one book, the latter taken from Zhu Gong's style name. When citing it, state the edition used and avoid treating the two titles as two separate works.

## Summary of contents

The book centres on qu and method. Qu saccharifies the starch in grain and yeast then turns sugar into alcohol; Song writers may not have used today's microbiological language, but they knew that the quality of qu directly determined whether fermentation would proceed. The middle volume records, for different kinds of qu, the proportions of raw materials, the water added, treading, culturing and drying, with stress on season and temperature; the lower volume sets out steaming rice, cooling, mixing with qu, placing in jars, fermentation, pressing and heat treatment in order.

These steps are not abstract formulas. The text tells readers to observe smell, colour and feel, and explains remedies when something fails. It records a tradition using rice as raw material, qu as the driving force and pottery as the main vessel; this is related to the technical lineage of today's [yellow wine](ref:yellow-wine), but it cannot be equated directly with standardised modern factory processes.

The lower volume's headings are themselves a process list: soaking the starter liquor, washing rice, boiling the sour liquor, tempering rice, steaming the fermented mash, adding qu, starting fermentation, preparing the mash, steaming sweet mash, adding the fermented liquor, pressing, collecting the wine and boiling it for keeping. The names differ but the functions are clear: sour-liquor handling, starch gelatinisation, yeast build-up, pressing and heat treatment for storage.

## Editions and transmission

Later bibliographies usually list the Classic of Wine under the title Beishan Jiujing, and the Qing Siku Quanshu included the three-volume text. Modern editions with punctuation, annotation and translation make it easier for general readers to compare; different editions may differ in wording, the division of chapters and annotations. When citing a specific process, name the edition used rather than saying only that an old book records it.

It is also necessary to distinguish works with similar titles. Su Shi wrote a Dongpo Jiujing of different length and approach; Zhu Gong's book is sometimes shortened to Jiujing, and the two are easily confused in searches. Author, number of volumes and chapter structure are more reliable than title alone.

The Siku Quanshu Zongmu places Beishan Jiujing in the catalogue sub-class and, while approving its qu-making and brewing methods as “fairly detailed”, also notes that its immortal-wine material leans on esoteric arts. That verdict suggests the right reading: use the process records and separate out the magic.

## Value and limits

Its value lies first in completeness. Qu-making and brewing sit in one book, so a reader can follow a single production chain instead of getting scattered recipes. Second, it is practical: its account of materials, vessels, temperature and time is enough to reconstruct an approximate process and provides concrete material for studying the Song drinks industry. Third, it invites comparison: read alongside the wine chapters of the Northern Wei [Qimin Yaoshu](ref:qimin-yaoshu), it shows how the names of qu, the raw materials and the steps changed over several centuries.

The limits are equally clear. First, it serves rice-wine production in a particular time and place, not modern microbiology; its immortal-wine material has an esoteric flavour and is not a reliable process. Second, ancient units of measure and vessel capacities differ from today's, so copying numbers directly distorts them. Third, the text was copied and printed over centuries, and some wording needs collation; treating one edition as the only original overstates certainty. Fourth, it does not discuss the health effects of alcohol and has no modern food-safety risk framework.

## Who should read it

- Readers who want to understand traditional [qu-making](ref:qu-making) and yellow-wine brewing can start with the middle and lower volumes, then return to the general survey.
- Readers of the Qimin Yaoshu or Song notebooks who want a more focused and systematic brewing text.
- Students of Chinese drinks history, the history of technology and food history can use it as a primary source, together with annotated editions and research literature.
- Anyone hoping to brew at home by the old methods should treat it as a historical document, not a manual; modern home brewing also requires food-safety and microbiological control.
