# Negroni recipe (IBA version)

This is a sourced version of the [Negroni](ref:cocktail) recipe. Recipe versions are kept separate from the cocktail identity, so that other sourced versions can sit alongside without overwriting this one. This page describes only this version's ratio and method, and does not mix in other versions.

## This is a sourced version

The public IBA (International Bartenders Association) recipe lists the Negroni as three equal parts: gin, Campari and sweet vermouth, stirred and strained into a rocks glass, garnished with orange peel. This page follows that version and explains the purpose of each step, so a home bartender can tell where a problem comes from.

A version matters because it is traceable. The same cocktail may exist with different ratios, base spirits or garnishes; without a stated source, a reader cannot tell whether a difference comes from the version or the execution. This page therefore lists the source version separately and does not merge it with other recipes.

## Ingredients and ratio

- 30 ml gin
- 30 ml Campari
- 30 ml sweet vermouth
- Ice, as needed
- 1 piece orange peel

The ratio is 1:1:1. This is not the only correct version, but it is the one used by the public IBA recipe and an easy starting point to reproduce. Make the standard ratio once before discussing adjustments; otherwise you cannot tell whether you changed the "version" or the "execution".

## What each ingredient does

[Gin](ref:gin) provides the frame and botanical aroma. Its relatively high strength holds the drink up; if the gin is very botanical, the result leans more herbal and the sweetness of the vermouth becomes more obvious.

Campari provides bitterness and colour. Its bitterness is not a fault but part of the structure: bitterness gives sweetness a boundary so the drink does not become cloying. Campari's colour also affects the look, but colour says nothing about quality.

Sweet vermouth provides sweetness, a wine base and spice. It adds both water and sugar, rounding out the high-strength gin. The quality of the vermouth clearly affects the result, but this page evaluates no specific brand.

## Stirring and dilution

Pour the ingredients into a mixing glass over ice and stir with a bar spoon until the glass is well chilled. Stirring does two things: it chills and it dilutes. Stir too briefly and the drink stays sharp with closed aroma; stir too long and too much dilution thins the body.

Stirring is gentler than shaking: it adds no large amount of air or ice shards, so the drink stays clear. The Negroni is a short drink that usually aims for clarity and controlled dilution, which is why it is stirred. The amount of dilution depends on ice quantity, ice shape and stirring time and cannot be judged from "how many turns" alone.

## Ice and glass

Strain the drink into a [rocks glass](ref:vessel) that already holds a large ice cube. Large ice melts slowly, keeps chilling the drink and lets dilution happen gradually; crushed ice chills fast but dilutes fast and suits occasions that want a refreshing texture from the start.

A rocks glass is short and wide, which leaves room for a large cube and for the orange peel. It has no stem, so holding the body transfers body heat; to keep the drink cold longer, hold it near the rim or use a coaster. For more on ice shape and temperature, see [Ice and temperature](ref:ice).

## Orange peel garnish

Express the orange peel so the essential oils spray over the rim and the surface of the drink, then place it on the glass. Orange peel provides aroma, not acidity; it does not noticeably change the sweet-sour balance, but it shapes the first impression of aroma.

Do not squeeze so hard that the pith turns bitter. The white pith is more bitter; a short, quick squeeze with the skin facing the drink works better. If you have no fresh peel, it is better to leave it out than to use peel that has dried out.

## Units and substitutions

This page uses millilitres and does not automatically convert to ounces or "parts". Home measures vary in accuracy, and millilitres are easier to reproduce than "a few spoons". Without a jigger, you can use a small cup of known volume as a temporary measure, but check its capacity first.

Substituting the base spirit, the bitter or the vermouth creates another drink, and the same recipe title should not be used without stating the difference. For example, swapping gin for another spirit or Campari for another bitter changes the sweet-sour-bitter balance and the aroma structure. Substitution is allowed, but record it as a "variation", not as the original recipe.

## Common mistakes

First, too little ice. Too little ice under-chills the drink and lets dilution run out of control, leaving it sharp and closed.

Second, stirring too briefly. The glass is not chilled enough and the entry feels sharp.

Third, using crushed ice and leaving the drink to sit. Crushed ice melts quickly and can make the drink thin within minutes.

Fourth, squeezing the orange peel too hard. Pith bitterness enters the drink and disrupts the intended bitter-sweet balance.

Fifth, treating the equal-parts ratio as the only answer. The ratio is part of the version; changing it changes the version and should be stated.

## Version differences

Negronis from different sources may use different ratios, different bitters, different garnishes and even different glasses. Some versions emphasise equal parts, others adjust the gin-to-bitter ratio; some use an orange slice, others only peel. These differences are not about which is "authentic"; they are different versions.

When comparing versions, fix the other conditions first: the same ice, the same glass, the same stirring time, then compare the ratio or ingredient difference. Otherwise you cannot tell whether a difference comes from the recipe or the execution.
