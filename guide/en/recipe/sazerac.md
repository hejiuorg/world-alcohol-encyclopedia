# Sazerac recipe (IBA cognac version)

This page records the cognac version of the [Sazerac](ref:cocktail) as it appears on the public IBA list: 50 ml cognac, 10 ml absinthe for the rinse, 1 sugar cube, 2 dashes of Peychaud's bitters, stirred and strained into a chilled rocks glass with lemon peel. It is not the same recipe as the rye version most bars pour. This page takes the cognac version only as far as a reader can reproduce it, and marks where common practice diverges.

## Why this version uses cognac, not rye

The IBA entry names cognac as the base spirit, and the same entry notes that cognac became hard to obtain after the American Civil War, which is when rye whiskey took over. Rye is therefore not the "original recipe" but a long-lived substitute forced by supply. Most bars default to rye today, so "Sazerac" in conversation usually means the rye version, while the IBA record preserves the older grape frame.

The difference is more than a label. [Brandy](ref:brandy) is made from grapes and brings fruit, floral notes and a softer texture; rye is spicier and drier, with grain and spice in the finish. Once you switch, the sugar and bitters need to move too: the cognac version carries a little more sugar without turning cloying, while the rye version turns sweet and buries its own spice if you keep the same amount.

Cognac is listed here so that the version stays traceable. If you want the rye version, record it as a variation with its own quantities rather than adjusting this page while you mix; otherwise you cannot tell whether a difference in the glass came from the ingredient or the execution.

## Is the absinthe a rinse or an ingredient

The 10 ml of absinthe in the ingredient list is an allowance for the rinse, not a volume that ends up in the drink. The method is to pour it into the chilled glass, turn the glass so the inside is coated, and discard everything that does not cling, leaving only the aroma.

The reason is simple. Absinthe is usually above 60% alcohol (products vary widely; check the label) and carries a very loud anise and liquorice character. Adding 10 ml of it to 50 ml of cognac gives a ratio of 5:1, and the anise would cover the fruit of the cognac, so the drink would taste like diluted pastis. A rinse leaves only a millilitre or two on the glass: the aroma stays, the volume does not.

The IBA method also asks you to add crushed ice to the rinsed glass and let it stand before discarding the ice and the excess liquid. The crushed ice does two jobs: it drops the glass to serving temperature and frosts the outside, and it carries the surplus [absinthe](ref:absinthe) away. Skip the discarding step and the rinse has quietly become an ingredient.

After the United States banned absinthe in 1912, New Orleans moved to the local anise spirit Herbsaint, and both are found in bars today. Herbsaint is sweeter and more liquorice-forward, so the rinse can be slightly smaller.

## How the sugar cube and two dashes dissolve

The order cannot be reversed. Put the sugar cube in the mixing glass first, dash the Peychaud's onto it, then press and stir with the back of a bar spoon or a muddler until it becomes a wet paste with no whole crystals left. Only then add the cognac.

Bitters go first because they are mostly water and alcohol, which wets the surface of the cube and speeds up the break-up, and their aroma clings to the sugar. Cognac is strong but has almost no water, so pouring it in first tends to crust the cube and slow dissolution. A sugar cube is roughly 4 g and takes ten or fifteen seconds of pressing at room temperature to dissolve fully.

If you would rather not work the sugar, 10 ml of 1:1 syrup is a reasonable substitute: the sugar is close, but it brings about 5 ml of extra water, so the starting point of dilution moves earlier and the stirring time should be shortened. This is a substitution, not the original step, and the ingredient table stays written around the cube.

A dash is roughly 1 ml, depending on the bottle and the dasher. Two dashes are an aromatic quantity, not a flavour quantity: they lay a cherry-and-anise undertone under the spirit without making the drink bitter.

## Why the glass goes into the freezer

This drink is served without ice. The IBA method rinses the glass, chills it with crushed ice, discards the ice and strains the drink into the empty glass. Nothing in the glass holds the temperature, so the glass itself is the only cold mass.

A pre-chilled [rocks glass](ref:vessel) does three things: it stops the drink warming after it is poured, which keeps the alcohol from stinging; it holds the anise and lemon oil at the rim instead of letting warm air lift them away; and it builds a thin layer of frost that extends the cold. Fifteen minutes in a freezer is enough, and a thick-walled glass needs longer.

A room-temperature glass changes the drink. The first sip is noticeably hotter and the absinthe rises like alcohol rather than aroma; by the second half the glass has been cooled by the liquid and the drink settles. The same recipe tastes like two different drinks, which is a glass problem, not a recipe problem.

A glass straight from the freezer will fog as moisture condenses on it; that is normal. Do not pour hot water into a frozen glass or shock it under cold water, because thick glass can crack. For more on ice and temperature, see [ice and temperature](ref:ice).

## What separates Peychaud's from Angostura

Both are bitters, but they point in different directions. Peychaud's is bright red, lighter in body, and built around cherry, anise and gentian bitterness that finishes fairly quickly. Angostura is darker and thicker, with clove, cinnamon and allspice up front and a longer, drier bitterness.

Their strength differs too: Peychaud's is about 35% alcohol and Angostura about 44.7%. The same number of dashes therefore carries less alcohol and a lighter aroma from Peychaud's. In a short drink built on only 50 ml of spirit, that difference is magnified.

Here, the cherry and anise of Peychaud's pick up the absinthe left by the rinse. Swap in Angostura and the clove pushes the anise down, and the drink becomes a different style. If you must use Angostura, start with 1 dash instead of a one-for-one swap, and do not add 2 dashes of each in the name of complexity.

Peychaud's is itself a New Orleans product, created by the French-speaking apothecary Antoine Peychaud, and its bond with this drink is as much local identity as flavour. For the general role of bitters in a cocktail, see [cocktail basics](ref:basics).

## From the Sazerac Coffee House to the 2008 designation

Antoine Peychaud was a nineteenth-century New Orleans apothecary, and it is commonly traced to him that his namesake bitters were made in the 1830s. The story that he served bitters in egg cups (coquetier in French), and that this turned into the cocktail, is widely repeated but lacks contemporary records, so it is best treated as legend.

In the 1850s a coffee house bearing the Sazerac name appeared in New Orleans; coffee house was then the local word for a bar. It served this drink with cognac from Sazerac-de-Forge, which is where the name comes from. Who first added absinthe is told in more than one way, and this page does not settle it.

After the Civil War the cognac supply was cut, and rye took its place; in 1912 the United States banned absinthe, and a local anise spirit stepped in. Both substitutions changed the drink and both remain in today's versions. The "traditional recipe" is therefore a line rewritten by supply conditions, not an archive that never moved.

The 2008 designation needs precision. In April of that year a bill to make the Sazerac the official state cocktail of Louisiana was defeated; on 23 June the legislature instead proclaimed it the official cocktail of the city of New Orleans. The accurate phrase is "the official cocktail of New Orleans", not "the official state cocktail". The statute also credits Peychaud as its creator; that is an official narrative, which is a different thing from historical proof.

## Stirring: what 20 to 30 seconds is for

This drink is stirred, not shaken. The reason is structural rather than ceremonial: there is no citrus, egg white or cream to emulsify or aerate, and shaking would add air and ice shards, cloud the drink and make dilution harder to control.

Fill a mixing glass to two-thirds with ice, add the cognac and stir for 20 to 30 seconds. Two signs tell you when to stop: the outside of the glass fogs and feels cold, and the liquid turns in an even vortex while the ice sits slightly lower. At that point dilution is roughly 15% to 25% and the aroma has opened.

Under-stir and the drink is warm and sharp, with the anise of the absinthe poking out; over-stir and the body thins and the fruit of the cognac is washed away. Ice quality sets the width of that window: large, hard cubes melt slowly and leave room to adjust, while crushed or half-melted ice closes the window to a few seconds. See [stirring](ref:stirring) for the principle.

## Lemon peel: expressing oil, not squeezing pith

The IBA garnish is lemon peel. Cut a strip of yellow zest with a peeler and keep the white pith off it; hold the strip skin-side down over the glass, pinch it quickly so the oil sprays across the surface, then wipe the rim with the skin.

Lemon peel gives aroma, not acidity. It does not make the drink sour, but it changes the first impression: citrus oil floats on the surface and reaches the nose before the cognac, lifting the sweetness of the anise. Press too hard and pith juice enters the drink, where its bitterness stacks on the bitters and reads as dirt.

If you have no fresh lemon, leave it out. A dried peel has no oil left and is decoration only. Orange peel works, but it is a variation: its sweeter, rounder oil pushes the cherry note of Peychaud's in another direction.

## Failure points

- Absinthe left in the glass. The rinse becomes an ingredient, the anise covers the cognac, and this is the most common failure.
- Undissolved sugar. Grit sits at the bottom and the last sips turn suddenly sweet.
- A warm glass. The first sip is hot, the aroma disperses, and the temperature curve of the whole drink is wrong.
- Angostura swapped in one-for-one. Clove overwhelms anise; the style changes while the name stays the same.
- Over-stirring or using crushed ice. The body thins and the fruit dilutes into water.
- Pith on the lemon peel. Bitterness stacks and the finish dries out.

## Variations and the limits of substitution

The rye version is the most common variation: replace the cognac with rye, keep the sugar and bitters, and the drink becomes spicier and drier. Some bars split the base half cognac and half rye to keep the fruit while restoring the spice; that is also a variation, and its ratio should be stated.

Substitutes for absinthe include Herbsaint and other anise spirits, with the rinse adjusted to their aromatic strength. Orange peel instead of lemon, or syrup instead of a cube, changes the aroma or the water content and should be recorded as a substitution rather than as the original.

Do not keep the same ingredient table after changing the base spirit. The value of a recipe page is traceability: what changed and why should be written down, so a reader can judge where a difference in the glass came from.

## Equipment, units and reproduction

The equipment list is short: a jigger for accurate 50 and 10 ml measures, a bar spoon for crushing the sugar and stirring, a mixing glass with room for ice, and a strainer to keep the ice out of the serving glass. No shaker and no fine strainer are needed.

This page uses millilitres and dashes and does not convert to ounces. A home [jigger](ref:jigger) is accurate enough; without one, use a small cup of known volume, but check the markings first. The back of a [bar spoon](ref:spoon) is enough to crush the cube, and a dedicated muddler is optional.

The order is fixed: chill the glass, work the sugar, rinse the glass, then stir and strain. Lock those three moves in place and you have a baseline you can adjust one variable at a time; only then does changing the cognac, the bitters or the ice tell you what each step was doing.
