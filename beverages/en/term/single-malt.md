# Single malt

Single malt whisky is whisky made at one distillery, from 100% malted barley, using pot stills. All three conditions matter: one distillery, one grain, one kind of equipment. The term describes a scope of origin and process, not an age, a cask type or a price tier. Treating single malt as a synonym for better whisky is the most common misreading in the market.

## The three conditions

The first condition is a single distillery. Every drop must be mashed, fermented and distilled at the same site; malt whiskies from several distilleries cannot be combined and still called single malt. The second is 100% malted barley, with no wheat, maize or unmalted barley in the mash. The third is pot still distillation, a batch process rather than a continuous column.

Together these conditions define an identity, not a guarantee. Single malt does not promise an age statement, a cask type or a flavour profile. A distillery may marry different vintages and cask types and still call the result single malt, or it may bottle one cask as a single cask. The first expresses house style; the second expresses a single container. They are different levels of description.

## Why pot stills

A pot still runs in batches. Reflux comes mainly from the shape of the neck and natural condensation, so more of the detail from the raw material and the fermentation survives. For the equipment itself, see [distillation](ref:distillation). Malted barley is mashed so that sugars and proteins enter the wash; yeast produces ethanol and esters; the pot still separates these components by volatility, and the operator uses cut points to decide which fractions go into the spirit.

Pot distillation is less efficient than continuous distillation and therefore more expensive, which is one reason single malt usually costs more than grain whisky from the same site. But inefficient does not mean better. The pot still offers a route that retains flavour; final quality still depends on the [barley](ref:barley), the fermentation, the cuts and the maturation.

## Single grain and blended malt

Single grain whisky also comes from one distillery, but the mash may include malted barley, unmalted barley, wheat or maize, and the equipment is usually a continuous column still. Its flavour tends to be lighter and more neutral, and it often forms the frame of a blend. Single malt versus single grain is a distinction of raw material and equipment, not a contest between tradition and modernity.

Blended malt, once called vatted malt, combines malt whiskies from two or more distilleries. The raw material is still 100% malted barley, but the whisky is no longer from a single site. Blended Scotch combines malt whisky with grain whisky. For the logic of combination, see [blend](ref:blend). Each route has its own goal: single malt stresses distillery character, blended malt stresses complementarity between malts, and blended Scotch stresses consistency and balance.

## Regulation: Scotland and elsewhere

Scotland defines Single Malt Scotch Whisky in law. It must be distilled at a single distillery from 100% malted barley in pot stills, distilled below 94.8% abv, matured in oak casks for at least three years with a capacity not exceeding 700 litres, and bottled at no less than 40% abv. These requirements appear in the Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 and are explained publicly by the Scotch Whisky Association. For the wider category, see [Scotch whisky](ref:scotch-whisky).

Other markets do not share that exact legal definition. US federal rules do not create a “single malt” class equivalent to Scotland's, so the phrase there is largely trade and marketing language. In Japan, an industry-body labelling standard introduced in 2021 asks that mashing, fermentation and distillation take place at one distillery. When you see “single malt”, first ask which region's rules, if any, apply.

## Where the flavour comes from

Single malt flavour is a stack of variables. On the raw-material side there are barley variety, peat drying and mashing; on the fermentation side, yeast and time; on the still side, pot shape, reflux and cut points; on the maturation side, cask type, age and warehouse conditions. Change one variable and the result changes. Peatiness comes from smoke used to dry malt, not from added flavouring; cask character comes from oak and its previous fill, not from a sauce.

The same distillery can therefore produce very different single malts. Sherry casks often bring dried fruit, spice and deeper colour; bourbon casks often bring vanilla, coconut and paler colour; heavily peated versions show smoke, iodine and medicinal notes. These are useful tendencies, not promises for every bottle.

## Single malt in the market

On a label, single malt usually appears with three further pieces of information: age, cask type and whether the whisky is chill-filtered. An age statement normally means the youngest whisky in the bottle, not the average. No age statement does not mean young; it means the producer does not disclose a minimum age. Cask strength means the whisky is bottled with little or no water added, so the alcohol may be well above 40% abv, and a few drops of water are often useful in the glass.

Prices for single malt are often higher than for blended whisky because of raw material cost, distillation efficiency, maturation losses and brand positioning. Price is not a substitute for tasting. A well-made blend can beat a dull single malt on balance and complexity, and a young, well-made single malt can beat an old bottle whose cask has overwhelmed the spirit.

## Common misunderstandings

First, single malt is not single cask; the first is a distillery scope, the second a container. Second, it is not one identical batch; a distillery may marry vintages and casks and still label the result single malt. Third, single malt is not automatically better than a blend, because the category describes origin rather than quality. Fourth, “single” does not mean undiluted; cask strength is a bottling choice, and most single malts are reduced to a standard strength.

Fifth, ignore regulation at your peril: Scotland's term is legal, while the same words elsewhere may be custom. Sixth, do not assume every single malt is peated; a good number show no obvious smoke. Read the distillery, the raw material, the age, the cask type and the strength together rather than letting one word carry the whole judgement.
