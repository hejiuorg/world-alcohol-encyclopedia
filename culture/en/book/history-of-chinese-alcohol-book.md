## Bibliographic note

A History of Chinese Alcohol (中国酒史) was written by Wang Saishi, a researcher at the Shandong Academy of Social Sciences, and published in September 2010 by Shandong University Press in Jinan. The book runs to about 430,000 characters; its ISBN is 978-7-5607-4205-2. Shandong University Press issued a hardcover edition in 2022, based on the 2010 text. There is more than one Chinese book with a similar title, so a citation must specify “Wang Saishi, Shandong University Press, 2010” to avoid confusion with general surveys, textbooks or local wine histories.

The book is presented as a historical monograph, not a drinks encyclopedia. It runs from prehistoric remains to the late Qing, organised by dynasty and period, covering the origin of alcohol, brewing, wine administration, drinking customs, famous wines and consumption. The claim that it is “China’s first systematic historical monograph on alcohol” comes from the publisher and reviewers; it describes a gap in the field, not a bibliographic “only” or “earliest.” It should be read as a pioneering synthesis, not a final word.

## Author and context

Wang Saishi worked for many years at the Shandong Academy of Social Sciences on the history of Chinese brewing and alcohol. His method is source-heavy: official histories, notebooks, local gazetteers, poetry, agricultural manuals, medical texts, archaeological reports and excavated vessels are all drawn into the argument. The book examines the origin, producing regions, techniques and quality of famous wines, and it periodises the social roles of alcohol across dynasties.

The book took about twenty years to write. It appeared in 2010, when China’s alcohol market was expanding rapidly and “wine culture” publishing was booming. Much of that output was anecdotal, legendary or promotional; an academic general history of alcohol was scarce. Wang’s project tried to pull alcohol back into the discipline of history: alcohol is not an isolated curiosity but part of agriculture, public finance, transport, religion, literature and daily life.

## Structure

The book moves chronologically and can be divided into four broad periods. The first is origins and the pre-Qin era: prehistoric fermented residues, the legends of Yi Di and Du Kang, Shang and Zhou ritual vessels, and the relation between alcohol and ritual. The second is Qin–Han through Sui–Tang: wine administration, taxes, state monopoly and private brewing, taverns, drinking customs, alcohol in poetry, and the differentiation of grape, rice and grain wines. The third is Song–Liao–Jin–Yuan: urban wine businesses, wine taxes, taverns, ethnic integration, the arrival of distillation and the appearance of shaojiu. The fourth is Ming–Qing: the maturity of huangjiu, the spread of shaojiu, the formation of famous-wine regions, taxes and prohibitions, popular drinking and literati wine culture.

The advantage of this arrangement is that it shows change. Alcohol is not an unchanging object; it is reshaped by agricultural surplus, urban markets, fiscal institutions and technological transmission. In the pre-Qin period, alcohol was closely tied to sacrifice and ritual; in the Song, taverns and wine taxes made it part of the urban commercial economy; in the Ming and Qing, the spread of shaojiu was connected to northern grain structures, distillation and consumption habits. The book handles the huangjiu–shaojiu relationship carefully and does not simply equate ancient alcohol with today’s baijiu.

## Argument and influence

The central argument is that Chinese alcohol history is not a gallery of famous brands but a composite history of production, institutions, consumption and culture. Brewing depends on grain, starter, water and vessels; distribution depends on transport, markets, taxation and monopoly; consumption depends on ritual, festivals, sociability and literature. A legend about “which wine began in which dynasty” cannot explain why an industry rose or fell.

A second argument is “region and period.” The types and styles of Chinese alcohol are not evenly distributed: huangjiu, shaojiu, grape wine, rice wine and fruit wine had different regions and consumers; wine administration, taxes and prohibitions differed by dynasty. Using “Chinese alcohol” as one blanket term erases those differences. Wang uses local gazetteers and poetry to restore them.

The book’s influence is mainly in alcohol-history research, wine writing and industry education. It provides a citable general framework that later scholars can extend by dynasty, region, administration and technology. It also reminds people in the trade that brand stories need evidence; “wine history” is not the same as advertising. A review called it a work that filled a gap in systematic academic exploration, which describes its position at the time without making every conclusion final.

## Limits as evidence

As a general history, its value is in connecting scattered material. Its treatment of wine administration, taxes, prohibitions, taverns, vessels and poetry by period helps a reader locate the features of a given era. It can be checked against agricultural manuals such as Qimin Yaoshu, technical texts such as Tiangong Kaiwu, medical works such as Bencao Gangmu, and local gazetteers rather than relying on a single type of source.

There are four limits. First, a general history cannot be research-depth in every dynasty; a study of a specific region or distillery still needs local gazetteers and archives. Second, archaeology moves on: discoveries after 2010 may revise some origin claims, and the date and nature of prehistoric fermented residues must be checked against the latest excavation reports. Third, “the first” is a publisher’s academic positioning, not a claim to be the only correct account; later studies and books with similar titles should be included. Fourth, the perspective is mainly mainland China, with little on Taiwan, Hong Kong, Macao or overseas Chinese alcohol industries, and it is not a global history.

## Common misreadings

The most common misreading equates “Chinese alcohol” with baijiu. Shaojiu appeared relatively late in Chinese alcohol history; huangjiu, rice wine, grape wine and fruit wine coexisted for centuries. Reading the present baijiu market back into ancient times hides the world of huangjiu and tavern culture. A second misreading treats Yi Di and Du Kang as historical figures with reliable dates; they are legendary symbols of brewing, not verifiable inventors. A third misreading treats “this wine began in that dynasty” as settled fact; the origin of a famous wine often layers legend, local gazetteers and commercial promotion. A fourth misreading turns the book’s “first” into “only”; it is a pioneering synthesis, not the end of every specialist study. A fifth misreading maps ancient wine names directly onto modern products; the same name can hide different techniques and regions.
