# Wine Institute: public policy advocacy for California wineries

Prohibition ended in the United States in December 1933 with ratification of the 21st Amendment, and the California wine industry began to rebuild. Before wineries could sell again they had to deal with tax, licensing, interstate shipping and labelling rules. Few could handle that alone, so the trade pooled its resources into an advocacy body: Wine Institute.

## After Prohibition, 1934

Wine Institute was founded in 1934, when the Wine Producers Association and the former Grape Growers League of California united. The association's timeline places the step immediately after Repeal: Prohibition ended in 1933, and the two bodies merged as Wine Institute in 1934.

Its legal form is a trade association, not a government department. It is headquartered in Sacramento, the state capital, at 915 L Street, Suite 1190, Sacramento, CA 95814. It describes itself as the premier public policy advocacy association of California wineries, and says it is the only US organisation advocating for wine at state, federal and international level. That claim is the association's own positioning.

## A thousand members and an eighty-member board

Wine Institute represents about 1000 California wineries and affiliated businesses. Its history page gives the comparison directly: membership grew from 42 wineries at founding to more than 1000 today.

An 80-member board is drawn from vintners across California's winegrowing regions, small, large and in between, supported by committees and working groups. The board sets policy priorities and a professional staff carries them out. In 2026, Gretchen Roddick of Hope Family Wines was elected board chair for 2026-2027. The association does not publish each member's subscription or voting weight, so outsiders judge from the public member list and board composition.

## Policy, exports and sustainability certification

The first output is policy advocacy. The association works before the California legislature, state agencies, the US Congress and federal agencies, and international bodies, on tax, labelling, interstate direct shipping, market access and alcohol regulation. Its listed achievements include defeating proposals to raise wine taxes at several levels, helping pass the Craft Beverage Modernization and Tax Reform Act, which it describes as the first cut in federal excise tax in 80 years, opening 48 states and the District of Columbia to direct-to-consumer wine shipments, and growing California wine exports to nearly USD 1.5 billion in 2018.

The second output is export promotion. California Wines appears as a single brand in overseas markets, with the association providing market access information, buyer contacts and media relations. The third is sustainable winegrowing. In 2003 the association and the California Association of Winegrape Growers (CAWG) created the California Sustainable Winegrowing Alliance (CSWA), which runs the California Code of Sustainable Winegrowing and the Certified California Sustainable Winegrowing (CCSW) third-party certification.

## The economic figures

The association's industry page gives the following account: 1.1 million jobs nationwide generated by California wine, including 422,000 within California; 248 million cases shipped in 2022 with a retail value of USD 54.8 billion; 6,200 wineries and 5,900 growers; 147 American Viticultural Areas; and 80% of US wine production, making California the fourth largest wine-producing region in the world.

Its home page adds a wider measure: USD 170.5 billion (1705 hundred million) in annual US economic impact, 1.1 million jobs and USD 59.9 billion in wages. The two sets have different scopes. The first describes the industry itself, the second adds multiplier effects through tourism, hospitality and the supply chain. Citations should say which measure is used.

## What makes California distinctive

California runs roughly 800 miles from north to south, and its coast, valleys and inland highlands produce very different climates; the same variety can ripen quite differently from one AVA to another. The association's regional promotion therefore rarely presents California as a single taste, stressing sub-regional diversity instead. For readers, understanding [terroir](ref:terroir) explains that diversity better than memorising a brand.

California is also the overwhelming centre of American wine: four fifths of national output comes from the state. At federal level, the association's positions therefore often represent California wineries and the US wine industry at the same time, which gives it weight beyond a state association. A consumer can start with [how to start drinking wine](ref:first-wine) and then return to the wider category of [wine](ref:wine).

## The built-in limits of an advocacy body

The first limit is representativeness. California has 6,200 wineries and the association has about 1000 members, a minority by count; its weight comes from members' output, brands and exports rather than from numbers. Small wineries and estate tasting rooms may not carry equal weight in its agenda.

The second limit is function. The association advocates, promotes and supports certification, but does not issue licences or approve labels. The federal Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau and state alcohol control boards enforce the rules. The third limit is ownership of the certification: CCSW is run by CSWA, with Wine Institute as co-founder and supporter, so not every certificate is issued by the association itself. A fourth point of contention is direct shipping. Wine Institute has long campaigned for winery-to-consumer shipments; supporters say it widens the market for small wineries, while critics worry about easier access to alcohol, lost state tax revenue and youth protection. The association stands clearly on the first side, and readers should know the argument is still live.

## Common misreadings

- Taking the association for a government regulator. It advocates and promotes; licensing and label approval belong to the federal bureau and the states.
- Taking the California Wines campaign as a quality classification. It is a regional brand, not a wine grade.
- Treating California wine as one style. An 800-mile coastline and 147 AVAs produce marked differences.
- Treating 1000 members as all of California wine. The state has about six times as many wineries.
- Taking CCSW certification as a certificate issued directly by the association. CSWA runs the programme and Wine Institute is a founding partner.
