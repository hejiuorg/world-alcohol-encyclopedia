# Sparkling wine

Sparkling wine is wine that contains carbon dioxide and keeps producing bubbles after the bottle is opened. The bubbles may come from fermentation or from added gas. Under the OIV definition, true sparkling wine derives its carbon dioxide entirely from fermentation (endogenous origin) and has an excess pressure in the bottle of at least 3.5 bar at 20 °C. This pressure threshold separates sparkling wine from still wine with only a slight prickle.

## Where the bubbles come from

The most common method is a second fermentation. A base wine first completes its first fermentation, becoming a dry, relatively acidic still wine. Sugar and yeast are then added, and a second fermentation starts in a closed vessel. Yeast converts sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide, the gas is trapped in the wine, and bubbles form. After fermentation, the yeast residue (lees) may stay in contact with the wine, adding bread, biscuit and nut aromas; this is often called lees ageing.

The traditional method carries out the second fermentation in the bottle, followed by riddling, disgorging and dosage to remove the yeast residue and adjust sweetness. Champagne, some French crémants, Spanish Cava and some Italian Franciacorta use this approach. The tank method (Charmat) carries out the second fermentation in a pressurised tank; it costs less and gives more direct fruit, and Prosecco often uses it. The ancestral method lets the first fermentation finish in the bottle, keeping some sugar for continued fermentation; the style is more rustic and the bubbles may be coarser. Artificial carbonation is not fermentative sparkling wine, and labels and regulations usually distinguish it.

## Champagne is not a synonym for sparkling wine

Champagne is sparkling wine produced in the Champagne region of France and protected by a designation of origin. Only sparkling wine made in Champagne, from permitted varieties, by the permitted process and under the permitted labelling rules may use the name “Champagne”. Sparkling wine made elsewhere may be called “sparkling wine” or “traditional-method sparkling wine”, but not Champagne. Using “Champagne” as a generic word for all sparkling wine is both inaccurate and contrary to labelling rules.

Champagne styles also vary widely. Non-vintage Champagne usually blends several years and several base wines for consistency; vintage Champagne is made only in declared years and emphasises that year's character. Sweetness ranges from very dry to sweet, and terms such as Brut and Extra-Dry on the label correspond to different residual-sugar ranges. [Dom Pérignon](ref:dom-perignon), for example, is a vintage Champagne brand owned by Moët & Chandon, not a separate regional category.

## Sweetness and labels

The OIV gives verifiable sweetness thresholds for sparkling wine. Brut, for example, has at most 12 g/L residual sugar with a 3 g/L tolerance; other grades from Extra-Brut to Doux correspond to different residual-sugar ranges. A sweetness label describes residual sugar, not subjective sweetness; acidity, pressure and aroma all change perception.

Terms such as “traditional method”, “bottle-fermented” and “lees-aged” describe process information. “Blanc de Blancs” means made only from white grapes; “Blanc de Noirs” means made only from dark grapes with the juice kept off the skins. These terms have definitions and should not be guessed from their literal wording.

## Regions and styles

Beyond Champagne, common sparkling wines include Spanish Cava, Italian Prosecco and Asti, French crémants from Alsace and Burgundy, German Sekt and South African Cap Classique. Regulations, varieties and methods differ greatly: some emphasise complexity from bottle ageing, others fresh fruit and easy drinking, and some span a wide sweetness range.

Climate and base-wine choices shape style. Cool-climate base wines usually have higher acidity and suit long lees ageing; warm-climate base wines have riper fruit and suit fast-consuming tank methods. Within one region, base-wine blending, yeast choice and dosage also create noticeable differences.

## How to drink

Sparkling wine is usually well chilled, but colder is not always better. Too cold suppresses aroma; too warm lets bubbles escape quickly and makes alcohol stand out. A wider bowl or a tulip glass releases aroma more easily than a narrow flute; choose according to the wine's complexity.

Start with the production rule. From there, consider the strength, then the usual serving. That is how a practical judgment about Sparkling wine is built.

## Pressure, temperature and bubble life

Bubble persistence depends on bottle pressure, the amount of dissolved carbon dioxide and temperature. The lower the temperature, the more soluble the carbon dioxide and the slower the bubbles are released; as temperature rises, dissolved gas escapes more readily and the bubbles become hurried. Once opened, sparkling wine has a larger contact area with air and carbon dioxide gradually escapes, so it is usually best drunk soon or kept with a stopper that limits gas loss.

Glass shape also affects the experience. A narrow glass helps retain bubbles, a wider bowl helps release aroma; the two are a trade-off, not an absolute ranking. Understanding this is more useful than memorising "you must use a certain glass".

## Common misconceptions

First, more bubbles do not mean higher quality. The fineness, persistence and harmony of the bubbles with the wine matter more than the count.

Second, Champagne is not all sparkling wine. Only sparkling wine made in the Champagne region of France under the rules may use the name.

Third, sparkling wine is not only an aperitif. It can also accompany a meal, especially fried food, savoury dishes and some desserts.

## Boundary

This page describes the category, process and labelling framework, not specific estates, vintages or prices. Check the label, producer information and professional reviews for base-wine proportions, residual sugar, strength, ageing time and drinking window. Sparkling wine is a category of [wine](ref:wine), but its process and labelling rules differ clearly from still wine.
