# Asking Liu Shijiu: Bai Juyi's Cup of Wine on a Winter Night

Some poems rely on grandeur, others on story. Bai Juyi's “Asking Liu Shijiu” relies on a little warmth. The whole poem has only four lines and twenty characters, with no emotional climax and no elaborate allusion; it simply asks a friend, on an evening about to snow, whether he would like to come and drink a cup. Precisely because it is simple, it makes “inviting someone over” unusually clear: wine, stove, weather, time, and an invitation that puts no pressure on the guest.

## The scene in four lines

The poem is short enough to read whole:

> Newly brewed lüyi wine, a small red clay stove.
> Evening comes, the sky is about to snow; can you drink a cup?

The first line is the wine, the second the stove, the third the weather, the fourth the invitation. Each line takes charge of one element, and together they make a complete indoor scene: the new wine still carries its floating dregs, the small red clay stove is burning, the light is failing, snow is gathering, and the host asks whether the guest will come for a cup.

The order matters too: wine, stove, weather, and only then the person. The poet does not begin with “I miss you”; he sets out the objects and the weather first, then issues the invitation. The sincerity of hospitality lies in the preparation, not in the adjectives.

## Bai Juyi and “Liu Shijiu”

Bai Juyi (772–846) was a Tang poet; Britannica describes him as a Tang-dynasty poet who used elegantly simple verse to protest the social evils of his day. Much of his work is plain in language without being casual, and “Asking Liu Shijiu” is an example: there is hardly a difficult word on the surface, yet the structure is carefully made.

“Liu Shijiu” in the title is a friend of Bai Juyi. “Nineteen” is a family rank, not a name; his exact name and life are variously explained. What is certain is that the two were close enough for the poet to issue an invitation in such a short, casual form. For a mere acquaintance, the poem would probably not have been written as “can you drink a cup?”

## Lüyi: the visual detail of new wine

“Lüyi” does not mean ants. It is the layer of dregs that floats on newly brewed rice wine before it is filtered. The dregs are fine, slightly green, and look like a swarm of small ants, so the ancients used “lüyi” to describe them. The term is both literal and a little playful: it does not make the wine sound more refined, but more “new” and more domestic.

What does new wine mean? It may not yet be fully clear, and its taste may not be settled, but its freshness is certain. The poet does not offer a famous aged wine but a pot just brewed, which makes the invitation intimate: not a formal banquet, but a household that happens to have wine. For the craft background of grain wine, see [Yellow Wine](ref:yellow-wine); note that Tang rice wine and today's yellow wine are not identical.

## The red clay stove and the winter night

“A small red clay stove” is another concrete object. Red clay is the colour, a small stove the scale; it is not a great kitchen range or a luxurious heater, but a little stove that can be placed nearby for two or three people to sit around. It gives the scene temperature and keeps “winter night” from being only a cold background.

“Evening comes, the sky is about to snow” fixes the time and weather: it is late, the snow has not fallen, but the air already holds the intention to snow. The word “about to” is deft: the snow is not yet down, the guest has not yet arrived, and the invitation happens at this unsettled moment. If the snow had already fallen heavily, the guest might not come; if the weather were too fine, there would be less reason to sit by a stove. The poet chooses the exact threshold.

## The measure of an invitation

“Can you drink a cup?” is the heart of the poem. The final particle is an interrogative, not a negation. The whole line means: can you come and drink a cup? It is not a command, nor enthusiastic urging, but a question that leaves the choice with the other person.

“A cup” is also restrained. The poet does not say “drink deeply” or “do not stop until drunk”, only one cup. The quantity makes the invitation light and a refusal less awkward. The sense of measure in hospitality lies here: the host prepares the wine and the stove, but does not decide for the guest how much to drink.

## Why a short poem lasts

Twenty characters, almost no adjectives, and yet much is left blank. The poet does not say he is lonely, does not say the snowy night is hard to bear, and does not say how happy he would be if the friend came; he sets out only the wine, the stove, the weather and a question. Readers fill in the feelings themselves, so the poem can feel different each time it is read.

This method resembles a short message today: no explanation, no preamble, just “are you coming?” Its warmth comes not from rhetoric but from specificity: the wine is new, the stove is red, snow is coming, the hour is evening. The clearer the concrete things, the more believable the invitation.

## Common misreadings

First, taking “lüyi” as real ants. It is the floating dregs on new wine, not an insect.

Second, taking the final particle as a negation. Here it is an interrogative, meaning “?”.

Third, treating the poem as a poem of urging someone to drink. The poet asks only for “a cup” and does not push the guest to drink more.

Fourth, taking “Liu Shijiu” as a definite famous person. “Nineteen” is a common form of address by rank, and the exact identity is unsettled.

Fifth, taking the poem as a lively banquet scene. It is closer to a small fireside gathering of two, and the point is “will you come?”, not “how much will we drink?”.

## How to read this drinking poem today

The poem can be read as a model for inviting someone over: prepare the concrete things first, then issue the invitation in a light sentence, without deciding for the other person. Wine here is not the protagonist but the reason for sitting together. For wine's place in seasons and daily life, read [Time in a Cup](ref:time-in-a-cup); to turn “a cup” into a serious tasting, start with [First Tasting](ref:first-tasting).

The poem offers no drinking advice, but it offers a way of receiving a guest: without noise, without pressure, keeping the warmth by the stove and the choice with the friend. That is how a practical judgment about Asking Liu Shijiu is built.
