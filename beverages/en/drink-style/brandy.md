# Brandy

Brandy is a spirit distilled from wine or another fruit wine. Grape brandy is the most common; apple, cherry, plum and other fruit brandies are separate traditions. It belongs alongside whisky, which is grain-based, and rum, which is sugarcane-based, as a distilled spirit category.

## Definition and boundaries

The core of brandy is ferment first, distil second. Wine or [fruit wine](ref:fruit-wine) completes fermentation and then goes into [distillation](ref:distillation) to make a stronger base spirit, which is aged and blended before bottling. It cannot be a macerated drink: fruit steeped in spirit gives a compounded drink, not brandy.

In EU spirit-drink law, "Brandy / Weinbrand" is a protected category name that requires a wine distillate base, a minimum strength of 36% ABV, and at least one year of maturation in oak containers. Cognac and Armagnac are geographical indications under this broad category, bound by stricter regional rules. Fruit brandy belongs to a different category, because its raw material is not grapes.

## History

Brandy's emergence is tied to the spread of distillation in Europe. In the late Middle Ages, wine was distilled into "burnt wine" for easier transport and preservation, and Dutch traders called it brandewijn, meaning "burned wine". In the 17th century, shipping and trade expanded wine distillation in western France, and Cognac and Armagnac developed their own regional traditions.

In the 18th and 19th centuries, transport and ageing in oak casks changed the flavour by accident: the liquid softened, took on colour and gained vanilla and spice. After that, grading, regional rules and geographical indications were established. In the 20th century, brandy became a global spirit category while keeping its appellation-named premium branches.

## Main types

- **Cognac**: grape brandy from its French appellation, mainly from Ugni Blanc and other grapes, double-distilled and aged in French oak. VS, VSOP and XO indicate different minimum ageing tiers.
- **Armagnac**: grape brandy from Gascony, often column-distilled, usually more robust and aromatic; vintage-dated products also exist.
- **Calvados**: apple brandy from Normandy, distilled from cider and protected by appellation rules.
- **Pomace brandy**: distilled from grape skins and seeds; Italian grappa and French marc belong to this tradition.
- **Fruit brandy**: distilled from fermented cherries, plums, pears and other fruit; German-speaking Kirschwasser and Williams pear brandy are common examples.

## Raw materials and distillation

Grape brandy is made from wine grapes, usually varieties with high acidity and moderate sugar, because high acidity keeps the distilled spirit lively. After pressing and fermentation, the white wine must be distilled before it spoils. Cognac traditionally uses copper pot stills for a double distillation, while Armagnac usually uses a continuous column still.

The cut decides the style: heads and tails carry more impurities, and only the heart is the target. The distiller judges the switch by experience, and differences in cut points show directly in the aroma. Pomace brandy ferments pressed skins and stems with water, so it is coarser and depends even more on raw-material freshness.

## Ageing and blending

[Oak ageing](ref:oak-ageing) brings vanilla, caramel, spice and wood, and turns the liquid from colourless to amber. New casks have a stronger effect and older casks are softer. Cognac is usually aged in French oak, with the cask type and toast level decided by the house.

Cognac and Armagnac have different vintage rules: Cognac is usually a blend and the grade indicates minimum age, with VS at least 2 years, VSOP at least 4 years and XO at least 10 years; Armagnac can be sold as a single vintage. Longer ageing does not automatically mean better quality; base-spirit structure and the blending aim matter too.

## Flavour and tasting

Young brandy leans grape, floral and lightly alcoholic; with age it shows vanilla, dried fruit, caramel and leather. Cognac is usually finer and more layered, while Armagnac is fuller and more aromatic. Fruit brandy emphasises the raw fruit, and Kirschwasser and pear brandy often show distinct almond or floral notes.

Taste in a tulip glass: look at the depth of colour, then smell for fruit first, then the vanilla and spice from oak, then dried-fruit notes from ageing. On the palate, check how well alcohol and body are integrated and whether the finish is dry. A few drops of water open the aroma, but do not add too much at once.

## Drinking and pairing

Serve neat in a tulip or balloon glass at room temperature, or slightly warmed. Ice lowers aroma release but can suit a high-strength or younger spirit. Brandy also works in classics such as the Sidecar and Brandy Alexander. As an after-dinner drink it suits nuts, chocolate and cheese.

At the table, Cognac's aroma works with roasted poultry and mushroom dishes; Armagnac's weight suits red meat and rich sauces; apple brandy suits apple desserts and cheese. High strength amplifies the heat of spicy food, so very hot and very acidic dishes are best avoided.

## Common misconceptions

First, equating brandy with Cognac. Cognac is one appellation within brandy, and other regions and fruit brandies are equally legitimate.
Second, that XO is always the oldest. XO is a minimum-age threshold, not a single age figure, and the youngest spirit in the blend determines the label.
Third, that darker means older. Colour is affected by cask type, toast level and caramel colour and cannot be used on its own.
Fourth, that fruit brandies are sweet. Most are dry, and the sweetness comes from fruit aroma and ageing rather than added sugar.
Fifth, ignoring strength differences. Minimum strengths differ by category and market, and the label is authoritative.
