# Vintage

Vintage is the year the grapes were harvested: “vintage 2018” means the wine grapes were picked in 2018. It records the weather, ripening conditions and harvest choices of that year. It is not the bottling date, and it is not the age of the wine. Understanding vintage begins with separating it from these related concepts.

## Definition and boundaries

Vintage is a “harvest year” label, not an “how old is the wine” label. EU wine labelling rules require that at least 85% of the grapes come from the stated year; that threshold shows vintage is a regulated declaration with a proportion condition, not a decorative addition. For products harvested in January or February in the southern hemisphere, the label year is the previous calendar year.

The boundary also separates vintage wine from non-vintage (NV) wine. NV is not missing information; it is a deliberate blend of base wines from different years to keep a consistent style. Only when the legal and product conditions are met does a vintage on the label mean the base wine mainly comes from that year. Treating NV as inferior or suspicious is a misunderstanding.

## Etymology and origin

“Vintage” comes from the French vendange, originally the grape harvest, and later came to mean both the harvest season and the wine of a particular year. The wine trade fixed it as a labelling term; the spirits trade often uses vintage for a distillation year, which is not the same as a grape harvest year. The first step with any number on a label is to identify which system it belongs to.

Vintage rules evolved differently by category. Champagne uses non-vintage blending to maintain a house style and makes a vintage wine only in selected years; Port uses a vintage declaration to mark a single harvest of high quality; whisky age numbers usually refer to the youngest liquid in the bottle. All three use vintage language, but the legal and market meanings differ.

## Mechanism and parameters

Vintage variation comes from growing-season temperature, rainfall, sunshine, disease pressure and harvest timing. A cool year may give high acidity and slow ripening; a warm year may give high sugar and low acidity; a wet year may bring disease and dilution. For a maritime-influenced region such as [Bordeaux](ref:bordeaux), vintage variation is especially pronounced; in stable climates it may be smaller.

Vintage is not a quality ranking. Good and poor years in the same region are relative: a year may favour early-ripening varieties but not late-ripening ones, or the left bank but not the right bank. Reducing vintage to “great year / poor year” ignores variety, site, harvest decisions and producer choices. Vintage is a condition, not a conclusion.

## Practical use in the drinks industry

In [wine](ref:wine) retail, vintage is used to compare styles and prices from the same producer across years; at auction and in collecting, it is a major valuation factor. For [sparkling wine](ref:sparkling), the non-vintage version aims for consistency while the vintage version emphasises one year; Champagne rules require at least 15 months of cellar ageing for non-vintage and 36 months for vintage, which directly affects release timing and price.

In [fortified wine](ref:fortified), Vintage Port must come from a single harvest and must state the harvest year; IVDP certification and bottling rules separate it from ordinary blended Port. On [whisky](ref:whisky) labels, the age number usually means the maturation of the youngest liquid in the bottle, not a harvest year; only a few products state a distillation year separately. Confirm which rule applies before comparing products.

## Related terms compared

The first pair is vintage and age. Vintage is the harvest year, age is the time from harvest or distillation to the present; a wine from vintage 2018 drunk in 2026 is about 8 years old, but it may have been bottled in 2020. The second pair is vintage and bottling date. Vintage records when the grapes were picked, bottling date records when the wine left its container, and the two can be years apart.

The third pair is vintage and non-vintage. A vintage wine emphasises a single year's origin, while NV emphasises a stable cross-year blend; NV is a design choice, not missing information. The fourth pair is wine vintage and whisky vintage. The former is a harvest year, the latter usually an age; reading a whisky's “12 years” as a 2012 harvest is a common error. The fifth pair is Vintage Port and “vintage-style Port”. The first has a strict definition and certification; the second may be only a market description.

## Common misuses and debates

First, treating vintage as age. Second, assuming older is always better; ageing potential depends on structure, storage conditions and wine type. Third, treating non-vintage wine as inferior. Fourth, looking only at vintage and ignoring region, variety and producer. Fifth, applying one year's reputation to every region and every variety.

Debate centres on the authority of vintage ratings. In the same year and region, different producers and sites can produce very different results; one “great year” score can hide microclimate and winemaking differences. Another debate is the truthfulness of vintage declarations: the EU sets an 85% grape threshold, but national and category rules differ, so buyers should combine official certification with label information.

## Typical figures and examples

The EU vintage rule gives a clear threshold: at least 85% of the grapes must be harvested in the stated year; for products harvested in January or February in the southern hemisphere, the year is the previous calendar year. Champagne requires at least 15 months of cellar ageing for non-vintage and 36 months for vintage. Vintage Port must come from a single harvest and state the harvest year; IVDP certification runs from January to June of the second year after harvest, and bottling can continue until 30 July of the third year.

A common calculation: a 2018 vintage bottled in 2020 and drunk in 2026 is about 8 years from harvest but about 6 years from bottling. Keeping the two timelines separate prevents mistaking “old vintage” for “long bottle age”. Likewise, an “18 year” statement on whisky should be read as the youngest liquid, not an average age.
