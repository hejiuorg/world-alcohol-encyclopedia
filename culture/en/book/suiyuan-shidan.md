# Suiyuan Shidan (tea and wine list)

Suiyuan Shidan is Yuan Mei's Qing notes on food, first printed in 1792. It is divided into a list of principles, a list of prohibitions and lists of ingredients, snacks, rice and porridge, tea and wine, recording methods, judgements and food ideas. The list directly relevant to wine is the “tea and wine list”: it does not discuss qu or fermentation, but what wine is good, on what occasion to drink it and how to match it with food.![Suiyuan Shidan (tea and wine list) thematic illustration](/media/photos/theme-wine-spirits.jpg)

## Author and background

Yuan Mei (1716–1798), courtesy name Zicai and art name Jianzhai, was a native of Qiantang, now part of Hangzhou. He passed the metropolitan examination in 1739, was selected for the Hanlin Academy, and later served as magistrate of Lishui and Jiangning. He resigned at the age of thirty-three, bought a garden on Xiaocang Hill in Jiangning and renamed it Suiyuan, where he lived for nearly fifty years as a poet, host and writer.

His circle was wide, including officials, scholars, cooks and monks. Part of the book comes from his own kitchen, part from friends' banquets and regional travel.

The “lists” in the title are not menus but notes and principles. The opening declares that “the way of learning is to know first and act afterwards; the same is true of food and drink”, treating cooking as a subject that requires understanding before practice. That set it apart from the common view of food as a minor skill.

## Editions and structure

The structure sets out principles first and categories second. The list of principles has twenty items on choosing ingredients, cleaning, seasoning, heat and combination. The list of prohibitions has fourteen items, including prohibitions on eating for reputation, eating for show, wasting food, heavy drinking and forcing guests to drink. Then follow lists on seafood, river fish, large livestock, small livestock, poultry, scaled and scaleless fish, vegetables, side dishes, snacks, rice and porridge, and tea and wine.

According to the standard modern edition, the book records more than three hundred dishes and snacks from north and south. The figure is often quoted, but editions differ in how lists are divided and counted, so any number should name the edition behind it.

There were Qing reprints and, in modern times, annotated, plain-language and illustrated editions. The tea and wine list may be split, renamed or reordered. The Complete Works of Yuan Mei, edited by Wang Yingzhi and published by Jiangsu Ancient Books Publishing House in 1993, is a common working edition.

## Core content and food principles

Yuan Mei's food thinking can be reduced to three points. First, respect the ingredient: good produce should not be buried under heavy seasoning, whose job is to support rather than conceal. Second, respect heat: a minute too long or too short changes texture, so he describes the use of fierce and gentle fire rather than giving quantities alone. Third, respect combination: dishes, and wine with food, should complete each other rather than overpower each other.

He also attacks display. The prohibitions on eating for reputation and eating for show criticise those who chase a name or a spectacle without caring what the food tastes like. This attitude lifts the book beyond recipes into an essay on how to eat seriously.

## Wine-cultural value: the wine list

The tea and wine list discusses wine first and tea second. The wine section names wines current at the time, including Jintan Yu wine, Dezhou Lu wine, Sichuan Pitong wine, Shaoxing wine, Nanxun wine from Huzhou, Lanling wine from Changzhou, Wufan wine from Liyang, Suzhou Chen Sanbai, Jinhua wine and Shanxi Fenjiu, judging each for flavour and manner of drinking. The tea section covers Wuyi, Longjing, Yangxian and Junshan teas, with origin, brewing and tasting.

The value of the list is its cross-section: it puts wines from Jiangnan to north China, from yellow wine to spirits, on one table, showing what a mid-Qing literatus could actually obtain and how he rated it. Shaoxing and Jinhua represent the yellow-wine tradition, Shanxi Fenjiu the northern spirits, and Pitong wine a Sichuan speciality.

The clearest statement of his view comes in the prohibition on heavy drinking: “Right and wrong in a matter can be known only by a sober person; so can the goodness of a flavour.” Drink is an aid to company; once judgement goes, neither taste nor argument survives. The neighbouring prohibition on forcing guests to drink attacks hospitality that turns into pressure.

## Limits and controversies

First, the book is personal criticism, not systematic survey. Praise or blame may reflect one man's taste and limited travels, not a national consensus. Second, it does not record complete methods or parameters: it says what is good to drink, not how to make it, and cannot serve as a brewing manual.

Third, ancient wine names cannot be mapped one-to-one onto modern products. “Shaoxing wine” or “Fenjiu” in 1792 may differ from the products bearing those names today in raw material, process and strength, so direct conversion misleads. Fourth, its claims about the properties of food and drink belong to the medicine of the time and are not modern nutritional or medical advice.

Fifth, the text itself is contested. Editions differ in attribution, wording and count, and some famous lines need checking in a critical edition. Citing the edition and the section is more reliable than quoting “Yuan Mei said”.

## How to read it today

Read it as a cultural work rather than a cookbook. Those interested in pairing can start with the tea and wine list and then read the meat and vegetable lists, to see how wine was meant to support food.

Anyone cooking from it should allow for differences in ingredients, stoves and seasoning, turning phrases such as “a little” into measurable quantities and making independent judgements about food safety. As for the wine, the safest reading is a record of mid-Qing drinking, not a buying guide.
