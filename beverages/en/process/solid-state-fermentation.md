# Solid-state fermentation

Solid-state fermentation is a form of [fermentation](ref:fermentation) used in baijiu: the substrate has a low moisture content, the grain mash stays solid, and microorganisms grow in a loose matrix of grain particles, qu powder and filler. Solid-state baijiu is built on this approach, and GB/T 15109-2021, Terminology of baijiu industry, together with GB/T 17204-2021, Terminology and classification of alcoholic beverages, sets out the relevant terms and classification.![Solid-state fermentation thematic illustration](/media/photos/theme-beer.jpg)

## Definition and boundaries

By substrate form, the industry usually distinguishes solid-state, semi-solid and liquid processes. In the solid-state process the mash does not flow; permeability is maintained by turning, loading and vessel structure. Semi-solid and liquid processes ferment in a liquid phase, giving more uniform mass transfer and higher production efficiency.

The three are different process routes, not a moral label of “traditional” versus “modern”. The revision of GB/T 15109-2021 and its companion standards state that solid-state baijiu may not add edible alcohol or colouring, aroma and flavour substances not produced by its own fermentation. That boundary answers “may this be called solid-state baijiu”, not “which process tastes better”.

## Place in the process

Solid-state fermentation is not an isolated step but the middle of a sequence: raw materials are moistened, cooked and gelatinised, cooled, mixed with qu, then loaded into pits, jars or troughs to ferment. After fermentation the mash goes to [distillation](ref:distillation), and some routes steam grain and spirits together or recycle mash through continuous or returned-mash practices.

[Qu making](ref:qu-making) sets the microbial base for saccharification and fermentation; [stacked fermentation](ref:stacked-fermentation) is an open-air warming and culturing step before the mash enters the pit. The high-temperature stacking of sauce-aroma baijiu, the mud-pit solid-state fermentation of strong-aroma baijiu and the ground-jar fermentation of light-aroma baijiu are all solid-state forms under different vessels and temperature regimes.

## Vessels and forms

Common vessels include [mud pits](ref:fermentation-pit), stone pits, ground jars and brick pits. Mud pits stress the microbial community cultured over time in pit mud and are usually linked to strong-aroma baijiu; stone pits are common in sauce-aroma production; ground jars are pottery jars sunk into the ground, stressing isolation, cleanliness and temperature buffering.

The vessel is not the only variable. Grinding degree, loading moisture, acidity, starch content, qu dose and culturing state, and ambient temperature all change the fermentation. Equating a vessel with a flavour ignores these parameters.

## Microbes and flavour

The microbial sources in a solid-state system are mixed: qu brings moulds, yeasts and bacteria, pit mud or jar walls provide long-established communities, and ambient air and tools also inoculate. Open handling makes the flora local and makes batch variation harder to eliminate entirely.

Flavour compounds form in both fermentation and distillation. Fermentation produces alcohols, acids, esters, aldehydes and other precursors and intermediates; distillation collects them in fractions by boiling point and solubility. The unevenness of a solid matrix makes mash at different layers and positions differ, which is why layered collection and quality-based cutting exist.

Quality control comes down to measurements taken when the mash is loaded and unloaded: moisture, acidity, starch content, alcohol and the temperature curve. Higher moisture speeds the temperature rise and can close the aroma; lower moisture leaves saccharification incomplete and lowers yield. Acidity suppresses unwanted microbes and supplies precursors for ester formation, but too much inhibits yeast. Producers record these values with loading temperature, qu dose and grain-to-mash ratio to set the next round of blending and continued mash. Here the general principles of [fermentation](ref:fermentation) become the practical rule of matching water to temperature and controlling it through acidity; specific limits vary by aroma type, season and plant, so the producer's process documents and declared standard govern.

## Comparison with liquid processes

Liquid fermentation gives more uniform mass and heat transfer and easier control of temperature and acidity, suiting stable large-scale production. Solid-state work depends more on manual experience and varies more between batches, but can deliver more complex aroma layers and mouthfeel.

Each route has its place. A blanket claim that solid-state is always better than liquid is neither accurate nor useful for understanding product differences. A more reliable approach is to read the declared standard, product type and producer notes on the label, then weigh them against your own flavour preferences.

## Common misconceptions

First, equating solid-state fermentation with “pure grain”. Whether the material is purely grain, and whether anything else is added, depends on the declared standard and label, not on the word “solid-state”.
Second, confusing ground jars, mud pits and stone pits. Different vessels mean different microbial sources and temperature control.
Third, assuming solid-state always smells better. Aroma complexity depends on management; poor management and materials can also produce off-flavours in solid-state work.
Fourth, treating “traditional process” as a quality grade. Tradition describes a route, not a score.
Fifth, ignoring how distillation reshapes flavour. Fermentation only produces the base; cutting and blending also decide the finished product.

Start with the inputs. From there, consider the sequence of steps, then what changes at the end. That is how a practical judgment about Solid-state fermentation is built.
