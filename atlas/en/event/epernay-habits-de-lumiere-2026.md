# Habits de Lumière 2026 in Épernay: Champagne's Festival of Light

From 11 to 13 December 2026, Épernay will hold the 26th Habits de Lumière. On Friday and Saturday evenings the Avenue de Champagne becomes a stage for light and video projection; Saturday daytime brings gastronomy demonstrations and a pastry competition; Sunday is a vintage-car parade. It is not a wine fair organised around how many champagnes you can taste, but a staging of Champagne's end-of-year way of life in the street. The event is still to come; this event records the dates, venue, programme outline and limits.

## What the event is

Habits de Lumière is jointly organised by the City of Épernay and the Comité de l'Avenue de Champagne, with “champagne-style end-of-year living” (art de vivre à la champenoise) at its centre. The official programme has three parts: light festivities on Friday and Saturday evenings, gastronomy on Saturday daytime, and a vintage-car parade on Sunday. The light programme includes video mapping, live performances, champagne bars, music, light installations and fireworks. It is concentrated on the Avenue de Champagne, with the champagne houses and mansions along the street as the backdrop, rather than in a temporary exhibition hall on the edge of town.

It differs from a champagne trade fair. There is no single tasting pass and no price list of every house's wines; the houses open bars, serve food and run cellar visits in their own courtyards, and visitors pay house by house. Street events are free; most in-house activities are paid. Understanding this two-layer structure, public street plus private house, is the key to planning the day.

The Avenue de Champagne is about 1.5 kilometres long, running south-east from the Place de la République area. Its sides are lined with late-19th-century merchant mansions and production buildings; beneath them are about 110 kilometres of cellars holding roughly 200 million bottles of champagne. In 1925 the street was officially named Avenue de Champagne; in 1994 it was designated a Site Remarquable du Goût; in 2015 it was inscribed by UNESCO as part of the “Champagne hillsides, houses and cellars” World Heritage property. During the festival these numbers are not scenery. They explain why the light is projected onto this street: it is both a city street and a spatial fossil of the champagne merchant system.

## History and mechanics

The first Habits de Lumière was held in December 2000, initiated by the Comité de l'Avenue de Champagne. The original idea was simply to decorate the street with lights and sets for one December evening and let residents and visitors walk an “exceptional avenue”. The event later expanded to three days: light festivities stayed on Friday and Saturday evenings, gastronomy was added on Saturday, and the vintage-car parade on Sunday. 2025 was the 25th edition and 2026 is the 26th; the edition number can be cross-checked on the Épernay tourism and Moët pages.

The organisation is a partnership of city, committee and houses. The City of Épernay handles public space, traffic and safety; the Comité de l'Avenue de Champagne coordinates the houses and growers along the street; each house arranges its own bars, food, cellar visits and tastings. In one event, the street is public culture and the courtyards are commercial hospitality; the first is free, the second follows each house's rules. The detailed 2026 programme is officially due in mid-November; as of 7 November only the dates and outline are available, with no full performance list.

Moët & Chandon is one concrete example. On 11 December 2026, from 18:15 to 22:30, the house holds a “Habits de Lumière” dinner at the Orangerie on the avenue: a sound-and-light show in the garden, then dinner in the Orangerie, with a menu by the house chef Jean-Michel Bardet, at €520 per adult. The Orangerie is a historic building on the Avenue de Champagne, designed in the early 19th century by the painter Jean-Baptiste Isabey. This dinner is the house's own paid event, not a ticket to the festival, and it does not include the street programme.

## Taking part on site

Start by separating free from paid. The city explicitly lists the Avenue de Champagne festivities, Habits de Saveurs (demonstration tastings with starred chefs and houses or growers), the P'tits Pâtissiers pastry competition and the Sunday vintage-car parade as free public events with no ticket sales. What is paid or booked includes the food-and-wine pairing workshops (booking opens in late November), cellar visits at the champagne houses, and the champagne bars and restaurants along the street. Cellar visits must be booked directly with each house, and prices and languages vary.

Friday and Saturday evenings are the core. Light and video mapping usually begin after dark, and the avenue is pedestrianised or restricted to traffic; it is busy, so park in a public car park in the centre and walk in. Sunday's vintage-car parade gathers more than 500 old vehicles and is one of the largest gatherings of its kind in eastern France. If you only want the parade, you do not need to endure the Friday and Saturday night crowds. Saturday's Habits de Saveurs suits anyone interested in food and wine pairing, but the demonstration tastings are usually stand-up around tables, and arriving late means a poor view.

For transport, trains from Paris Est take about 80 minutes to Épernay, and the station is about a five-minute walk from the top of the Avenue de Champagne. Driving on the A4 takes about 90 minutes. During the festival the streets around the avenue close and parking is tight, so the train is the safer choice. Do not rely on the last train; if you plan to stay longer in a house bar, check the final departure and taxi options in advance. December in Champagne is damp and cold, and cellars are colder still; a thin extra layer is more useful than a formal outfit.

## Wine and the city

Épernay is one of the commercial and logistical centres of Champagne. The merchant mansions, production buildings and underground cellars on the avenue turn champagne from a wine grown in vineyards into an urban system: merchants, growers, cooperatives, ageing, blending and export all happen here. Habits de Lumière briefly opens these normally semi-private courtyards and uses light, music and food to put champagne back into the street. For the city, it is an annual self-presentation; for the houses, it is a year-end occasion to thank clients, receive press and test new releases.

In French, habits means habits or clothes. The event name can be read as “dressing light in custom” or as “light visiting the city's everyday life”. Either way, the point is not which cuvée you taste but where and at what rhythm you drink it. The scale of the avenue, the depth of the cellars, the December night and the year-end gift economy together form the setting for the glass. Writing the event up as “unlimited champagne” misses its most important part: urban space and the merchant system.

## Disputes and limits

First, the boundary between free and paid. Street lights, gastronomy demonstrations, the pastry competition and the vintage-car parade are free; cellar visits, food-and-wine workshops, champagne bars and restaurants are paid. Do not assume that because the festival is free, entering a house is free too.

Second, the programme is not yet complete. As of 8 November 2026, the official pages give only the dates and outline; the detailed programme is due in mid-November. This event does not name bands, performance times or wine lists; check the official programme for those.

Third, do not get the edition wrong. 2025 was the 25th edition and 2026 is the 26th; the first was in December 2000. Calling 2026 the 25th or the first would conflict with the official record.

Fourth, the Moët dinner is a separate event. The Orangerie dinner on 11 December, 18:15–22:30, must be booked separately at €520 per adult. It is not a festival ticket and does not include the street programme. Treating it as “the way into Habits de Lumière” misunderstands the public nature of the event.

Fifth, weather and crowds. December in Champagne can bring rain and wind, which affect light installations and outdoor stages; the avenue is dense on Friday and Saturday nights, so visitors with children or limited mobility should allow extra space.
