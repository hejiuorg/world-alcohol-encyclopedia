# Grape

The grape is the fruit of a vine in the genus Vitis. The European grape used for winemaking (Vitis vinifera) and the table grape sold in supermarkets are not bred for the same goals: wine grapes are valued for sugar-acid balance, pigment, tannin and aroma precursors, while table grapes are valued for berry size, texture and keeping quality. The OIV definition of [wine](ref:wine) requires it to come entirely from the fermentation of fresh grapes or grape juice, which shows that the grape is both a raw material and part of the boundary of the wine category.

## Definition and identity

A grape is a fruit; wine is its fermented product. The OIV definition limits wine to a drink obtained by fermenting fresh grapes or grape juice, so "grape juice plus alcohol" or "fermented drink from other fruit" cannot be called wine. The identity of the raw material determines the category boundary and the scope of labelling and regulation.

In content organisation, grape belongs to the ingredient layer, wine to the drink-style layer and a specific wine to the product layer. The ingredient page answers "what is it made from", the style page answers "which category it belongs to", and the wine page answers "what is this bottle". The three layers cross-reference but should not be mixed.

## Wine grapes and table grapes

Wine grapes usually have smaller berries, relatively thick skins and more seeds, with high sugar and acidity. Thick skins mean more pigment and tannin, which suits the skin-contact maceration of [red wine](ref:red-wine); higher acidity gives the wine structure through fermentation and ageing. Table grapes are bred for large berries, few seeds, thin skins and high sweetness, traits that are often not advantages for winemaking.

Making wine from table grapes is not absolutely impossible, but flavour and stability usually fall short of wine grapes. Table grapes have lower acidity and fewer aroma precursors, so the wine can taste thin after fermentation; home winemaking also struggles more with hygiene and sulphur dioxide management. Understanding the two breeding goals prevents the false assumption that "sweeter grapes must make better wine".

## Berry structure and winemaking

The fermentable part of the grape is mainly the pulp, which holds water, sugar and organic acids; the skin carries pigment, tannin and aroma compounds; the seeds contain tannin and oil; the stems also contain tannin. Maceration in red winemaking lets the liquid contact the skins and extract colour and tannin, while [white wine](ref:white-wine) is usually pressed early to limit skin contact, so it is paler and lower in tannin.

The natural wax and attached yeasts on the skin are also a starting point for fermentation and are not the same as pesticide residue. Modern winemaking mostly uses selected yeasts, but some producers retain wild-yeast fermentation for more complex aromas. Stem and seed tannins are coarser, and pressing and destemming choices affect the final mouthfeel, so process choices must match the state of the raw material.

## Main varieties

Common wine varieties include Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc, Syrah, Pinot Noir, Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling and Chenin Blanc. Red and white varieties differ in skin pigment and tannin, and their winemaking routes differ; the same variety can present completely different styles in different regions and processes.

Variety is only the starting point. Cabernet Sauvignon may show herbal notes in a cool region and riper fruit in a warm one; Chardonnay has a neutral aroma, and its style depends more on oak, lees and malolactic fermentation. A variety name sets expectations but provides no guarantee.

## Main regions

The cultivation of Vitis vinifera can be traced to the Caucasus and the Mediterranean surroundings, and it is now planted on a commercial scale in Europe, the Americas, Oceania, Africa and Asia. France, Italy and Spain are traditional regions; the United States, Australia, Chile, Argentina, South Africa and New Zealand form modern regions; China has wine production in Ningxia, Xinjiang, Shandong, Hebei and Yunnan.

Heat, rainfall, day-night temperature swings and soil drainage in different regions change the ripening curve and flavour direction of the grape. Cool regions emphasise acidity and delicate aromas, warm regions emphasise ripe fruit and body. Regional stories need to be read together with climate, soil and viticultural facts, not judged by place name alone.

## Harvest and ripeness

Harvest decisions consider sugar, acidity, pigment, tannin and aroma at the same time. Rising sugar usually comes with falling acidity; when ripeness is insufficient, tannin may be coarse and aroma green; when overripe, acidity is low and fruit feels dull. Producers also adjust harvest timing according to the weather forecast, disease pressure and target wine style.

Sparkling base wines are usually picked earlier to retain higher acidity; sweet wines may be picked later, or left for noble rot to develop. Day-night temperature differences, rain and wind all affect the harvest window. Different parcels, or even different passes in the same parcel, may be picked separately so that fruit at different ripeness goes into different products.

## Viticulture and fruit quality

Viticulture changes fruit quality. Yield control, canopy management, airflow and light, and irrigation all affect sugar accumulation, acid retention and phenolic ripeness. Chasing yield tends to dilute flavour; excessive restriction can leave fruit overripe or short of acidity.

Organic and biodynamic viticulture emphasise reducing chemical inputs and improving soil and biodiversity, but they are not a quality guarantee; the state of the fruit and the winemaking choices still decide the result. Grafting, rootstock selection and pest management also affect vine longevity and stability. Viticulture is a continuous chain of decisions, not a single measure that determines quality.

## Common misconceptions

First, sweeter grapes are always better for wine. Wine needs a balance of sugar and acid; high sugar alone does not mean high quality.

Second, the white bloom on grape skins must be pesticide. The bloom is mostly natural wax and attached yeasts, one of the natural sources of fermentation.

Third, all grapes can make the same wine. Variety, region, vintage and process all change the result; the grape is only one variable.

Fourth, "wine grape" is a botanical classification. It is a description of use, covering many varieties and viticultural systems.

Fifth, organic farming always tastes better. Farming affects the raw material, but winemaking and ageing also determine the final flavour.

**Short definition:** The fruit of Vitis vines; wine grapes are selected for sugar, acid, pigment and tannin balance, unlike table grapes bred for eating.
