# Cognac

Cognac is a grape brandy region in western France, in the Charente area, and the name of the grape brandy made around it. It is based mainly on Ugni Blanc, distilled twice in pot stills, then aged in French oak to produce the VS, VSOP and XO grades defined by minimum age.

## Geography and crus

The Cognac region follows the Charente river and its tributaries, covering parts of Charente and Charente-Maritime. It is divided by soil and climate into crus, including Grande Champagne, Petite Champagne, Borderies, Fins Bois, Bons Bois and Bois Ordinaires. Limestone soils and slope orientation in each cru shape grape ripening and the aroma of the distilled spirit.

Grande Champagne refers to soil and geology, not to the Champagne sparkling wine region, and it is not a quality rank. Cru names help explain where a style comes from, but wineries within one cru still differ, so a cru name is not a quality ranking.

## History and legal status

Cognac export trade expanded in the 17th and 18th centuries with shipping and European demand. Charente wines were high in acid and low in alcohol, which suited distillation and long transport. In the 19th century, glass bottles, grading and brand systems took shape, and Cognac became an internationally known grape brandy.

Cognac received appellation protection in 1936, and production rules have since been managed by the regional body and the French appellation institute. As a protected geographical indication, only products made in the defined area under the rules may use the Cognac name. Name protection confirms origin and process, not a quality rating.

## Grapes and raw materials

Cognac uses mainly Ugni Blanc, along with Folle Blanche and Colombard. Ugni Blanc is high in acid, moderate in sugar and late ripening, and the spirit it yields suits long ageing. The grapes are pressed into a high-acid white wine that goes to distillation, usually without aiming at a drinking wine style.

Planting density, yields and harvest timing are constrained by regional rules. Raw material quality affects the base spirit, but Cognac style comes more from distillation cuts, barrel ageing and blending. Treating one grape variety as a flavour guarantee ignores the later stages.

## Distillation

Cognac uses Charentais pot stills for double distillation. The first run produces a coarse distillate, and the second run, the bonne chauffe, cuts heads, hearts and tails, keeping only the heart as new spirit. The maximum strength of the new spirit is set by regulation, commonly 72.4% ABV, and it is later reduced with water.

Still capacity, heating and cut points are the distiller's decisions and directly shape aroma and texture. Contact with copper brings subtle reductive notes and body, which is one reason Charentais distillation is treated as a style element. Compared with continuous distillation, pot distillation is less efficient but retains more flavour compounds.

## Ageing and grades

New spirit must age in French oak, commonly from the Limousin or Tronçais forests. Oak contributes vanilla, spice and tannin, while barrel ageing slowly lowers alcohol and rounds the texture. Cellar conditions and the proportion of new barrels affect the final style.

Cognac grades are defined by minimum age: VS at least 2 years, VSOP at least 4 years, XO at least 10 years and XXO at least 14 years. Since 2018 the minimum age for XO has risen from 6 to 10 years. The grade on the label is a legal minimum, not the average age of the liquid, and Cognac is usually a blend of several vintages.

## Style and tasting

Cognac aromas include grape, flowers, vanilla, honey, dried fruit and spice, shifting from fruit toward nuts, leather and wood with age. The texture is usually round, with alcohol commonly around 40% ABV and the label as the authority. Base wines from different crus bring different lightness and structure.

For Cognac, taste in a small glass at room temperature, first without movement, then with a gentle swirl. A drop of water opens the aroma but also changes balance, while ice suppresses aroma and highlights sweetness. Comparing VS and XO shows how ageing changes texture and aromatic layers, but a higher grade is not a personal preference.

## Common misconceptions

First, equating Cognac with brandy. Cognac is one protected regional category within [brandy](ref:brandy).
Second, confusing Grande Champagne with Champagne sparkling wine. It is only a soil cru in Cognac.
Third, treating grades as a quality ranking. Grades only set a minimum age.
Fourth, assuming Cognac must be a single vintage. Most Cognac is a blend.
Fifth, assuming darker colour means older age. Colour depends on barrel, ageing and possible colouring.

Cognac is built on Ugni Blanc, double distillation in pot stills and ageing in French oak. VS, VSOP and XO are minimum-age designations, not a simple strength or quality ladder. It shares the grape-brandy family with Armagnac, but the appellation, distillation method and vintage conventions differ. Read the appellation, the age designation and the bottler before comparing two bottles.
