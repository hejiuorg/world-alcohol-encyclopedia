# Merlot

Merlot is one of the most widely planted red grape varieties in the world and originated in Bordeaux, France. Its berries are medium in size, its skins relatively thin, and it ripens early; tannin and acidity are moderate, which is why it is often described as supple and easy to drink. In Bordeaux, Merlot is the leading variety on the Right Bank and an important softening component in Left Bank blends.![Merlot image](/media/photos/variety-merlot-2a33ecba.jpg)

Figure: Merlot. Image: Felloni claire, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons ([source page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Merlot.jpg)).

## Definition and aliases

Merlot's name is the same in most languages, and Chinese translations include 梅乐 and 美乐. It belongs to the European grape Vitis vinifera and sits in the Bordeaux variety group with Cabernet Sauvignon and Cabernet Franc. Merlot is not a mutation of Cabernet Sauvignon; the two differ in leaf shape, ripening time and berry structure. Treating Merlot as "a lighter-coloured Cabernet" ignores its independent role in blends.

The origin of the name has several explanations, but these etymological stories do not affect identification. Merlot should be identified by leaf, cluster and berry traits, not by a translated name on a label.

## Botanical characteristics

Merlot clusters are relatively loose, berries are medium and roundish, and skins are thin, with moderate pigment and tannin. Budbreak is early and ripening is also early, so cool regions risk spring frost while warm regions may see over-rapid ripening and falling acidity. Thin skins mean tannin is less firm than in Cabernet Sauvignon, and also that botrytis risk is higher.

Vigour is moderate to high and yields need control. Excessive yields dilute flavour and can give grassy, vegetal notes; moderate yield control brings more concentrated black-fruit flavour and a more complete structure.

## Main regions

Merlot originates in Bordeaux. Saint-Émilion and Pomerol on the Right Bank are Merlot-dominant, while the Left Bank often blends Merlot with [Cabernet Sauvignon](ref:cabernet) to add suppleness and fruit. Beyond France, Italy, the United States, Chile, Australia and China all grow it on a significant scale.

Merlot styles differ clearly by region. Right Bank Bordeaux Merlot often shows plum, black cherry and cocoa with a rounder structure; Merlot from warm New World regions is more fruit-forward with higher alcohol. Climate, soil and winemaking explain a specific style better than the variety name alone.

## Typical flavours

Typical Merlot aromas include plum, black cherry and blackberry, with cocoa, bay leaf and a light cedar note. Acidity is medium, tannin is medium and relatively soft, and the body ranges from light to medium-full. While young it is fruit-driven; with age it may develop leather, truffle and dried-fruit notes.

Merlot is not the same as "sweet". Its round fruit can be mistaken for sweetness, but true sweetness comes from residual sugar or the roundness brought by alcohol. Judge Merlot by whether acidity supports the body and whether tannin is integrated with the fruit, not only by how soft the entry feels.

## Representative wines

The grand vin of [Château Lafite Rothschild](ref:lafite) is Cabernet Sauvignon-dominant with Merlot as a supporting variety, where Merlot softens the structure and adds fruit layers. Many Right Bank Bordeaux wines are Merlot-dominant and rounder, ripening earlier. These wines are useful samples for understanding Merlot as both a varietal wine and a blending partner.

Note that specific blends change with vintage and producer decisions; one wine's ratio is not a fixed rule for all Bordeaux. A variety page describes potential, while a wine page records a specific recipe and style.

## Viticulture

The main viticultural challenges with Merlot are yield control and ripening management. Excessive yields dilute flavour and bring green notes; over-rapid ripening can leave acidity low and alcohol high. Producers balance sugar and acidity through thinning, canopy management and harvest timing.

Thin skins make Merlot more sensitive to botrytis, so wet vintages demand careful canopy work. Spring frost can also affect buds and fruit set. Soil drainage, water stress and diurnal range in different regions all change the ripening rhythm.

## Common misconceptions

First, that Merlot is always soft and has no ageing potential. Quality Merlot can have structure and age well.

Second, that Merlot and Cabernet Sauvignon can be swapped freely. Ripening, tannin and blending roles differ.

Third, that Merlot must be sweet. Round fruit does not mean high residual sugar.

Fourth, that all Bordeaux reds are Cabernet-dominant. Right Bank wines are often Merlot-dominant.

Fifth, that Merlot is only for beginners. Potential depends on region, yield and winemaking, not on a stereotype.

Merlot ripens relatively early, with medium skin thickness, moderate tannin and moderate acidity. It can be made as a single-variety wine or blended with Cabernet Sauvignon and other Bordeaux varieties. Its soft fruit can make a wine approachable young, but uneven ripening in hot, wet conditions is a risk. “Merlot” does not automatically mean low-acid and plush; climate, soil and winemaking change the structure.

**In brief:** A red grape common on Bordeaux's Right Bank, with medium berries, relatively thin skins and early ripening; its tannin and acidity are moderate, and it is used both as a varietal wine and in blends with Cabernet Sauvignon.
