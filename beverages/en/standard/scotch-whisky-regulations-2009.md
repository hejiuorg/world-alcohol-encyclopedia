# The Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 (SI 2009/2890)

The Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 are the UK statutory instrument that governs the name “Scotch Whisky”. Made on 26 October 2009, laid before Parliament on 30 October and in force from 23 November 2009, SI 2009/2890 was made under section 2(2) of the European Communities Act 1972. Its purpose was to implement the EU spirit-drinks rules in UK law while tightening the categories, protected area names and labelling of Scotch. It is not a distiller’s manual but a naming law: it decides what may be called Scotch Whisky and how that name must appear on a bottle.

## A product-definition law, not a style guide

Regulation 3 states that Scotch Whisky must be whisky produced in Scotland and must satisfy nine conditions. Regulation 5 goes further: no whisky other than Scotch Whisky may be manufactured in Scotland. Regulation 6 closes the marketing loop: a drink that is not Scotch Whisky may not be labelled, packaged, sold, advertised or promoted as “Scotch Whisky” or “Scotch”, and no other presentation may create a likelihood of confusion.

This structure means the regulations do not judge whether a whisky is good, nor do they prescribe peat levels, cask flavours or distillery style. They answer a prior question: does the product qualify? Raw materials, place, distillation strength, cask type, maturation, additives and minimum strength are the tests. Flavour variation is left to distillers, regions and the market. That explains why a whisky can taste unlike a stereotypical Scotch and still be fully compliant, and why a whisky that tastes “Scottish” may have no right to the name.

## The five legal categories

Regulation 3(2) creates five categories, and their names are compulsory sales descriptions. “Single Malt Scotch Whisky” must be distilled in one or more batches at a single distillery, from water and malted barley with no other cereals, and in pot stills. “Single Grain Scotch Whisky” is distilled at a single distillery but is neither single malt nor blended Scotch; it may use other cereals and column stills.

“Blended Malt Scotch Whisky” is a blend of two or more single malt Scotch whiskies from more than one distillery. “Blended Grain Scotch Whisky” is a blend of two or more single grain Scotch whiskies from more than one distillery. “Blended Scotch Whisky” is a blend of one or more single malts with one or more single grains. There is no legal category for “single cask” or “cask strength”; those are additional label claims, not categories.

## The five protected names

Regulation 10 protects five names at two levels. The localities are Campbeltown and Islay; the regions are Highland, Lowland and Speyside. Campbeltown is defined by the South Kintyre ward, Islay by the Isle of Islay in Argyll, and the Highland/Lowland boundary by a line running from the Clyde to the Tay. Speyside is made up of specified wards in Moray and Highland.

The rule is direct: if a whisky or whisky-based drink uses one of these names on its label, packaging, advertising or promotion, the whisky must be Scotch Whisky distilled in that locality or region. For a mixed drink, every whisky in it must come from there. Similar names that may confuse the public are also prohibited. “Highland” is not a flavour term, and “Speyside” cannot be borrowed as a style description. Reading the five names against the geography of [Scotland](ref:scotland) is more useful than treating them as marketing language.

## Three hard thresholds: still, cask and bottle

The first threshold is distillation strength. Regulation 3(1)(b) requires distillation at less than 94.8% vol, so that the distillate retains aroma and taste from the raw materials and the method. This limit applies to the still, not to the finished bottle. Together with the choice of [distillation](ref:distillation) equipment, it separates Scotch from neutral alcohol. Single malt must use pot stills; single grain has no such requirement.

The second threshold is cask and maturation. Scotch must be matured only in oak casks of no more than 700 litres, only in Scotland, only in an excise warehouse or a permitted place, and for at least three years. The key words are oak, 700 litres, Scotland and three years. The rules do not require bourbon casks, sherry casks or new oak, and three years is a floor rather than a target. [Oak ageing](ref:oak-ageing) is the process that adds colour, aroma and texture; the legal minimum is not a guarantee of maturity.

The third threshold is bottling strength. The finished product must be at least 40% vol, measured as [alcohol by volume](ref:abv). Cask strength may be higher, but never lower. No substance may be added except water, plain caramel colouring, or both. Sweeteners, flavourings and glycerol are not on the permitted list.

## Names, years and regions on the label

Regulation 8 requires the category name on the front of the container, or on transport and display packaging where the front is not visible. The words must be conspicuous, legible, indelible, equally prominent and not overlaid or interrupted, and the category name may not be used with other words. One permitted addition is a Scottish locality or region placed immediately before the category name, as in “Speyside Single Malt Scotch Whisky”. This is why compliant labels often give the category a line of its own.

Regulation 12 governs age claims. Maturation or age must be expressed in years and may refer only to the youngest whisky in the bottle, matching the EU rule. A distillation-year claim is allowed only if it refers to a single calendar year, all the whisky was distilled in that year, and the same field of vision also shows the bottling year, the maturation period or the age. Numbers that might confuse consumers about age or vintage are prohibited. Regulation 10 restricts area names to the place of distillation and bars confusingly similar names.

## Enforcement and common misconceptions

Regulations 16 to 40 set up enforcement. Local authorities are responsible; authorised officers may inspect, sample, issue improvement notices or fixed penalty notices; breaches of the definition, manufacture, marketing, category, area-name or age rules are offences punishable by fines. Because the same rules underpin the geographical indication “Scotch Whisky”, enforcement protects not only consumers but the reputation of the whole sector.

Six misconceptions recur. First, three years is the minimum for all Scotch, not the age of every bottle; a 12-year statement means the youngest component is at least 12. Second, “single malt” does not mean single cask or single vintage; it means one distillery, 100% malted barley and pot stills. Third, “blended” does not mean neutral grain spirit; blended Scotch must contain both malt and grain whisky. Fourth, the rules require oak casks up to 700 litres, not bourbon or sherry casks specifically. Fifth, 40% vol is a bottling minimum, so cask strength is lawful. Sixth, plain caramel may be used, so colour says nothing reliable about age or additive-free status.
