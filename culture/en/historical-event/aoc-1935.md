# The French AOC System (1935): The Birth of Controlled Appellation of Origin

On 30 July 1935 France created the Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée (AOC) by decree and set up a national committee for the appellations of wines and spirits. The system tied a place name to grape varieties, yield ceilings, planting and winemaking rules, so that only qualifying products could use the name. It later evolved into the INAO system and became an important reference for European origin protection.

## Date and place

The system was born in France in July 1935, driven chiefly by Joseph Capus, a senator from the Gironde and an agronomist. AOC was not an abstract legal invention but a response to half a century of wine crisis: phylloxera arrived in France in 1865, followed by recovery of output, rampant adulteration and boundary disputes.

Geographically, AOC inherited the logic of 'region and parcel'. Champagne, Bordeaux and Burgundy had already seen associations and delimitation practices in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries; the 1935 decree raised these local experiences to a national system and gave the committee powers of recognition and enforcement.

## Background: phylloxera, surplus and fraud

In 1865 phylloxera, arriving from the United States, appeared in France and caused unprecedented vineyard losses. Reconstruction brought a series of problems: to fill the gap, the market saw artificial substitutes such as 'sugar wine' and 'raisin wine', and large imports of Spanish, Italian and Algerian wine; Algerian wine was high in alcohol and low in cost and was often blended with inferior wine.

In 1907 winegrowers in Languedoc and Roussillon protested on a large scale over collapsing prices and adulteration, and on 29 June 1907 France passed an anti-fraud law seeking to ban watering and illegal sugaring. In 1911 Champagne winegrowers rose again over the delimitation of the 'Champagne' name. In 1928 Champagne began to define the land entitled to the name through parcel lists.

These conflicts pointed to one problem: a place name is a public asset that anyone may abuse. To protect it, one must also define the land, permitted varieties, yields and methods, or 'origin' is merely an advertising phrase.

## Course: the 1935 decree

The decree of 30 July 1935, titled 'on the defence of the wine market and the economic regime of alcohol', created AOC and set up the Comité national des appellations d'origine des vins et eaux-de-vie (CNAO). The committee recognised appellations, defined their boundaries, wrote rules and could defend the names in court.

On 15 May 1936 the first AOCs were formally recognised: Arbois, Cassis, Cognac, Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Monbazillac and Tavel. The list shows that AOC was not limited to wine from the start: Cognac is a distilled spirit and was brought into the same origin logic.

On 16 July 1947 the CNAO became the Institut national des appellations d'origine (INAO). INAO then became the central French body for origin appellations; in 2006 its name was broadened to the National Institute of Origin and Quality, and its remit extended from wine and spirits to more agricultural products and foods.

## System: what AOC regulates

AOC is not a simple trademark registration but a combination of geography, rules and approval.

Geographically, it delimits the parcels or communes allowed to produce, usually according to soil, slope, aspect and historical planting records. In rules, it specifies permitted varieties, minimum planting density, pruning methods, yield ceilings, minimum alcohol strength and winemaking methods; the yield ceiling is a key tool for quality and scarcity. In approval, a product must pass tasting or technical review before it may use the name.

The point of the combination is verifiability. A place name rests no longer only on reputation but on parcel lists, declarations, inspection and penalties. The Senate's Rural Code article defines AOC as grantable only to qualifying products, which is the legal expression of this approach.

## Influence: from France to Europe

First, it stabilised the French wine market. By limiting yields and fighting adulteration, AOC raised the credibility of famous-origin wine and supported grower prices; but it also excluded small producers from the names and created new distributional conflicts.

Second, it became the template of French agricultural quality policy. The law of 2 July 1990 extended the AOC concept to all agricultural products and foods, so AOC was no longer only a wine system but the parent of France's geographical indication system.

Third, it influenced European rules. The EU later built protected designations of origin (PDO/AOP) and protected geographical indications (PGI/IGP), whose 'origin, rules, approval' structure has a clear lineage from the 1935 AOC.

Fourth, it changed how consumers understand wine. A place name on a label is no longer just geographical information but a combination of quality grade, production constraints and legal responsibility; this is also the basis for reading 'Old World' labels today.

## Sources and disputes

First, the date of the 1935 decree differs by one day across sources. Most French official and scholarly sources give 30 July, while some give 31 July (the date of publication in the Official Journal); this essay uses 30 July and notes the version difference.

Second, the list of first AOCs comes from later compilations. The six names recognised on 15 May 1936 appear in research overviews and specialist encyclopedias; checking each one requires the recognition notices and INAO archives of the time.

Third, the 'quality' meaning of AOC needs qualification. It guarantees that origin, varieties and methods meet the rules, but not that every bottle is good; tasting approval is a threshold, not the end of a quality ranking.

Fourth, AOC and the 1855 classification are not the same logic. The 1855 classification ranks châteaux, while AOC is a system of origin names and rules; they are often confused, but their legal basis, object and updating mechanism differ.

## Timeline

- 1865: phylloxera arrives in France, causing unprecedented vineyard losses.
- 1907: Languedoc and Roussillon winegrowers protest; an anti-fraud law passes the same year.
- 1911: Champagne winegrowers rise over the delimitation of the name.
- 1928: Champagne begins to define the appellation area through parcel lists.
- 1935: the decree of 30 July creates AOC and the CNAO.
- 1936: the first AOCs are recognised on 15 May.
- 1947: the CNAO becomes INAO.
- 1990: AOC is extended to all agricultural products and foods.
- 2006: INAO is renamed the National Institute of Origin and Quality.
