# Armagnac

Armagnac is a grape brandy region in Gascony, in south-western France. Unlike Cognac's double pot distillation, Armagnac traditionally uses a continuous still, giving a rougher body and more expansive aroma; single-estate and single-vintage bottlings are also more common than in Cognac.![Armagnac image](/media/photos/region-armagnac-e5e16174.jpg)

Figure: Armagnac. Image: Rama, CC BY-SA 2.0 fr, via Wikimedia Commons ([source page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Armagnac-img_0465.jpg)).

## Geography and sub-regions

Armagnac is centred on the Gers department and divided into Bas-Armagnac, Ténarèze and Haut-Armagnac. The sandy soils of Bas-Armagnac are considered best suited to long ageing, Ténarèze lies in between, and Haut-Armagnac has more inland soils and climate. Sub-region names describe geography, not quality.

The region sits on the Gascon hills, with a climate influenced by both the Atlantic and the Mediterranean and a long autumn. Grape ripeness and acidity vary by vintage, which is one reason Armagnac emphasises vintage labelling.

The appellation does not cover the Gers department alone but parts of Gers, Landes and Lot-et-Garonne. Only when all the grapes come from one zone may the label name Bas-Armagnac, Ténarèze or Haut-Armagnac on its own; a cross-zone blend may be labelled simply Armagnac. The soils differ clearly: Bas-Armagnac is mainly acidic sandy soil, Ténarèze has more clay and limestone, and Haut-Armagnac is limestone hills. That is the geographical basis for stylistic divergence under one distillation method. Zone membership is fixed by declared plots and cellar records, and the specific list of plots should follow the regional body's publication.

## History

Armagnac distillation dates back to the Middle Ages, with references in 14th century documents. It is often called one of the oldest distilled spirit regions in France, but the claim depends on how documents are defined and should not be read as an exact ranking.

Armagnac received appellation protection in 1936, and production rules have since been managed by the regional body. In the late 20th century its market remained much smaller than Cognac, which helped preserve many smallholder and single-estate producers.

## Grapes and raw materials

Armagnac uses mainly Ugni Blanc, Folle Blanche, Colombard and Baco Blanc. Baco Blanc is a hybrid variety that gives a distinctive fruit character and texture, and it is one of the clear raw-material differences from Cognac. The grapes are pressed into a high-acid white wine before distillation.

Raw materials and planting are constrained by regional rules, and harvest timing affects acidity and potential alcohol. As with Cognac, grape variety is only a starting point; distillation and ageing decide the final style.

## Distillation

Armagnac traditionally uses the alambic armagnacais, a continuous still in which the wine passes through the distillation in one continuous flow, usually distilled once. The resulting spirit is lower in alcohol and retains more flavour compounds, giving a heavier texture. Some producers also use pot stills, but continuous distillation is the region's signature method.

Continuous distillation is efficient, yet temperature and cut control still demand skill. The still design, heat and flow rate all shape aroma. Compared with the general principles of [distillation](ref:distillation), Armagnac puts more emphasis on retaining rugged aroma and oily body.

## Ageing and grades

Armagnac ages in French oak, commonly Gascon oak. Ageing slowly lowers alcohol and brings vanilla, dried fruit, leather and spice. Grades are also defined by minimum age: VS at least 1 year, VSOP at least 4 years, XO at least 6 years and Hors d'Age at least 10 years.

Unlike Cognac, Armagnac often appears as a single vintage (millésime) and single-estate bottling. A vintage release must come from one year's harvest and age in barrel, with labelling rules set by regional standards. Vintage is not automatically better than a blend; it offers traceability and the character of a particular year.

## Style and tasting

Armagnac aromas often include plum, dried fruit, leather, tobacco and spice, with a heavier body and more obvious alcohol than Cognac. Bas-Armagnac tends to be finer, Ténarèze fuller and Haut-Armagnac more rustic. Aged Armagnac develops nuts, cocoa and wood.

For Armagnac, taste in a small glass at room temperature, and a little water opens the aroma. Vintage Armagnac suits sipping and is often served after a meal; ice suppresses aroma and changes texture. Compared with other categories of [brandy](ref:brandy), Armagnac depends more on the specific estate and vintage.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating Armagnac as a cheaper substitute for Cognac. The two differ in process and stylistic aim.
Second, assuming a single vintage is always better. Vintage shows origin, not preference.
Third, treating sub-regions as a quality ranking. They describe soil and climate.
Fourth, assuming single distillation means roughness. Continuous distillation can also be controlled precisely.
Fifth, equating dark colour with old age. Colour depends on barrel, ageing and possible colouring.

Armagnac is distinguished by continuous distillation, named subregions and a strong tradition of vintage bottling. Bas-Armagnac, Ténarèze and Haut-Armagnac differ in soil and style. It shares grape brandy and oak ageing with Cognac, but the stills, appellation rules and vintage labels are different. VS, VSOP and XO are minimum-age designations, not a ranking of quality; the year on a vintage bottle refers to the harvest, not the year of distillation alone.

For Armagnac, begin with the appellation and its permitted varieties. Then look at the vintage and the producer, and finally ask how the wine is labelled. The answers point to different choices, not to a single score.
