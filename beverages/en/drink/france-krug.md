# Krug

Krug is a historic Champagne house in Reims, France, founded in 1843 by Joseph Krug and part of LVMH since 1999. The wine belongs to [sparkling wine](ref:sparkling-wine), specifically Champagne, and is known for the multi-vintage Grande Cuvée. The common format is 750 mL at about 12.5% ABV; batch, disgorgement date and label details should be checked on the bottle.

## Definition and position

Krug is both the name of the house and the name of the brand. The house is based around Reims in the Champagne region of [France](ref:france), and its wines are made and sold within the Champagne protected-designation system. Grande Cuvée is the multi-vintage flagship, and there are also vintage Champagnes and single-vineyard wines, with clear differences in positioning and price.

Within the Champagne category, Krug is sparkling wine, not a still wine and not a fortified wine. Brand history and pricing are a market identity, not a legal grade; judging a specific bottle still depends on the batch, disgorgement date and label.

## Origin and classification

The Champagne region lies in north-eastern France, with a cool climate and extensive chalk soils. The cool climate lets grapes retain high acidity as they ripen, while chalk aids drainage and root growth. Champagne vineyards are classified by village and parcel, and that classification affects the positioning of fruit without directly equalling finished quality.

Krug blends base wines from many vintages, villages and varieties to reduce the risk of a single year. Compared with Champagnes built around a single vintage, such as [Dom Pérignon](ref:domperignon), Krug's flagship emphasises the combination across years.

## Varieties and blend

Champagne usually uses Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier. Chardonnay provides acidity, floral notes and minerality, Pinot Noir provides structure and fruit, and Meunier adds roundness. Different varieties and parcels are fermented separately before [blending](ref:blending).

Multi-vintage blending is Krug's identifying feature: base wines from different years complement one another in flavour, acidity and structure. The blend proportions are company process information that the label does not fully disclose; the batch number and disgorgement date are what identify a specific bottle.

## Production

Champagne undergoes two fermentations: the first alcoholic [fermentation](ref:fermentation) in a vessel, and the second in bottle, which creates the bubbles and evolves flavour. The carbon dioxide from the second fermentation dissolves into the wine to form bubbles; time on the lees affects the depth of aroma and texture.

After disgorgement a small amount of liqueur d'expédition is added, determining the final sweetness. Sweetness type and disgorgement date are usually given on the back label or in the batch information. Process descriptions only indicate a style route; specific parameters are the house's arrangements and may not be published year by year.

## Specification and label

The common format is a 750 mL bottle at about 12.5% ABV, as sparkling wine. The label usually carries the brand, wine name, region, strength, net volume and batch number, and the back label also states the disgorgement date and sweetness type.

Batches and market versions may differ, and packaging differences do not equal differences in the liquid. Buyers should check the batch number and disgorgement date and avoid treating a gift box as a separate grade.

## Flavour and tasting

The aroma often shows layers of citrus, green apple, toasted bread and nuts; the bubbles are fine, acidity is relatively high, and the palate moves from fresh to richer. Multi-vintage blending keeps the style fairly consistent within a year, though batch differences remain.

Serve at about 8-10°C in a tulip-shaped or flute glass. Too warm lets the bubbles escape too quickly, too cold suppresses aroma. Once opened, drink soon, as the bubbles weaken with time.

## Drinking and pairing

Krug works as an aperitif and with seafood, poultry, creamy dishes and soft cheese. Acidity cuts through fat and bubbles cleanse the palate; with fried food the effect of bubbles and acidity is especially clear.

If a bottle is not finished, use a dedicated stopper, refrigerate and try to finish within a day. Sparkling wine loses bubbles over time and the flavour oxidises; a re-pressurising tool can slow this but cannot restore the original state.

## Buying and storage

Champagne circulates mainly through merchants, restaurants and retail, with prices shaped by brand, batch and market expectations. Buyers should check the brand, wine name, batch number and disgorgement date, and consider whether the channel is reliable.

Unopened Champagne should be stored on its side, away from light, at a steady temperature, ideally 10-15°C. Long storage needs stable temperature and humidity, avoiding sharp swings; Champagne does not improve indefinitely, and the drinking window depends on the wine type and batch.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating Champagne as ordinary sparkling wine. Champagne is a sparkling wine from a specific region under a protected designation.

Second, treating multi-vintage blending as low-end. Multi-vintage blending is a style choice and does not mean lower quality.

Third, treating bubble quantity as a quality marker. Bubble fineness and persistence depend on process and batch.

Fourth, judging quality by brand alone. Batch, disgorgement date and storage condition matter equally.

Fifth, assuming Champagne must be aged. Most Champagnes are best drunk within a few years of release.
