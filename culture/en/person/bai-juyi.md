# Bai Juyi

Bai Juyi (772–846), styled Letian, in later life called Xiangshan Jushi and also the Drunken Chanting Gentleman, wrote wine poems that depend not on strange imagery but on ordinary detail: the new wine is ready, the stove is lit, snow is coming, so he asks a friend whether he can come for a cup. Wine here is not a ladder to paradise; it connects people to one another and to the hours of a day.

## Life and official career

Bai Juyi was born in 772 in Xinzheng, Henan, with family roots in Taiyuan. He passed the jinshi in 800; he was county defender in Zhouzhi (806), a Hanlin academician (807), a junior censor (808), Marshal of Jiangzhou after demotion (815), governor of Zhongzhou (819), Hangzhou (822) and Suzhou (825), and from 829 held a sinecure in Luoyang, where he died in 846.

In “A Letter to Yuan Zhen” he argued that writing should respond to its age. His satirical poems contributed to his demotion, but in office he managed West Lake at Hangzhou and opened the Shantang canal at Suzhou. The leisure in his wine poems is one slice of that life.

## Where wine sits in daily life

Bai Juyi uses wine in three ways: to invite (“Asking Liu Nineteen”), to remember (“Drunk with Li Eleven, Thinking of Yuan Jiu”, where blossom serves as a wine tally), and to console himself (“Facing Wine”: enjoy wealth or poverty alike, since a man who never laughs is a fool).

Unlike Li Bai's cosmic solitude, Bai Juyi's wine settles inward, into a room.

## Asking Liu Nineteen: an invitation before snow

The poem has four lines: “Green ants, new unfiltered wine; a small red clay stove. Evening, and snow is coming; can you drink one cup?” The first line is the wine, the second the stove, the third the weather, and only the fourth is the invitation. Everything is ready except the person.

“Green ants” are the fine greenish foam on the surface of new wine, not insects and not a brand. “New unfiltered wine” is wine that has not yet been strained. The final “can you drink one cup?” is a question, not a command; its tone is a proposal between friends.

The warmth comes from preparation rather than statement: the poet never says “I miss you”, only that wine, stove and snow are in place.

## Song of the Pipa: the farewell cup and disappointment

In 815 Bai Juyi was demoted to Marshal of Jiangzhou. The next autumn he saw a guest off at the mouth of the Pen River and wrote “Song of the Pipa”. It begins: “The host dismounts, the guest is aboard; we raise cups to drink, but there is no music. Drunk without joy, we sadly face parting; at the moment of parting the broad river is steeped in moonlight.”

Here wine is a ritual pause that fails to hold. Without music, the farewell has no buffer, and the wine cannot steady the mood. When the pipa player tells her story and the poet says, “We are both wanderers at the end of the world; what need is there to have known each other before?”, disappointment joins the fate of a stranger. Wine solves nothing, but it gives them a reason to sit and talk.

## The Drunken Chanting Gentleman: a persona

In old age Bai Juyi called himself the Drunken Chanting Gentleman and wrote a self-portrait under that name. It says the gentleman has forgotten his surname, home and rank, and describes him as addicted to wine, devoted to the qin and excessive in poetry. The name binds wine, music and poetry into one ideal of life; it is not a medical record of alcohol use.

He wrote of “the madness of wine” stirring “the demon of poetry”. But the more important function of “drunken chanting” is self-fashioning: an old man in Luoyang arranges his days around drink, recitation, music and verse. Around 845 he formed the Nine Elders of Xiangshan with Hu Gao, Ji Jiao and others, feasting and writing poems together, a scene later painted as the Nine Elders.

## The material details of Tang grain wine

The “new unfiltered wine” in Bai Juyi is fermented wine, not distilled spirit. Tang wine was made from rice, starter and water, fermented with sediment and strained before drinking; it was lower in strength than today's baijiu, so a “measure of wine” was not a measure of strong liquor.

Li Zhao's Tang Guoshi Bu lists famous Tang wines, including “spring-burnt wine from Jiannan” and “Fushui spring” from Fushui. The names show that the Tang trade already had regional brands and grades. Bai Juyi's home-brewed wine belongs to daily life; the famous wines belong to market and tribute.

## Reputation and dispute

Su Shi, in an essay mourning Liu Ziyu, wrote “Yuan light, Bai vulgar”, pairing Yuan Zhen's lightness with Bai Juyi's vulgarity as two styles to be wary of. It is the most famous criticism of Bai Juyi: his language is too direct and too complete, leaving little aftertaste.

But “vulgar” does not mean crude. “Song of the Pipa” describes music from “the great string pattering like sudden rain” to “the small string whispering like private speech”, with dense and exact images; “Song of Everlasting Regret” turns its narrative through many reversals. Plainness is a choice, not a lack of skill. Another dispute concerns his life: the feasting and the pose of “contentment” in late Luoyang have been criticised as a withdrawal from reality. Read work, persona and life separately.

## Common misreadings

First, that “green ants” names an insect or a brand; it describes foam on wine. Second, that “new unfiltered wine” means freshly distilled strong liquor. The dominant Tang drink was fermented grain wine, and new unfiltered wine is wine not yet strained. Third, that because he called himself the Drunken Chanting Gentleman he drank heavily every day. The name is a literary posture, not a diary. Fourth, that the warm scene of “Asking Liu Nineteen” was the normal condition of Tang drinking; a stove, fresh wine and leisure all required some comfort. Fifth, that Bai Juyi was only a poet of leisure. His satirical poems, new yuefu and long narratives are equally central.
