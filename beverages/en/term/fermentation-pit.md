# Fermentation pit

A fermentation pit is a solid-state fermentation vessel and one of the most recognisable facilities in baijiu production. GB/T 15109-2021, Terminology of baijiu industry, defines it as one kind of solid-state fermentation vessel, built from materials such as yellow mud, stone, brick, concrete or wood and usually rectangular. The definition stresses a vessel used for solid-state fermentation, not any place where liquor is stored.

## Definition and boundaries

In the Chinese industry the word jiao is used for several different things. A fermentation pit is a vessel for fermenting grain mash; a cellar is a storage space; cellaring is a maturation process. They should not be confused. A pit holds mash that is still fermenting, while finished liquor is stored and aged outside it.

Another common boundary is the buried jar. Qingxiang, or light-aroma, baijiu often ferments in earthen jars set into the ground. These are fermentation vessels, but the industry generally does not call them pits. Describing a buried jar as a pit would suggest that light-aroma baijiu also uses mud or stone pits.

A pit is also not the same as an old pit. Old pit is a colloquial term for a pit that has been used continuously for a long time and has a mature pit-mud community. It describes a process condition, not a separate product grade.

## Materials and types

By construction material, common types include mud pits, stone pits, brick pits, concrete pits and wooden pits. Mud pits have walls and floors built from yellow mud and are typical of strong-aroma baijiu. Stone pits are lined with cut stone and are common in sauce-aroma baijiu; the current sauce-aroma standard describes the pit as preferably a stone pit with a mud bottom. Brick and concrete pits are used in some regions and companies, while wooden pits appear in particular traditional processes.

Material affects heat conduction, sealing, acid resistance and microbial attachment. Mud walls support pit-mud culture but demand high maintenance. Stone walls are strong and easy to clean, but the mud layer at the bottom has to carry the microbiology. Concrete pits are cheaper and easier to standardise, yet their surfaces differ from traditional materials in microbial attachment and air permeability. Material choice depends on product positioning, regional tradition and scale, and there is no single best answer.

## Pit mud and microbiology

Pit mud is the core of the pit. Mud used continuously for a long time accumulates a stable microbial community, and ester-producing organisms such as caproic-acid bacteria are especially important for the main aroma of strong-aroma baijiu. Pit mud is not ordinary mud but a microbial carrier that has been through many rounds of fermentation, maintenance and acclimatisation.

The condition of the mud directly affects the fermentation result. Degraded, compacted, sour or contaminated mud makes the aroma flat or even off. Pit management therefore includes maintaining the mud by replenishing nutrients, adjusting moisture, keeping a suitable acid-base environment and covering and cleaning the pit between production cycles.

Pit age is often used to describe how mature the mud community is. Pits in continuous use for a long time are usually more stable, but this does not mean older is always better. A neglected old pit degrades as well. Judging a pit means looking at the colour, texture, smell and use records of the mud, not only the year it was built.

## Role in the process

The pit carries in-pit fermentation. Once the mash is loaded and the pit is sealed, the environment becomes closed and anaerobic; yeast converts sugar to alcohol while bacteria and enzymes continue to form acids, esters and other flavour compounds. Loading temperature, moisture, acidity and starch concentration are the main variables that steer fermentation.

Different styles use pits differently. Strong-aroma baijiu usually loads the blended mash straight into the pit and organises multiple rounds through continuous grain topping. Sauce-aroma baijiu first completes [stacked fermentation](ref:stacked-fermentation) outside the pit and then loads the mash for sealed fermentation, so the pit handles anaerobic alcohol production and later flavour conversion. Light-aroma baijiu uses buried jars and does not follow the pit route.

A pit and [qu making](ref:qu-making) are two different things. Qu sets the starting point for microbes and enzymes, while the pit sets the environment and duration of fermentation. Together they determine the result.

## Use and maintenance

Routine management covers cleaning and inspection before loading, temperature and moisture control during loading, temperature and seal checks during fermentation, and cleaning and maintenance after unloading. Sealing material, pit depth, wall integrity and drainage all affect sealing quality and the risk of contamination.

The empty period matters too. A pit left empty, waterlogged or exposed to strong sun can lose its mud condition. Regions and companies usually arrange production according to the seasons so that pits balance continuous use with maintenance. Maintenance records are more reliable than memory, especially where many pits of different ages are in use.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating a pit as a cellar. A pit is a fermentation vessel; a cellar is a storage space, and their functions differ.
Second, calling a buried jar a pit. A jar is not a pit built from yellow mud, stone, brick, concrete or wood.
Third, assuming older pits are always better. Old pits need continuous care, and degraded mud can drag down flavour just the same.
Fourth, assuming all baijiu uses pits. Light-aroma baijiu mainly uses buried jars, which is a different process route.
Fifth, ignoring materials and maintenance and looking only at a pit-age number. Pit age describes time in use and cannot stand alone as a quality conclusion.
