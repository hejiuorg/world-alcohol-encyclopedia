## Classification: polishing ratio and added alcohol

The legal categories of sake are decided by two variables: the polishing ratio, meaning the proportion of the rice grain left after milling, and whether brewers' alcohol is added. Junmai uses only water, rice, koji and yeast, with no added alcohol. Honjozo allows a small amount of brewers' alcohol and has an upper polishing limit of 70%. Ginjo also allows added alcohol and has an upper limit of 60%. Daiginjo has an upper limit of 50%. Junmai ginjo and junmai daiginjo add no alcohol and correspond to 60% and 50% respectively.

The more the rice is milled, the less protein and lipid remain in the outer layers, so fermentation is slower, aroma is cleaner, and notes of apple, pear and blossom are more likely. Less milling leaves more rice aroma and umami and a fuller texture. The number on the label is not a quality grade but a style coordinate: a high polishing ratio suits delicate sashimi, while a low ratio suits dishes with more fat and sauce.

## Where umami comes from: koji, yeast and amino acids

Sake's umami comes from rice and koji. Starch in the rice is broken into sugar by koji mould, while protein is broken into amino acids and peptides; yeast produces organic acids and esters during fermentation, giving apple, banana, pear and steamed-rice aromas. Glutamate and aspartate among the amino acids supply umami, lactic and succinic acids give soft acidity, and [residual sugar](ref:residual-sugar) decides sweetness.

This chemistry overlaps closely with Japanese seasoning: glutamate in soy sauce, peptides in miso, kombu stock and inosinate in dried bonito are all umami molecules. When sake meets [Japanese food](ref:japan), umami does not simply add up to "saltier"; it creates layers, with the rice aroma holding the ingredient and the ingredient's savour making the wine fuller in return.

## Mechanism: umami layering, acidity, alcohol and temperature

Umami layering is the first mechanism. Moderate umami makes a drink seem rounder and less alcoholic; but very strong umami, such as heavy kombu, bonito or aged cheese, amplifies bitterness and saltiness, so intensely savoury dishes need higher acidity and a cleaner aroma.

[Acidity](ref:acidity) is the second. Sake's acidity is usually lower than white wine's but sufficient to stimulate saliva and cut fat. The oil in tempura, the sauce on yakitori and the glaze on grilled eel all need acidity and a sense of lift; without it the drink feels sticky and the food heavy.

Alcohol and temperature are the third. Most sake is between 15% and 16% alcohol, higher than wine but lower than baijiu. Temperature changes aroma and texture: cold tightens the aroma and emphasises freshness, room temperature opens rice and umami, and warming makes alcohol and sweetness more obvious while acidity feels softer.

## Sashimi and sushi

Sashimi is the baseline for sake pairing. White fish, flounder and sea bream are delicate and suit a clean, softly acidic junmai ginjo served a little below room temperature; lean tuna has more flavour and suits a junmai with more rice aroma; fatty tuna needs higher acidity and a drier finish, otherwise the fat buries the aroma.

The key to sushi is the vinegared rice. Its acid and sugar interact with the sake's acidity and residual sugar: when the rice is sharper, the sake should be drier; when the rice is sweeter, the sake should be fresher. Soy sauce and wasabi are variables, since soy adds salt and umami while wasabi heat amplifies alcohol burn; the heavier the dip, the lighter and colder the sake should be.

## Tempura and yakitori

Tempura batter holds oil, so the entry is crisp and fatty. A clean ginjo or junmai can cut the oil, but too light a body is crushed by the fat; a wine with slightly higher acidity and a faint sparkle works better. The sweet-salty dipping sauce amplifies alcohol, so avoid high-strength or heavily aromatic sake.

Yakitori's charcoal, glaze and chicken-skin fat need a junmai with more rice aroma and clearer acidity. Salt-grilled skewers suit a cleaner sake; glazed skewers with sweet-salty sauce suit a fuller sake with a little residual sugar. Offal such as liver and heart has metallic and bitter notes and suits a deeper aroma with higher acidity.

## Hotpot, chawanmushi and fermented side dishes

Hotpot, whether shabu-shabu or sukiyaki, is hot and strongly savoury. Sake's role is to cool and refresh, so it should be served cool and not too heavy. Sukiyaki's sweet-salty sauce amplifies alcohol, so a wine with higher acidity and lower residual sugar is better; shabu-shabu's kombu broth suits a clean, aromatic junmai ginjo.

Chawanmushi's egg and dashi are gentle, and suit a softly acidic junmai served a little cool. Fermented side dishes such as pickles, natto and salt-fermented seafood are strong and suit a fuller aroma with higher acidity; but natto's ammonia note clashes with rice aroma and is usually a poor combination.

## The temperature spectrum: from hanahie to atsukan

Sake's serving range is wide. Hanahie, around 10 degrees Celsius, suits ginjo and nama sake and emphasises freshness and aroma; hinata-kan, around 30 degrees Celsius, begins to open the rice aroma; hitohada-kan, around 35 degrees Celsius, is the softest on the palate; nuru-kan, around 40 degrees Celsius, suits junmai; atsukan, around 50 degrees Celsius and above, suits sake with thick rice aroma and high acidity.

Hotter is not better. Heating amplifies alcohol and sweetness and drives off aroma; if the sake is delicate, warming loses detail. Judge by structure: high-acid, rice-rich sake tolerates heat, while clean, low-acid sake is better cold.

## Regional differences: Niigata, Yamagata, Hiroshima

Niigata is known for a light, dry style with soft water, clean aroma and a dry finish, suiting sashimi and delicate dishes. Yamagata is known for fruity ginjo with a more outgoing aroma, suiting poultry and slightly fatty food. Hiroshima's harder water gives fuller body and more obvious umami, suiting oysters, sauces and stronger dishes.

These regional styles are not absolute labels, since brewery, rice variety and yeast matter more. The more reliable method is to read the polishing ratio, alcohol strength and sake meter value on the label, then judge the actual concentration of the dish.

## Pairings that fail

First, heavily glazed yakitori with daiginjo. The sweet-salty sauce buries the ginjo aroma and amplifies alcohol.
Second, oysters with a highly aromatic junmai daiginjo. The aroma covers the oyster's mineral, sea-water freshness.
Third, very spicy food with sake. Capsaicin amplifies alcohol irritation and the delicate aroma becomes almost invisible.
Fourth, dessert with a dry sake. The wine's acid and alcohol make the dessert cloying, and the dessert makes the wine bitter.
Fifth, blue cheese with junmai. Strong salt and spice clash with rice aroma.
Sixth, scalding hotpot with ice-cold ginjo. The temperature gap interrupts both aroma and texture.

## Non-alcoholic alternatives

Without alcohol, unsweetened amazake can replace sake's rice aroma and sweetness, cold-brewed green tea can replace acidity and refreshment, and kombu stock can replace umami. Amazake is sweet and suits glazed and grilled food rather than raw fish; green tea tannin creates slight astringency with raw fish, so keep it light.

Further reading: [Sake for beginners](ref:c-extra-1).## Ordering and portion size

At a Japanese restaurant, order a light sashimi or chawanmushi first, then decide the sake temperature and portion. For two people, one go (about 180 ml) is a comfortable start; pour 60–90 ml per person and ask for small cups or split pours if you want to compare styles. Sake loses aroma after opening, so order a small bottle first and add more halfway through rather than filling every cup at once. If someone is not drinking, use [Sake intro](ref:c-extra-1) to choose a style and temperature, then apply the [pairing principles](ref:food-pairing) to sashimi, tempura and hot-pot dishes; for a fuller rice profile, see [Junmai](ref:junmai).
