# Champagne AOC: The Appellation Rules

Champagne is not a generic word for sparkling wine. It is a French appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC) and a European protected designation of origin (PDO). Its legal identity rests on three layers: the law of 22 July 1927 delimiting the vineyard area, the decree of 29 June 1936 recognising the appellation, and the current product specification (cahier des charges), approved by ministerial order, which governs varieties, viticulture, pressing, second fermentation, ageing, pressure and labelling. The Institut national de l'origine et de la qualité (INAO) administers the appellation system; the Comité Champagne handles promotion and industry statistics. To understand Champagne, start with that binding of name, land and method.

## From the 1927 delimitation to the 1936 AOC

The modern boundary was drawn by law, not by nature. The law of 22 July 1927 delimited the Champagne vineyard zone and turned a dispute over departments into a dispute over parcels, following earlier adjustments going back to 1908. In 1935 France created the AOC system and a national committee; on 29 June 1936 Champagne was formally recognised as an AOC. The two dates are often conflated: 1935 is the birth of the system, 1936 the recognition of Champagne.

The current specification was approved by an order of 30 November 2022, published on 10 December 2022, and replaced the 2010 decree. It limits Champagne to sparkling wine made in the delimited area by the traditional method and protects “Champagne” as the sales name. The rules connect to the French [appellation](ref:appellation) system, to the EU PDO register and to the [AOC system created in 1935](ref:aoc-1935). Champagne's strong global protection comes from the combination of national law, EU law and international geographical-indication agreements, not from a single clause.

## Area, villages and varieties

The vineyard lies about 150 kilometres east of Paris and spans five departments: Marne, Aisne, Aube, Haute-Marne and Seine-et-Marne. It covers roughly 34,000 hectares across more than 300 communes (crus). Industry sources often cite 319 crus; historical accounts of the 1927 settlement mention claims over 407 communes before the area was reduced to about 34,000 hectares and some 300 communes. There are four main growing districts: the Montagne de Reims, the Vallée de la Marne, the Côte des Blancs and the Côte des Bar. Seventeen villages may use “grand cru” and 42 may use “premier cru”; the classification dates from the old cru scale, which INAO abolished in 2010 while keeping the two terms.

Seven grape varieties are principally allowed: Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier, plus Arbane, Petit Meslier, Pinot Blanc and Pinot Gris. The first three dominate; the other four are historical varieties planted in small quantities, mostly by a few traditional houses. The current specification also allows Voltis, an adaptation variety, under strict proportion and agreement rules; the exact limit should be checked in the text. Harvesting must be done by hand to keep bunches intact and limit oxidation and premature breakage.

## Second fermentation: bubbles made in the bottle

Champagne's bubbles come from a second fermentation in the bottle, not from a large pressure tank as in most inexpensive sparkling wines. After harvest, grapes are pressed gently and only the free-run and tail fractions are used, within the specification's yield limits. The first fermentation produces a dry base wine, usually in stainless steel or oak. The wines are then blended (assemblage) across villages, varieties and, for non-vintage wines, years. Non-vintage Champagne relies on this blend for consistency; vintage Champagne uses grapes from a single year.

The blend is bottled with the liqueur de tirage, a mixture of wine, sugar and yeast, and sealed. The second fermentation takes place in the bottle, producing carbon dioxide that dissolves into the wine; it usually lasts six to eight weeks. The wine then ages on its lees, and yeast autolysis gives the bready, biscuity and creamy aromas associated with Champagne. After ageing, riddling (remuage) gathers the sediment at the neck, disgorging (dégorgement) removes it, and a dosage adjusts sweetness before the cork and wire cage are fitted.

## Ageing, pressure and yields

Minimum ageing is the figure most often simplified. Non-vintage Champagne must age for at least 15 months from bottling, including at least 12 months on the lees; vintage Champagne must age for at least 36 months. “At least” matters: most houses age longer, and prestige cuvées often spend three to ten years or more in the cellar. The clock starts at bottling (tirage), not at harvest or first fermentation, so “36 months” does not mean three years in oak; the main ageing happens in the bottle.

On pressure, EU sparkling-wine rules require an excess pressure of at least 3 bar at 20°C; Champagne, as a quality sparkling wine, has a higher specification threshold, and finished bottles typically sit at 5 to 6 bar. The exact minimum should be checked in the current specification and EU rules rather than reduced to one figure for every bottle size. Yields are capped by the specification and may be adjusted by INAO in difficult years. Pruning, planting density, maximum yield and pressing ratios turn “terroir” into verifiable agricultural parameters.

## Labels: the word Champagne and the dosage scale

“Champagne” is the legal sales name and must appear clearly on the label; “sparkling wine” is not a substitute. Labels must also state alcohol strength, nominal volume, lot number, producer or brand, country of origin (“Produit de France”), allergen warnings and the health message required in France. A vintage claim requires all grapes to come from the stated year; a varietal claim requires 100% of that variety; “blanc de blancs” uses white varieties only, “blanc de noirs” black varieties only, and “rosé” follows the permitted methods.

The dosage scale is Champagne's other label language. From driest to sweetest, the common terms are brut nature (below 3 g/L, also called non dosé), extra brut (0–6 g/L), brut (below 12 g/L), extra sec (12–17 g/L), sec (17–32 g/L), demi-sec (32–50 g/L) and doux (above 50 g/L). These are labelling rules, not the whole sensory story: acidity, body and ageing all change perceived sweetness, so “brut” does not mean “completely dry”.

## How it differs from other sparkling wines

Champagne and [sparkling wine](ref:sparkling) stand in a species-to-genus relationship: all Champagne is sparkling wine, but not all sparkling wine may be called Champagne. Three differences matter. First, origin: only wine made in the delimited villages of the [Champagne region](ref:champagne) under the specification may use the name. Second, method: Champagne must undergo a second fermentation in the bottle and age on the lees; Spanish Cava also uses the traditional method but has different regions, varieties and ageing rules, while Italian Prosecco mostly ferments in tanks and is fresher and fruitier. Third, name protection: “méthode champenoise” is restricted in the EU, so traditional-method producers elsewhere usually say “traditional method” or “méthode traditionnelle”.

For drinkers, the useful test is not “does it have bubbles” but the region, variety, dosage and producer on the label. For writers, the trap is to use Champagne as a generic term or to treat “traditional method” and “Champagne method” as synonyms. Champagne's identity comes from a set of verifiable rules, not from one step in the process. It is also the clearest example of the French [appellation](ref:appellation) system in action.
