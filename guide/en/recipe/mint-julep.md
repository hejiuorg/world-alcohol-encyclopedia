# Mint Julep recipe (IBA version)

The modern identity of the Mint Julep is almost inseparable from the Kentucky Derby: on the first Saturday in May, the crowd at Churchill Downs holds this drink, the metal cup white with frost and a thick bunch of mint at the rim. That picture is only about a century old, while the julep itself is older — nineteenth-century pharmacies and hotels in the American South were already selling mint-and-ice drinks of the same family. This page uses the IBA Contemporary Classic version: 60 ml bourbon, 4 fresh mint sprigs, 1 tsp powdered sugar and 2 tsp water, gently pressed in a julep cup, filled with crushed ice and stirred until the cup frosts.

## Crushed ice is the body

Many people treat crushed ice as the tool that makes the drink cold, but in a Mint Julep the ice is the structure itself. Its large surface area pulls the liquid close to freezing within seconds and keeps diluting it slowly, so 60 ml of bourbon moves from a strong first sip to a softer second half. Make the drink with large cubes and the temperature curve changes completely; it drinks more like iced whiskey with mint.

Crushed ice also decides the cup. It must mound near the rim, leave room to stir and sit deep enough to frost the outside; too small a cup cannot hold enough ice to chill the drink, while too wide a cup makes a thin ice layer that frosts poorly and gathers little aroma. That is why a julep cup is usually tall and narrow.

The size of the crush matters too. Ice crushed too fine melts fast and turns the drink watery within minutes; too coarse and it behaves like ordinary cubes, chilling slowly and frosting badly. Good crushed ice is distinct in grain and holds together in the hand without dripping — quick to chill, not instant to melt.

## 60 ml bourbon with 1 tsp sugar and 2 tsp water

The IBA ratio reads as 60 ml of base spirit with 1 tsp powdered sugar and 2 tsp water. The sugar and water are small in volume; they do not make the drink sweet, they draw out the mint and give the bourbon a little roundness. Bourbon dominates, and that is the premise; push the sugar or water higher and the drink becomes a minted sweet.

Powdered sugar dissolves more easily than granulated. Its fine grain forms a damp paste with the 2 tsp of water, and the mint oils are easier to capture during the gentle press; granulated sugar dissolves slowly, sinks, and makes the last sips suddenly sweet. If only granulated is available, dissolve it in a little water first — an execution adjustment, not a recipe change.

The 2 tsp of water also pre-wets the leaves. Dry mint shreds easily under pressure, while mint mixed with sugar water bends and releases its oil more evenly. The water does not noticeably dilute the finished drink, but it stops the sugar clumping at the bottom and makes the later ice dilution more predictable.

## Press the mint, do not crush it

A gentle press and a hard muddle are two different operations. A press only breaks the leaves slightly and releases the surface oils; hard muddling pulls out chlorophyll, bitter compounds and fibre, so the drink turns bitter, green and black within minutes. The IBA step says "gently press a few times", and those words are a technical instruction, not politeness.

In practice, use the blunt end of a muddler or bar spoon and press straight down two or three times until the leaves just break, without grinding in circles. Mint aroma comes mainly from glands on the leaf surface; breaking them is enough, and grinding only adds bitterness. Let the mint and sugar water stand for half a minute before adding spirit so the aroma settles.

Freshness matters as much. Yellowing leaves and blackened stems carry weak aroma and a grassy taste; four sprigs is the IBA quantity, but variety and leaf size change the intensity. Keeping one good sprig for the garnish and pressing the other three is the usual division.

## The frosted cup: a visible signal of temperature

"Stir until the cup frosts" is the most visible sign that the drink is ready. Metal conducts heat quickly, so when the inside is cold enough, moisture in the air condenses and freezes on the outside; white frost means the liquid is near freezing and ready to serve. A glass cup also frosts, but more slowly and less visibly, so you rely on touch.

Frost is not only decoration. It means the chilling is complete and the crushed ice has done its work, and it means dilution has happened so the first sip is not too strong. If the cup will not frost after a long stir, the usual causes are too little ice, too large a cup or a warm room; add ice rather than stirring longer.

Frost fades after serving, and that is normal. The ideal rhythm is to drink the first half while the frost is still there, with temperature and dilution at their best; drink too slowly and the ice all melts, leaving mint water in the second half.

## Metal cup, glass cup and vessel shape

The IBA specifies a "Julep Stainless Steel Cup". Metal conducts heat quickly, frosts readily and feels cold in the hand; the [rocks glass](ref:vessel) registered on this site is the published vessel closest in capacity and use, though the exact vessel still follows the official IBA version. A rocks glass is short and wide, so the ice layer is thinner and the frost is less pronounced than on a tall, narrow metal cup.

A glass cup works, but the temperature curve differs. Glass conducts slowly, so chilling depends more on contact between ice and liquid and frosting is slower; the advantage is that you can see the layers and the colour of the mint, the disadvantage that the cold does not last. Pre-chill the glass and add a little more ice.

Shape also affects aroma. A tall, narrow rim concentrates the mint, while a wide rim lets it escape. Mint is the main aromatic source here, so the rim should not be too wide; with only a wide glass, use a little less garnish so the aroma is not spread too thin.

## The racing tradition and the setting

The Mint Julep's link to the Kentucky Derby is a twentieth-century development. The racecourse turned it into a social ritual: the metal cup, the ice mound, the bunch of mint, set against formal dress and hats to form a complete picture of a Southern summer. That picture made the drink famous and also reduced it to an impression of "sweet, very icy, very minty".

A real julep is not a sweet drink. The IBA version uses only 1 tsp of sugar, so the result is dry, with mint as aroma rather than as a vehicle for syrup. Racecourse versions sometimes add more sugar, use finer ice and are batched ahead of time; those choices suit a crowd and a schedule, but they are not the same recipe as the IBA version.

The setting also changes the method. Outdoors in the heat, ice melts fast and the drink should be made to order; in an air-conditioned room, ice melts slowly, so a little less ice avoids over-dilution. For the general principles of ice and temperature, read [ice and temperature](ref:ice); for cocktail structure, read [cocktail basics](ref:cocktails-guide).

## Common mistakes

First, shredding the mint. Hard muddling releases bitterness and chlorophyll, and the mint turns black quickly. Second, undissolved sugar. Powdered sugar at the bottom makes the last sips too sweet and upsets the balance. Third, too little ice or using large cubes: chilling is insufficient, the cup does not frost, and the drink stays strong and less refreshing.

Fourth, too short a stir. The liquid and ice do not meet enough, the temperature falls slowly and the mint aroma never opens. Fifth, garnish left too long: a mint sprig soaked in the drink turns black, loses aroma and can bring a grassy taste. Sixth, a cup that is too small: crushed ice and mint take space, so a small cup limits the ice and makes stirring difficult.

Seventh, mint that is not fresh. Yellow leaves or black stems carry weak aroma, and what the press releases is grass; changing the mint is more effective than adding sugar. Eighth, treating frost as the goal rather than the temperature. Frost is a result, not a method; stirring longer just to see it only melts more ice.

## Variations

Replacing bourbon with rye gives a spicier frame; replacing it with aged rum makes another base-spirit version. Replacing powdered sugar with syrup or honey syrup changes sweetness and dissolution; honey syrup has a longer finish, while syrup mixes more easily with crushed ice.

Replacing crushed ice with large cubes gives slower chilling and slower dilution, pushing the drink towards iced whiskey; replacing the metal cup with glass changes both the frost and the temperature curve, and the equipment difference should be stated. Some branches add a little soda water or lemon juice for freshness, but that changes the sweet-sour structure and cannot keep the same title without a note.

When recording a variation, say which ingredient changed, how the ratio moved and whether the equipment changed; otherwise the same name will describe very different drinks. This page records only the IBA version; other approaches should carry their own sources.
