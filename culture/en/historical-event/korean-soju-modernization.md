# A History of Korean Soju: From Distilled Spirit to the Green Bottle

Soju today usually means the clear liquid in a green glass bottle: cheap, low in alcohol, sweet on the tongue, made for repeated rounds at barbecue restaurants and company dinners. It is not, however, the same drink as the historical soju distilled from rice. The two lineages split in the mid-20th century: one continues a centuries-old grain-distilling tradition, represented by Andong soju; the other is an industrial diluted soju born under the food policy of 1965. The history is less about which is more authentic than about how grain, taxation and industrialisation successively rewrote what the same name refers to.

## Date and place

The origin of soju is usually traced to 13th-century Goryeo, when distilleries appeared around the capital, then Kaegyeong (today Kaesong). Exchange between the Mongol Yuan and Goryeo brought distillation techniques from the Middle East and Central Asia into the peninsula, where they were combined with local rice wine to make a strong grain spirit. Andong, in today's North Gyeongsang Province, preserved a household distilling tradition using rice, barley, millet, sorghum and beans; the result can reach 45 percent alcohol and was long used as medicine and for entertaining guests. Industrial production in the 20th century concentrated around Seoul and the Chungcheong and Gyeongsang regions; the food policy of 1965 was national, while the regional production system of 1973 divided the market by administrative area.

## Background: rice wine, distillation and the Joseon dynasty

The earliest alcohol on the Korean peninsula was rice wine: grain fermented with nuruk starter, with the clear upper liquid drunk as cheongju and the cloudy bottom layer as makgeolli. After distillation arrived, breweries redistilled fermented liquor to obtain a spirit that kept longer and carried more alcohol. In the Joseon period soju entered court, official and household banquets and was also used as a medicinal wine; when grain was scarce, the government issued bans or limits on brewing, so soju repeatedly swung between luxury and everyday drink.

During the Japanese colonial period (1910–1945), alcohol production was brought under licensing and taxation, and the space for household brewing narrowed. In 1924 JINRO was founded in Korea, becoming an early industrial producer of soju. The market was still dominated by traditional distilled spirit and rice wine, but licensing, tax burdens and raw-material controls had already prepared the ground for later concentration.

## Course: the 1965 Grain Management Act and diluted soju

In the 1960s the South Korean government prioritised food supply within an export-oriented industrialisation drive. After Park Chung-hee took power in 1963, food self-sufficiency remained low; the 1965 Grain Management Act restricted the use of rice in brewing, and the rice shortage of 1966 tightened enforcement further. The result was not merely less rice but a different production method: alcohol of at least 95% was made from cheap starch such as tapioca, sweet potato and wheat, then diluted with water and sweeteners to produce very low-cost diluted soju.

Traditional distilled soju suffered badly. Andong soju and other rice-based distilled spirits were banned from production, and household breweries went underground or closed; green-bottle diluted soju took the market with low prices, lower strength and steady supply. It began at around 30% alcohol and later fell to the teens as competition and tastes changed. In 1973 the government introduced a regional production system, allocating the national market by area and creating local brands and local protection.

According to the account compiled by a Korean drinks organisation, the rice ban was lifted in 1977; by then, however, green-bottle soju had already shaped consumer habits, and traditional distilled soju struggled to return to the mainstream. In 1981 sales-area limits were relaxed and regional brands began entering each other's markets; in 1996 all restrictions on alcohol production were removed and the ban on household brewing was also lifted, bringing soju into nationwide competition and brand marketing.

## Impact and institutions

First, industrial concentration. The protected era left local producers, but the two liberalising steps of 1981 and 1996 pushed competition nationwide; advertising, endorsements and distribution decided the winners, and large makers such as JINRO expanded their share.

Second, the shape of consumption. The green bottle, 360 millilitres, low strength and sweetness made soju the standard accompaniment of gatherings and company dinners; it demands no tasting ritual and suits fast, repeated drinking.

Third, protection of traditional alcohol. In 1987 Andong soju was designated an intangible cultural heritage of North Gyeongsang Province, giving institutional recognition to its distilling craft and household tradition; the traditional-liquor revival later brought it back to the market, though on a much smaller scale than industrial soju.

Fourth, overseas spread. As Korean food and popular culture travelled, soju became one of the fastest-growing Korean alcohol exports; the green-bottle image familiar abroad is itself a product of the industrial path taken after 1965.

## Sources and disputes

First, origin stories compete. Korean and Japanese companies and local histories all stress a tradition of several centuries, but scholarship does not agree on the exact route by which distillation arrived in the 13th century, the earliest documentary record, or the original meaning of the word soju.

Second, the scope of the 1965 policy is described differently. Government material says rice was banned in brewing and distilled soju production was banned; some popular articles summarise it as a ban on all grain brewing. The two are related but not identical.

Third, the year the rice ban ended needs care. 1977 is the common figure, but policy was often adjusted by administrative notice, and the effective date differs across sources; presenting it as a clean repeal of a statute may oversimplify.

Fourth, the fall in alcohol strength from 30% to the teens cannot be attributed only to "consumers preferring lighter drinks". Taxation, price competition, distribution structure and health awareness all played a part, and single-cause explanations lack evidence.

Fifth, most sources are companies, government publicity and drinks-culture bodies. JINRO and other firms have an interest in promoting their own history, and government documents emphasise policy intent; judgments about market structure are best cross-checked against statistical yearbooks.

## Timeline

- 13th century: exchange between the Yuan and Goryeo brings distillation to the peninsula; soju appears (inferred).
- 1910–1945: Japanese colonial rule brings alcohol production under licensing and taxation.
- 1924: JINRO is founded in Korea.
- 1965: the Grain Management Act restricts rice in brewing and bans distilled soju production.
- 1966: the rice shortage worsens, and alternative starch and diluted soju expand.
- 1973: the regional production system divides the market by area.
- 1977: according to industry history, the rice ban is lifted.
- 1981: sales-area limits are relaxed and local brands enter each other's markets.
- 1987: Andong soju is designated an intangible cultural heritage of North Gyeongsang Province.
- 1996: all restrictions on alcohol production are removed and the household brewing ban is lifted.
