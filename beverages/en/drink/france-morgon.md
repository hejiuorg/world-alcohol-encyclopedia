# Morgon

Morgon is a village-level cru in northern [Beaujolais](ref:beaujolais), France, making [red wine](ref:red-wine) from [Gamay](ref:gamay). Its vineyards sit mainly on granite slopes around Villié-Morgon, and Côte du Py is its signature slope. It is commonly 750 mL at about 12.5%–14% [alcohol by volume](ref:abv).

## Definition and position

Start with the levels: [red wine](ref:red-wine) is the category, Gamay is the variety, Morgon is one of the ten village crus of Beaujolais, and individual estates and wines are the producer level. Morgon is both a village name and an appellation name that may appear on the label alone, usually without the regional Beaujolais name.

Morgon has no premier or grand cru tiers, but parcel differences still matter. Named sites such as Côte du Py, Les Charmes, Grand Cras and Douby are often labelled separately by producers to distinguish style and ripeness.

## Producer and brand

Family estates dominate the village, with merchants and cooperatives also taking part. Some estates own core parcels such as Côte du Py, while others buy grapes or wine and bottle it; the bottler information on the label is the key to telling estate wine from merchant wine.

Producer, appellation and product are three levels: the appellation answers for rules and parcel range, the producer answers for who farms, who makes and who bottles, and the product answers for vintage, site and specification. The same Morgon can differ noticeably by slope position and estate.

## Region and ingredients

Morgon lies in northern [Beaujolais](ref:beaujolais) in [France](ref:france), with vineyards on the hills around the village of Villié-Morgon. The northern cru belt is mostly weathered granite, draining well and low in fertility; the Côte du Py area also contains schist and volcanic rock, giving more complex soil.

Aspect and altitude produce differences in ripeness: south- or south-east-facing slopes see more sun, and higher parcels keep better acidity. [Gamay](ref:gamay) ripens well here, so the wine is fuller than regional Beaujolais. The official delimitation governs the boundaries.

The raw material is [grape](ref:grape), of the Gamay variety. Gamay is thin-skinned with high acidity, and on granite soils it shows red and dark fruit, flowers, and clear mineral and earthy notes.

## Winemaking

Morgon often uses whole clusters, and some estates use semi-carbonic maceration: whole grapes start intracellular fermentation in a carbon-dioxide-filled vessel before pressing and a conventional fermentation. This brings fruit and lower initial tannin while retaining some structure.

Fermentation temperature and maceration time shape colour, tannin and aroma. Wines aiming at ageing see longer maceration and more extraction; those aiming at easy drinking are shorter and lighter. Some wines go into [oak](ref:oak-ageing) to integrate aroma and soften tannin, usually with a modest proportion of new barrels so as not to mask Gamay's fruit.

## Specification and label

The common volume is 750 mL, with [alcohol by volume](ref:abv) at about 12.5%–14% vol, varying by vintage and estate. The label should be checked for the cru name, site name (such as Côte du Py), vintage, bottler and importer.

Morgon is a standalone cru name and need not appear alongside Beaujolais; where both appear, check whether the wine is village-level or another tier. The Chinese back label, importer and anti-counterfeit information are the basis for checking identity, and the bottle on hand governs.

## Flavour and tasting

Typical aromas are black cherry, plum, raspberry, violet and earth, with some wines showing pepper and granitic minerality; the palate has clear acidity, medium-plus tannin and a medium-to-full body. Compared with lighter crus such as Fleurie, Morgon has more backbone and better withstands ageing.

Serve at about 15–17 degrees Celsius in a tulip-shaped or Burgundy glass. Young wines can be decanted for 30 minutes to an hour, while older wines should be decanted briefly. Watch the layering of fruit and earth; excessive oak masks Gamay's floral side.

## Drinking and pairing

It suits poultry, roast pork, charcuterie, mushroom dishes and medium-strength cheese; the wine's structure carries fat and savoury depth. Avoid very hot or sharply acidic dishes, which mask the fruit and minerality.

Most wines are for drinking within 4–10 years, and core sites such as Côte du Py can go longer; the window depends on vintage, site and estate. After opening, refrigerate and finish within a few days. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

## Buying and storage

Buy from reputable channels and check the cru name, site name, vintage and bottler information. Morgon is one of the larger and fuller crus in Beaujolais, and prices vary widely with site, estate and vintage, so no figure is given here.

For Morgon, unopened bottles should be kept away from light, lying flat in a cool place at an ideal 12–15 degrees Celsius, avoiding heat and sharp temperature swings. Cork-sealed bottles need humidity and can be stored lying down. After opening, use a vacuum or inert gas and refrigerate rather than warming and cooling repeatedly.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating Morgon as Beaujolais Nouveau. It is a separate village cru built on structure and ageing rather than the fruit of the current year.
Second, thinking Gamay cannot age. Core sites in Morgon can age noticeably.
Third, equating Côte du Py with all of Morgon. It is only one signature slope within the village.
Fourth, assuming every Morgon is heavy. Extraction varies greatly by slope position and estate.
Fifth, judging authenticity by price or packaging. Channel, label and bottler information are more reliable.

Read [Red wine](ref:red-wine) for the style side of Morgon, and [Beaujolais](ref:beaujolais) for the region and vintage side. Together they locate it.
