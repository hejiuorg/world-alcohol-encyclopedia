# Wheat

Wheat is an annual cereal of the genus Triticum in the grass family, with common wheat, Triticum aestivum, dominating cultivation. It is both a global staple and a brewing raw material: qu making, multi-grain baijiu, beer and whisky may all use wheat or wheat products. Compared with sorghum and rice, wheat contains more protein and gluten, which gives it a different role in qu making and fermentation than a pure starch source.

## Definition and forms

A wheat grain consists of bran layers, endosperm and germ. The endosperm is mostly starch, while the bran contains fibre and some protein. Grain colour separates white wheat from red wheat; sowing season separates spring wheat from winter wheat; hardness separates hard wheat from soft wheat. Brewing does not prize "the whiter the flour, the better"; it looks at total starch, protein proportion, grain integrity and freshness.

Hard wheat usually has more protein and stronger gluten, while soft wheat has a higher starch proportion and is easier to extract and ferment. Different uses drive very different breeding goals: bread wheat emphasises gluten quality, whereas brewing wheat emphasises starch availability and performance in qu. Applying the grading standards of food wheat directly to brewing raw material tends to ignore process differences.

## Main producing regions

Wheat is grown across temperate zones, with China, India, Russia, the United States, France and Canada among the main producers. Chinese wheat regions run from the North China Plain into the Yangtze basin, with clear differences in climate, varieties and cultivation. Regional differences affect grain moisture, protein and impurity levels, and therefore the stability of qu making and fermentation.

Wheat from the same region also varies by year. Rain at harvest can cause sprouting or mould and lower grain quality, while poor storage brings pests and moisture uptake. Large distilleries usually test incoming wheat for moisture, impurities, starch and mouldy kernels; these indicators say more about actual quality than appearance.

## Role in qu making

[Qu making](ref:qu-making) is one of the most typical brewing uses of wheat. Daqu is often made mainly from wheat, sometimes with barley and peas, formed into bricks and cultured in a qu room. Wheat protein is broken down by microbes during culture, supplying amino acids and flavour precursors, while starch provides carbon for moulds, yeasts and bacteria.

Wheat qu is not a case of "more protein is better". Too much protein makes the qu block heat up quickly, clump and raise the risk of unwanted microbes; too little leaves the aroma layers thin. Qu makers balance these factors through raw material ratios, water content, block tightness and the timing of turning. Qu quality ultimately shows in saccharifying power, fermenting power and flavour, not in a single compositional number.

## Place in baijiu

In [baijiu](ref:baijiu), wheat usually appears in two ways: as a daqu raw material and as a grain in multi-grain recipes. [Strong-aroma baijiu](ref:strong-aroma) commonly uses sorghum, rice, glutinous rice, wheat and maize together; wheat contributes protein breakdown products and a certain roundness. Sauce-aroma baijiu puts more emphasis on wheat for qu making, while sorghum remains the main grain charged into the fermentation.

A distinction matters here between "wheat for qu" and "wheat for grain". The former enters qu making, the latter is steamed and fermented alongside sorghum, and the quality requirements are not identical. Multi-grain recipes are not a contest to include as many grains as possible; they combine different raw materials for complementary starch, protein and aroma precursors. This helps explain why, even within similar qu or fermentation systems, different distilleries' recipes produce different styles.

## In beer and whisky

In [beer](ref:beer), wheat is often used as wheat malt. Wheat malt brings a fuller body, better foam retention and a light bread or clove note; German wheat beers are a typical example. When the wheat proportion is too high, protein and aleurone material can cause haze and filtration difficulties, so it must be matched with base malt, enzyme action and the saccharification programme.

[Whisky](ref:whisky) is based mainly on malted barley, but some products use wheat as a cereal ingredient, and American wheat whisky requires wheat as the main grain. Wheat supplies fermentable sugar during saccharification and, after distillation, often shows a softer, bready and spicy style. Different drinks treat wheat differently; beer's raw material standards cannot simply be transferred to whisky.

## Protein, gluten and process fit

Wheat protein and gluten are double-edged. Gluten gives dough elasticity, but in brewing, very strong gluten can make the mash viscous and filtration difficult; moderate protein breakdown supplies amino acids that support yeast growth. Saccharification temperature, enzyme contact time and grind size all change how efficiently protein and starch are used.

Brewing wheat therefore cannot be judged by protein level alone; the question is whether it fits the intended process. Qu making needs some protein to support microbial growth, beer saccharification cares more about starch extraction and smooth filtration, and whisky cares about fermentable sugar yield. The same lot of wheat can behave differently under different processes, which is why ingredient pages should be read together with process pages.

## Buying and storage

When buying, look at grain plumpness, colour uniformity, impurities and broken kernels, moisture and mouldy grains. Brewing wheat should be fresh, free of musty odour and pests. Store it dry, ventilated and dark to prevent moisture uptake and insects; mouldy wheat can carry mycotoxin risk and should not enter brewing.

For Wheat, begin with the form and provenance. Then look at the preparation, and finally ask how it behaves in the drink. The answers point to different choices, not to a single score.

## Common misconceptions

First, that wheat is only a flour ingredient. It has a clear place in qu making, beer and whisky.

Second, that higher protein is always better for brewing. Too much raises viscosity and microbial risk; the key is process fit.

Third, that more wheat in multi-grain baijiu is better. Ratios serve a flavour goal, not a quantity contest.

Fourth, that wheat beer differs from ordinary beer only in raw material. The wheat proportion changes saccharification, filtration, foam and aroma.

Fifth, that mouldy wheat can be washed and used. The risks from mould do not disappear with washing.

**Short definition:** An annual cereal of the genus Triticum, used as a staple food and as a key ingredient in qu making, multi-grain baijiu, beer and whisky; the balance of protein and starch shapes both qu quality and the body of the drink.
