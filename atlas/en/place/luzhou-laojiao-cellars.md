# Luzhou Laojiao Cellars

Luzhou sits where the Yangtze and Tuo rivers meet, in south-eastern Sichuan, and is known as a city of wine. Guojiao Square in Jiangyang District begins with a stone marker reading "Origin of Chinese Strong-Aroma Baijiu", then opens into a Ming Wanli-era brewing workshop and rows of fermentation pits. This is not a museum of bottles behind glass; it is a working heritage site. The 1573 National Treasure Cellar group has been brewing continuously since 1573, more than 450 years without a break.

## Setting and history

Luzhou Laojiao's heritage is a system, not a single point. At its core is the 1573 National Treasure Cellar group, built in the first year of the Ming Wanli reign (1573) and used continuously for more than 450 years. It is described as one of China's earliest, longest-used and best-preserved "living" cellar groups. Around it are 1619 century-old pits, 16 Ming and Qing brewing workshops, and three natural storage caves: Chunyang, Longquan and Zuihong. In 1996 the 1573 group entered the fourth batch of national key cultural relics; in 2013 the 1619 century-old pits, the 16 workshops and the three caves were added to the same national list; in 2018 the pits and workshops entered the national industrial heritage list.

The numbers need separating. "1573" is the founding year of the core group, not the age of all 1619 pits. "More than 450 years" is the length of continuous brewing, not a rough statement that "the Ming dynasty was exactly 450 years ago". "1619 pits" is the total of pits over a century old, of which the 1573 group is the oldest and most central. Official material also says Luzhou Laojiao holds 91.3% of the industry's century-old pits; that share shows its position in strong-aroma resources, not that "all old pits are in Luzhou".

Technique is the other half. Luzhou Laojiao's traditional brewing technique has passed through more than 700 years and 24 generations, and in 2006 it entered the first national intangible cultural heritage list. Together with the 1573 pits it forms the "living dual national treasures". Strong-aroma baijiu follows the rule "a thousand-year old pit, a ten-thousand-year old grain": the older the mud pit, the more stable the microbial community in the pit mud, and the more readily the wine develops a rich cellar aroma. Luzhou Laojiao's pits are built with yellow mud from the Wudu River, water from the Longquan well and bamboo pins; the microbes in the mud have been shaped over centuries, which is the material basis of "old pits make good wine".

In 2024 the company was recognised by the China Food Industry Association as the "origin of Chinese strong-aroma baijiu". In 2025 the Luzhou Laojiao archives (1771–1983) entered the China Archives Heritage List; in 2026 the Luohan pit group was listed as a Sichuan provincial cultural relic. The titles keep accumulating, but the point is that this is not merely a distillery; it is a sample of Chinese baijiu heritage.

## The site and what to see

The route at Guojiao Square runs roughly "square—Longquan well—Ming cellar group—Qing brewing workshop—heritage exhibition hall—wine-history corridor—intangible-heritage workshop". Three things are essential. First, the 1573 National Treasure Cellar group: the pits sit inside the workshop, covered with pit mud, while workers follow the traditional sequence of lifting the grain, mixing, loading the steamer and distilling. Visitors see living production, not a static model. Second, the Longquan well: its water is part of the brewing system, and the well, pits and workshops together form a "water–mud–grain–starter" system. Third, the natural caves such as Chunyang: constant temperature and humidity, ceramic jars, and wine maturing in storage. Cave storage is not a marketing conceit; it is a practical part of strong-aroma maturation.

The 16 Ming and Qing workshops are distributed across the city and its surroundings. Guojiao Square is the core visitor site, not the whole heritage. Xiaoshi, Yinggoutou and Luohan have their own workshops and pits, some still producing and some preserved for display. An ordinary visitor can cover Guojiao Square and the museum in a day; visiting all 16 requires advance arrangement with the scenic area or a specialist study trip, not a spontaneous "see it all" stop.

In the museum and exhibition halls, focus on the implements, wine history, old photographs, archives and brewing tools. Do not only look at the "gold medal" and "famous wine" panels. More useful are the pit cross-sections, the introduction to pit-mud microbes, the brewing process diagrams and the wine labels of different periods. They explain why strong-aroma baijiu needs old pits, and why Luzhou Laojiao treats "living" production as the core of conservation.

## A first visit

Allow 2–3 hours. The site opens at 09:00; exchange your ticket at the visitor centre and follow the route: photograph the stone marker, see the Longquan well, watch the Ming cellar group, look at the steamers and cooling floors in the Qing workshop, then the museum. If a guided tour is available, take it; the guide is faster than reading every panel, and guides usually need advance booking, especially in peak season.

The point is not how much you drink but how much you understand. At the pits, notice the colour and smell of the mud, and how workers lift the grain, mix it and load the steamer. In the distillation area, notice the steamer, condenser and collection point. In the cave, notice the jar numbers and storage years. Photography is allowed, but no flash, no crossing barriers, no touching the pit mud or jars.

The tasting usually offers a small glass of raw cellar wine, such as 68% Guojiao 1573. Sip it slowly; non-drinkers can smell or skip it. Do not taste on an empty stomach and do not turn it into a drinking contest. Smoking and open flames are prohibited in the brewing core area; this is a production-safety rule, not an ordinary "no smoking" notice.

## Practical information

**Address**: Guojiao Square, Sanxing Street, Jiangyang District, Luzhou 646000, China. Navigate to "Luzhou Laojiao Scenic Area" or "Guojiao Square"; the visitor centre is at the entrance, and there is an underground car park that fills up in peak season.

**Transport**: Bus 148 or 271 to Guojiao Square, then walk; about 20 minutes by taxi from Luzhou bus station and about 40 minutes from Luzhou Yunlong Airport. The city is small and ride-hailing is easy; roads around the site are congested at rush hour.

**Opening hours**: 09:00–17:30, with last entry around 17:00. Whether the museum closes on Mondays and whether holidays change the hours should be checked with the site. `operating_status` is `verified`, meaning the scenic area and official tourism page still showed it as open on 20 November 2026; this does not guarantee that it opens every day.

**Tickets and reservations**: A CNY 35 pass includes the audio guide and a small tasting of raw cellar wine; students pay CNY 25. Discounts are subject to the day's notice. Book through the scenic area's WeChat account, Tongcheng, Ctrip or Meituan, or buy on site. In peak season and around the sealing ceremony, book a guide 2–3 days ahead.

**Visitor restrictions**: No smoking, open flames or drones in the brewing core area; do not touch pits or jars; some workshops are closed during production and visitors must follow the signs. Wear flat shoes, because the floor can be wet and slippery with spent grain. In summer the workshops are hot, so carry water.

## Etiquette and common mistakes

First, do not treat 1573 as the year the whole scenic area was built. 1573 is the founding year of the National Treasure Cellar group; the visitor centre and museum came later. Second, do not treat "1619 pits" as "all the pits". It is the number of pits over a century old; younger pits are not counted. Third, do not treat "living heritage" as "old things you can touch". The pits are still producing, and the mud and fermenting grain are production materials, not exhibits.

Three more mistakes are common. One is assuming older pits always mean better wine. Old pits are an important condition for strong-aroma baijiu, but grain, starter, technique and storage matter as much; origin alone is not enough. Another is treating cave storage as a gimmick. Stable temperature and humidity do help wine mature, but not every wine must be cave-stored. The third is treating the site as "a place to buy liquor". The core is the pits, workshops and technique; shopping is secondary, not the main event.

The site is not right for someone with no interest in brewing who only wants a photo, anyone bringing very young children into the production halls (the smell and heat are unsuitable), or someone who expects an electric cart to cover everything. It suits anyone interested in fermentation, microbiology, industrial heritage and Chinese drinks history, and anyone building a wine trip through southern Sichuan.

## Nearby and routes

Guojiao Square is in the city. North are the Tuo River and the riverside road; south is the Yangtze and the direction of Zhangba Osmanthus Forest; west are the Luzhou Laojiao Museum and the old Sanxing Street area. Put the site on a "Guojiao Square—Luzhou Laojiao Museum—riverside road" route: pits in the morning, Luzhou white cake, yellow cake or tofu pudding at midday, museum and river in the afternoon, the confluence of the Yangtze and Tuo at dusk. About half a day with walking and taxis.

For a "Chinese baijiu heritage" theme, add the Shuijingfang Museum in Chengdu and the Fenjiu Museum in Fenyang. They represent strong-aroma, a strong-aroma site and light-aroma respectively; the distances are large, so split them across trips. For a deep Luzhou visit, Guojiao Square plus Chunyang Cave and the Xiaoshi workshop are enough for a day; do not try three cities in one day.

Luzhou is hot and humid in summer, and the temperature difference between the workshops and the caves is large, so bring a light jacket. Roads are slippery in rain; do not walk quickly near the pits. During the sealing ceremony, the wine expo or group inspections, crowds and traffic increase, so check notices in advance.
