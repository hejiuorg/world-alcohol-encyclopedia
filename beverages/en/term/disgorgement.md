# Disgorgement

Disgorgement (French dégorgement) is the step in traditional-method sparkling wine, after riddling, in which the yeast sediment gathered at the bottle neck is ejected. The sediment comes from the second fermentation in bottle: yeast converts sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, then dies and forms lees. Riddling moves the lees gradually into the neck, and disgorgement removes them. Dosage usually follows, then the final cork and wire hood. It is the closing move of the traditional method and the end of the wine's contact with the lees.

## Definition and boundaries

First, disgorgement is not filtration. It removes only the sediment gathered at the neck, and the wine does not pass through a filter medium. Second, disgorgement is not riddling: riddling gathers, disgorgement ejects. Third, it is not required for every sparkling wine. Tank method completes the second fermentation in a pressure tank, and transfer method empties the wine into a tank, filters it and refills bottles, so neither disgorges bottle by bottle. Fourth, the disgorgement date is not the bottling date; the two can be months or years apart. Fifth, disgorgement can happen in bottle but can also be replaced by tank handling in the transfer method; read the label or production notes rather than the word "disgorgement" alone.

## Etymology and origin

French dégorgement comes from dégorger, to discharge or clear, whose root image is the throat or narrow passage where something is blocked and then expelled. English borrowed disgorgement and kept the sense of ejecting sediment. The trade also uses two phrases: à la volée ("with a flick"), meaning hand disgorgement without freezing, relying on pressure, and à la glace ("with ice"), meaning a frozen neck. Chinese terms for the step are descriptive renderings of "remove the sediment".

## Mechanism and parameters

Traditional-method second fermentation creates carbon dioxide pressure of about 5–6 atmospheres. Before disgorgement the bottle is neck-down and the sediment has collected in the neck. The frozen method plunges the neck into a brine or glycol bath at about −25°C, forming an ice plug about 4 centimetres long that traps the sediment; the bottle is then opened and the pressure drives out the plug and sediment with very little wine loss. In the hand method, the sediment is ejected by pressure at the moment of opening, with the thumb or a little wine controlling the loss. Too cold or too long freezes too much wine; too warm leaves sediment behind. The frozen method requires the neck to be cooled thoroughly, otherwise the plug is weak and sediment can disperse back into the wine when the bottle is opened. Modern lines link disgorgement, dosage and corking, running from several thousand to 18,000 bottles per hour.

## Practical use in the industry

Traditional-method sparkling wines, including Champagne, some Cava, Franciacorta and traditional-method sparklers, generally use disgorgement. A skilled hand disgorger processes about 400 bottles a day; mechanised equipment handles 2,000–18,000 bottles per hour. After disgorgement, 0–5–6 centilitres of liqueur d'expédition are added before closure. The amount determines the sweetness category: Brut Nature below 3 g/L, Brut below 12 g/L and Demi-Sec about 32–50 g/L. Some houses print the disgorgement date on the label so drinkers can judge freshness; the later the disgorgement, the longer the wine spent on the lees, and the more pronounced the bready notes may be. The angle and duration of riddling decide whether the sediment sits tightly at the neck; the disgorger must check clarity quickly after opening and move to dosage, and the rhythm of disgorgement and dosage must match or pressure and fill level will drift.

## Confusable terms compared

First, disgorgement versus [riddling](ref:riddling): riddling gathers the sediment at the neck, disgorgement ejects it, and the order cannot be reversed. Second, disgorgement versus filtration: disgorgement removes sediment bottle by bottle, filtration passes wine through a medium, and only the transfer method uses filtration to remove yeast. Third, disgorgement versus [dosage](ref:dosage): disgorgement causes a loss of volume and pressure, dosage restores volume and sets sweetness, and the two are often done on the same line. Fourth, disgorgement date versus bottling date: the first is when sediment leaves, the second may be earlier (before the second fermentation) or later (after dosage and closure), and they must not be confused.

## Common misuses and disputes

First, assuming disgorgement removes all yeast; it removes only the sediment at the neck, and trace yeast and enzymes may remain. Second, assuming disgorgement makes the bubbles disappear; pressure is still maintained by residual carbon dioxide and the closure. Third, assuming yeast sediment is harmful; it is a normal fermentation by-product and a small amount is not a safety issue. Fourth, assuming hand disgorgement is always better than the frozen method; hand work depends more on experience, freezing is more consistent, and the two are different routes. The dispute is whether the disgorgement date should be mandatory on labels: it helps judge freshness but adds complexity to production and inventory. Fifth, assuming later disgorgement is always better; lees contact changes flavour, but not every style suits long ageing on the sediment.

## Typical figures and examples

Freezing bath about −25°C; ice plug about 4 centimetres; bottle pressure about 5–6 atmospheres; manual disgorgement about 400 bottles per day; mechanised disgorgement 2,000–18,000 bottles per hour; dosage 0–5–6 centilitres; Brut Nature below 3 g/L, Brut below 12 g/L, Demi-Sec 32–50 g/L. In a 0.75-litre bottle, 5 centilitres is about 50 millilitres, or about 6.7% of bottle volume, so dosage is both seasoning and volume compensation. These figures come from public Champagne trade-body material and are useful references when reading process notes.

## Dosage and closure after disgorgement

Disgorgement leaves a gap in the bottle that must be filled. The liqueur is made from base wine and sugar, prepared several months ahead with reserve wines at least two years old and filtered to prevent a second fermentation. The bottle is closed immediately with a cork, crown cap or similar closure, then fitted with a wire hood and label. Some wines rest for weeks or months after closure so the dosage and wine can integrate; others are released quickly to keep fresh fruit. For non-vintage Champagne, lees ageing before disgorgement is at least 12 months; vintage Champagne is usually at least 36 months. Disgorgement is the dividing point of that timeline, not its end.
