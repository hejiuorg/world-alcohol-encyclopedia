# Drinking Wine, No. 7: Autumn Chrysanthemums, the Care-Forgotten Thing and the Eastern Gallery

“Drinking Wine” is a series of twenty poems by Tao Yuanming. The seventh begins with a dew-wet autumn chrysanthemum, floats the flower in wine, calls the drink “the thing that forgets care” and says it puts worldly feelings at a distance; then comes a single cup, an emptied pot, sunset, returning birds, and a long whistle under the eastern gallery. There is no banquet, no guest and no urging anyone to drink—only one person, one pot of wine and an evening slowly going dark.

## The text

The poem has ten lines. Only the key passages are given here, with a plain rendering:

> Autumn chrysanthemums have a fine colour;
> wet with dew, I pick their petals.
> I float this thing that forgets care,
> and put the world's feelings far from me.

“Wet with dew” is literally “soaked in dew”; “pick” is to pluck; “petals” is the flower's bloom. The opening fixes time, action and colour: the autumn chrysanthemums are beautiful, and the poet picks the petals before the dew dries. “Float” can mean floating the petals on the wine or steeping the chrysanthemum in it; “the thing that forgets care” is an old name for wine. Later the poem says “one cup, though drunk alone, / the cup empties and the pot pours itself out”, then sunset and returning birds, and closes with “whistling proudly under the eastern gallery, / I have, for now, got this life”. Variants: “drunk alone” is sometimes “offered alone”, “returning birds” sometimes “flying birds”, “eastern gallery” sometimes “eastern window”. The received text is followed here.

## The poet and background

Tao Yuanming (c. 365–427) was an Eastern Jin poet who held several official posts before returning to farm life. His drinking poems rarely describe the bustle of a banquet; they are more often about solitude, work, reading and the seasons. The series “Drinking Wine” has a preface saying that in idle days with little joy, as autumn nights grew long, he happened to have good wine and did not spend an evening without drinking; when drunk, he wrote a few lines for his own amusement. This matters: drinking is the setting of the series, not a prop that every poem must name.

No. 7 is like that. Wine appears, but there is no drunken behaviour; chrysanthemums appear, but there is no Double Ninth climbing or dogwood. It describes an ordinary autumn evening: picking flowers, floating them in wine, drinking alone, watching birds, whistling. Only when the poem is returned to the life of a farmer can the closing phrase “got this life” be understood: not fame or success, but a day that fully belongs to oneself.

## Layers of the wine image

The first layer is the meeting of chrysanthemum and wine. Chrysanthemums bloom in autumn; wine is a grain ferment, old or new. Floating petals on the surface is an action of taste and sight at once. It need not be explained as a medicinal wine or a health tonic; it is closer to a literati improvisation—whatever is at hand goes into the cup.

The second layer is “forgetting care”. The poet calls wine “the thing that forgets care”, but this forgetting is not numbness; it is “putting the world's feelings far from me”, letting entanglement with the world recede a little. Wine here adjusts distance rather than destroying awareness. He does not say “drink until unconscious”; he says “far”.

The third layer is “alone”. “One cup, though drunk alone” does not mean loneliness in a pitiful sense; it means self-sufficiency. No one accompanies him, and no one need. The cup empties, and the pot pours out its last drop by itself. That “itself” is deft: it looks like the vessel's own movement, and like the poet no longer straining, letting things take their course.

The fourth layer is sunset and “getting this life”. After sundown all things quieten and birds return to the wood; the poet whistles under the eastern gallery. The whistle is not a performance but an action that lets out what is held in the chest. The last line settles the whole poem on “this moment”: not a prayer for long life, but a day not wasted.

## Vessels and wine

Only two objects appear: the cup and the pot. The cup is for drinking, the pot for holding wine; the cup empties and the pot tips, showing that wine is poured from pot to cup. This detail gives solitary drinking a process: not one cup to the end, but cup after cup until the pot is also empty. The plainness of the vessels answers the plainness of the life.

As for the drink, Eastern Jin wine was mainly grain-fermented: rice or millet wine, low in alcohol and usually needing filtration. It was not today's distilled baijiu. For the craft lineage of such grain wine, see [Yellow Wine](ref:yellow-wine); note that Eastern Jin conditions and modern yellow wine are not identical. Chrysanthemum wine has recipes in later texts, but this poem says only “float this”; it gives no formula, and we should not conclude that he was drinking a fixed medicinal chrysanthemum wine.

## Reception

Tao Yuanming's poetry has been praised again and again, commonly with words such as “plain”, “natural” and “true”. Su Shi in the Song wrote many “matching Tao” poems, including matches to the Drinking Wine series, which shows that the group had become a model for literati by the Song. The Jin critic Yuan Haowen, discussing poetry, wrote of “one phrase, natural, ever new; when luxury is gone, true purity appears”, a line often applied to Tao's texture.

For No. 7 specifically, commentators often notice “float this thing that forgets care”: it presses flower, wine and mood into one action, without elaboration or explanation. Others admire the everyday quality of “the cup empties and the pot pours itself out”—not heroic drinking, but a slow emptying. These are not final verdicts, but they suggest a way to read: do not turn Tao into a man above the world; what he writes is everyday objects and everyday actions.

## Common misreadings

First, taking “the thing that forgets care” to mean chrysanthemum. It means wine; the chrysanthemum is what floats in it.

Second, reading “drunk alone” as heavy drinking. “Finished” means finishing the cup, not falling into a stupor.

Third, explaining “the pot pours itself out” as collapsing from drunkenness. It is the pot tipping out its last wine, a quiet and even composed movement.

Fourth, taking “eastern gallery” as a fixed architectural name. It is simply an eastern window or gallery; “eastern window” is a variant.

Fifth, treating the poem as a Double Ninth poem. There is no Double Ninth, no climbing, no dogwood, only an autumn evening. The later link between chrysanthemums and Double Ninth is a cultural association and should not be pushed back into the poem.

## How to read

First read by action: picking chrysanthemums, floating them in wine, raising the cup, the cup emptying, the pot tipping, sunset, birds returning, whistling. The clearer the actions, the quieter the poem. Then notice time: there is still dew at the start, and the sun has set at the end; nothing dramatic happens in between, only the light changes.

Read it beside No. 5. No. 5 opens the gaze outward with “picking chrysanthemums by the eastern fence, carefree I see the southern mountain”; No. 7 keeps voice and body close, “whistling proudly under the eastern gallery”. Neither poem describes drunkenness, yet both describe the clarity that follows wine.
