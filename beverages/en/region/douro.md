# Douro

Douro is a wine appellation in the Douro valley of north-east [Portugal](ref:portugal), demarcated in 1756 as one of the world's earliest wine regions. It makes both [Porto](ref:porto) base wine and structured dry [red](ref:red-wine) and [white](ref:white-wine) wines.![Douro image](/media/photos/region-douro-fd8dc772.jpg)

Figure: Douro. Image: Vitor Oliveira from Torres Vedras, PORTUGAL, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons ([source page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rio_Douro_-_Portugal_(32615481975)_(cropped).jpg)).

## Definition and scope

Douro is an appellation and also the source of Porto grapes. Grapes from the region may be used for Porto or for ordinary dry wine, and the two products differ in rules, process and market position.

Read Douro on three levels: the valley is the geographic area, Douro is the appellation, and Porto is one fortified-wine product within it. Equating Douro with Porto overlooks the region's dry wines.

## Geography and climate

The Douro valley lies in north-east Portugal, with the river flowing east to west from Spain and vineyards on steep slopes on both banks, from near river level up to more than 700 metres. The climate is extreme continental, with hot dry summers and cold winters.

Schist soils lie in tilted layers, draining well but holding little fertility, so roots must go deep for water. Aspect and altitude decide sunshine and ripeness, and heat reflected from the river affects riverside parcels. Drought and heat are the main pressures, making irrigation and canopy management important.

## History

Douro's demarcation dates to 1756, when boundaries and supervision were set up to protect the Porto region. Porto trade then drove the development of terraces, cellars and transport, and modern appellation rules were built up during the 20th century.

The terraced landscape and variety structure built up over that history let Douro keep its Porto tradition while also developing a separate dry-wine route from the late 20th century. The two routes share vineyards but have different winemaking goals.

## Sub-zones

- Baixo Corgo: the western part, lower and wetter, ripening earlier with relatively higher yields.
- Cima Corgo: the central part around Pinhão, with steeper slopes and a drier climate, the core of Porto and quality dry reds.
- Douro Superior: the easternmost part near the Spanish border, the driest and hottest, with high ripeness.

The three sub-zones reflect climate and terrain rather than a quality ranking. The same variety can show different acidity, fruit and body in the west and the east.

## Varieties and styles

Red grapes are mainly Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz, Tinta Barroca and Tinto Cão; whites include Malvasia Fina, Viosinho, Gouveio and Rabigato. The raw material is [grape](ref:grape).

[Red wine](ref:red-wine) is usually deep in colour with medium to firm tannin, showing blackberry, violet and spice; [white wine](ref:white-wine) has high acidity with citrus, white flowers and mineral notes. Dry Douro reds have grown in international market share in recent years.

## Soils and terrain

Soils are mainly schist with some granite and alluvial soil. Schist fractures easily, letting roots follow cracks for water, and differences in schist direction and slope create differences in drainage and retention.

The terrain is defined by steep terraces, with traditional stone-walled terraces alongside modern mechanised ones. Aspect, altitude and terrace structure decide light and airflow, and also affect harvest methods and yields.

## Winemaking and ageing

Reds are crushed, macerated and [fermented](ref:fermentation); some estates use granite lagares and foot treading, others temperature-controlled stainless steel. Maceration time and temperature decide colour, tannin and aroma.

Ageing may use [oak](ref:oak-ageing), large casks or inert containers. The proportion of new wood, barrel age and length of ageing affect vanilla, spice and tannin feel; some estates deliberately use less new oak to preserve fruit.

## Labels and vintage

Labels usually carry Douro, vintage, alcohol, volume and bottler; some name a sub-zone, village or parcel. Porto labels instead carry Porto and a category, and the two should not be confused.

Vintage reflects the weather of that year. Douro vintage differences mainly show in ripeness, acidity and yield, so vintage should be read together with estate and parcel.

## Market and drinking

Dry Douro reds commonly have about 13%–14.5% [alcohol by volume](ref:abv) and suit grilled meat, braised meat, mushrooms and hard cheese at about 16–18 degrees Celsius; whites are served at about 8–10 degrees Celsius.

Douro also makes [fortified wine](ref:fortified-wine), namely Porto, covered on the Porto page. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

## Common misconceptions

First, equating Douro with Porto. The region also makes dry red and white wines.
Second, thinking Douro is only steep stone terraces. Modern parcels also include mechanised terraces and valley-floor vineyards.
Third, assuming schist soils mean a single style. Aspect, altitude and vintage create clear differences.
Fourth, confusing Douro with Dão. They are different Portuguese regions.
Fifth, thinking all Douro reds need long ageing. Vintage and estate styles vary widely.

**At a glance:** A wine appellation in the Douro valley of north-east Portugal, demarcated in 1756 as one of the world's earliest wine regions; vineyards run along steep stone-walled terraces on schist soils, Touriga Nacional and other varieties dominate, and the region makes both Porto base wine and structured dry reds and whites. 

Douro is best approached through the appellation and its permitted varieties, then the vintage and the producer, and then how the wine is labelled. One check alone will not settle it.

[Portugal](ref:portugal)'s geographic boundary and [Red wine](ref:red-wine)'s climate and soil form a useful pair: the contrast shows where one ends and the other begins.

A common mistake is to treat Douro as an administrative place name. Start with where its boundary lies, then check which climate and legal category it has. If they conflict, trust the more specific source.
