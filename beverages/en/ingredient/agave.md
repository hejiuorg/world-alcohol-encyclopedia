# Agave

Agave is a genus of perennial succulents native to the Americas, with more than two hundred species. Distillers do not use the leaves but the heart, or piña, formed by the swollen stem and leaf bases. That tissue stores fructans rather than starch, so sugar release depends on cooking or roasting rather than malt enzymes. Much of the difference between tequila and mezcal begins here.![Agave image](/media/photos/ingredient-agave-6671c637.jpg)

Figure: Agave. Image: Marc Ryckaert ( MJJR ), CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons ([source page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Agave_americana_R01.jpg)).

## The genus and its rosette structure

Agave grows as a rosette of thick, toothed leaves and uses crassulacean acid metabolism, fixing carbon dioxide at night and closing its stomata by day to limit water loss. Growth is slow: blue Weber agave usually needs 6–8 years to reach harvest sugar levels, and larger plants can take more than 10 years. At harvest the leaves are cut away and the heart is dug out, weighing from tens to over a hundred kilograms.

Agave is propagated mainly from basal shoots, or hijuelos, and a transplanted pup matures in about 6–8 years. Planting density, rainfall and soil affect fructan accumulation: dry years can give higher sugar but lower weight. The jimador cuts the leaves away one by one and judges ripeness from leaf colour, plant shape and the thickness of the heart, an assessment where experience beats instruments.

## Fructans and thermal hydrolysis

Fructans may make up 50–70% of the dry matter of the agave heart, largely in inulin-type forms. Brewing yeast cannot use fructans directly; heat or acid hydrolysis must first release fructose and glucose. Cooking softens fibre, sanitises the material and concentrates sweetness, while roasting adds Maillard and caramelisation products that read as smoke, caramel and spice.

Traditional mezcal uses an earthen pit: stones are heated, covered with fibre and earth, and the hearts roast for several days. Tequila more often uses autoclaves, which are faster and give little smoke. The heating method sets the balance of cooked fruit and smoke in the final spirit.

## Tequila's legal raw material and process

Mexican standard NOM-006 restricts tequila to blue Weber agave (Agave tequilana Weber var. azul) and to five states: Jalisco, Nayarit, Guanajuato, Michoacán and Tamaulipas, with Jalisco as the core. Standard tequila must draw at least 51% of its sugar from agave, with the rest allowed from other sources; a "100% agave" label requires all sugar to come from agave.

Tequila is normally distilled twice and bottled at 35–55% ABV. Age categories are blanco, reposado, añejo and extra añejo.

The Tequila Regulatory Council (CRT) verifies origin and applies tamper-evident labels; every compliant bottle carries an NOM number and batch information, and 100% agave products also state "100% de agave". Mezcal is overseen by bodies such as the CRM, but with many small palenques its standardisation is lower than tequila's. A bottle labelled simply "agave spirit" can therefore represent very different raw-material shares and processes.

## Mezcal and its regions

NOM-070 allows many Agave species and covers states including Oaxaca, Durango and Guanajuato. Oaxaca is the largest and best-known producer, traditionally working with espadín and other varieties. Mezcal emphasises pit roasting and small stills, and smoke is a recognisable trait, though not every mezcal is heavily smoky.

## Fermentation and distillation

Roasted hearts are crushed and pressed into sugary juice and fibre, which is diluted with water before fermentation. Traditional palenques rely on wild yeast and ambient microbes for 3–10 days; modern plants use commercial yeast and temperature control for speed and consistency. The wash is distilled twice in pot stills, with heads and tails cut away.

## Substitutes and differences

Agave syrup can supply fructose quickly in place of agave hearts, but it lacks the plant fibre, minerals and roast-derived flavour, so it cannot build the layers of a traditional tequila. Maize or cane molasses is cheaper still but changes the legal category and the aroma. Fructan structure and flavour precursors differ between species, and espadín cannot simply stand in for blue Weber.

## Sensory impact

The core aromas are cooked agave, green pepper, citrus, black pepper and minerality. Cooking brings sweet cooked fruit and caramel; pit roasting adds smoke, ash and spice. Warm fermentation produces banana and pineapple esters, while cool fermentation keeps more vegetal character. Oak ageing adds vanilla, coconut and sweet spice but softens the green agave note. Still design matters as much as wood: copper pot stills strip sulphur and lift clean cooked-agave sweetness, while a column still can retain more vegetal weight but less texture. Roast intensity is a stylistic choice rather than a legal requirement, even within the same denomination.

## Common misconceptions

First, all agave spirit is tequila. Tequila is a separate protected category using only blue Weber agave. Second, the agave heart is a fruit. It is a swollen stem and leaf-base structure. Third, all mezcal is heavily smoky. Roasting methods and species vary widely. Fourth, agave syrup is agave spirits. The former is a sweetener, the latter a distilled drink.
