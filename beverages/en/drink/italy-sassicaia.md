# Sassicaia

Sassicaia is a red wine from Bolgheri in Tuscany, Italy, produced by Tenuta San Guido and based on [Cabernet Sauvignon](ref:cabernet) with Cabernet Franc. It is often described as a leading "Super Tuscan", but that label is a market and historical category, not an official Italian grade. Since 2013, Sassicaia has had its own DOC appellation, Bolgheri Sassicaia.![Sassicaia category image](/media/photos/drink-style-wine-bbcaddfe.jpg)

## Definition and identity

Sassicaia is first a specific wine and only second a style label. Its legal identity is the Bolgheri Sassicaia DOC: the appellation defines the area, varieties, yields and ageing rules under Italian agriculture ministry and consortium oversight. It is not Chianti Classico and not based on Sangiovese; that is the key difference from traditional Tuscan reds.

"Super Tuscan" originally described late-twentieth-century Tuscan reds that departed from traditional appellation recipes but won market recognition. It is not a grade and has no single rulebook; different wines may use different varieties, areas and ageing. Treating "Super Tuscan" as a quality guarantee misses the identity and vintage of the specific wine.

## History

Tenuta San Guido lies on the Bolgheri coast. The property covers about 2,500 hectares, but only a small part is planted to vines. In the 1940s, Mario Incisa della Rocchetta planted Cabernet on the estate, and the first wines were mainly for family and friends. 1968 was the first commercial vintage, and the wine reached the market in 1971.

With Piero Antinori and winemaker Giacomo Tachis, Sassicaia later reached a much wider audience. Its success helped turn Bolgheri from a relatively marginal coastal area into an important source of Italian premium reds. In 2013 the Italian government approved the separate Bolgheri Sassicaia DOC, moving the wine from a market category into a legal appellation.

## Region and terroir

Bolgheri lies on the Tuscan coast, moderated by the Mediterranean, with sea breezes and day-night temperature swings shaping ripening. Sassicaia's grapes come from parcels including Castiglioncello, Aia Nuova, Cerreta, Sassicaia di Sopra and San Martino, at altitudes of about 100–360 metres. The soils are calcareous with marl, gravel and some clay; the name Sassicaia is linked to the stony character of the site.

Stony soils drain well and reduce water stress after rain, helping Cabernet develop colour, tannin and blackcurrant flavour; marl and clay buffer water and nutrients. The coastal position brings sea breezes and a longer ripening season, so the fruit can reach sugar ripeness while retaining acidity. Differences in altitude, exposure and soil between parcels create the layers in the final blend.

## Process and ageing

Sassicaia is mainly Cabernet Sauvignon with a small share of Cabernet Franc, the proportions changing by vintage. Grapes are hand-harvested and sorted, fermented in stainless steel or temperature-controlled vessels, then aged in [oak](ref:oak-ageing). The estate decides new-barrel proportion and ageing time according to the structure and concentration of the year, aiming to integrate wood, tannin and fruit rather than let oak dominate.

After ageing the wine is usually clarified and lightly filtered before bottling. Bottle ageing matters too: firm tannin softens, and blackcurrant moves towards dried fruit, cedar and spice. Vintage differences come from climate as well as harvest date, maceration and barrel choice, so one year cannot stand for the whole wine.

## Flavour and tasting

Sassicaia typically shows blackcurrant, black cherry, cedar, pencil lead, Mediterranean scrub and mineral notes, with a medium-to-full body, fine but firm tannin and an acidic backbone. Young wines can show noticeable oak and spice; with age they move towards leather, tobacco, mushroom and dried flowers. Compared with Sangiovese-based Tuscan reds, the fruit is darker and the structure closer to the Bordeaux left bank.

Serve at about 16–18°C. Young vintages can be decanted for about an hour to open the tannin and aroma; older vintages should be decanted briefly and tasted first. Use a large red-wine glass and fill no more than a third of the bowl. If there is sediment, stand the bottle upright and decant carefully.

## Drinking and pairing

Sassicaia suits Tuscan steak, roasted meat, braised meat, game and hard cheese; fat and protein buffer the tannin. Tomato-driven acidic dishes, spicy sauces or delicate seafood can clash with the weight of the wine. For a high-value bottle, check the vintage, provenance and storage before opening.

Store away from heat, strong light and vibration; long-term storage needs constant temperature and humidity. After opening, use a vacuum stopper or inert gas and refrigerate, and finish within a few days. Aged wines have a short aromatic window, so reduce contact with air between servings.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating "Super Tuscan" as an official grade. It is a market term, not a DOCG or DOC category.
Second, assuming Sassicaia is a Sangiovese wine. It is Cabernet-dominant and structurally different from traditional Chianti.
Third, treating every Bolgheri red as Sassicaia. Bolgheri has many estates and several appellation wines.
Fourth, treating 1968 as the first wine ever made. It was the first commercial vintage; family wine came earlier.
Fifth, ignoring DOC rules. Bolgheri Sassicaia has its own variety, yield and ageing requirements and is not the same as generic Bolgheri.
