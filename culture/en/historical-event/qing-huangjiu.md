# Qing Huangjiu: Shaoxing Wine Reaches the Whole Country

The Qing period (1644–1911) was the high point of Shaoxing huangjiu. The Kuaiji county gazetteer of 1681 already said 'Yue wine travels the world; its kinds are many, and the old wine is especially current'. By the Daoguang reign, Liang Zhangju put it more directly in the Langji Xutan: 'Shaoxing wine is now current throughout the seas, the true orthodox wine... shipped across the whole realm and even as far as the far borders of Xinjiang.' Huangjiu became a national commodity, and that depended not only on quality but on water transport, breweries and a cross-regional network of wine houses.

## Date and place

Qing huangjiu was not produced only in Shaoxing, but the narrative long centred on Shaoxing. Shanyin and Kuaiji counties of Shaoxing prefecture stood in the Jian Lake water system, and Liang Zhangju specifically noted that 'between Shanyin and Kuaiji the water is best suited to wine, and elsewhere it cannot be good', tying wine quality to the local water system. This 'water to wine' relation later became the core of Shaoxing huangjiu's origin story.

In time, the phrase 'Yue wine travels the world' appears already in the Kangxi reign, and the Shen Yonghe brewery, founded in 1664, is one of the old names still in use; the Langji Xutan of the Daoguang reign records nationwide shipment. In the late Qing, Shaoxing breweries opened wine shops, taverns or wine houses in Hangzhou, Shanghai, Beijing, Tianjin and Hankou, extending the sales network from producing area to consumer cities.

## Background: huangjiu and shaojiu at the Ming-Qing turn

The Ming pattern was huangjiu first and shaojiu second. Shaojiu spread in northern everyday drinking, but banquets and gifts still valued huangjiu. On entering the Qing this pattern did not reverse at once: huangjiu, with its mature distribution network and gentry consumption, kept its place as the famous wine, while shaojiu expanded in the north and in lower-tier markets. The two were not substitutes but a division of labour.

Huangjiu itself was differentiated. Beyond Shaoxing wine, Jinhua wine, Fujian red-qu wine and Shandong Jimo aged wine each had markets; Shaoxing's advantage lay in a concentrated producing area, stable technique and an organised transport system, which gave it the most visible place in the 'famous wine' narrative.

## Course: how 'Yue wine travels the world' took shape

'Yue wine travels the world' was not an empty phrase; several conditions supported it.

First, the producing area was concentrated. Shanyin and Kuaiji counties had the Jian Lake water system and a mature cluster of breweries, so quality was stable and a unified regional reputation could form. Second, technique and ageing mattered. Shaoxing wine was aged, and Yuan Mei's Suiyuan Shidan even said that 'Shaoxing wine under five years old cannot be drunk'; breweries advertised 'old wine' and 'distant-year Chen Shao', making age a priced attribute. Third, literature and gentry endorsement helped. Judgements by Liang Zhangju and Yuan Mei wrote Shaoxing wine into national food knowledge and reinforced its status.

Tong Yuejian's Diaoding Ji explained its popularity in terms of quality: 'Among the wines of the world many are treated with ash, and drinking them makes one thirsty, but Shao wine alone is not; most wines of the world are sweet, and drinking them makes the body feel heavy, but Shao wine is fragrant, mellow and strong, it moves and does not stay.' This passage separates Shaoxing wine from the sweet and ash-treated wines common at the time, showing that its competitiveness was not only regional fame but a clear difference in taste.

## Production and sale: breweries, wine houses and the Beijing and Guangdong parcels

Qing Shaoxing wine was mainly produced in breweries. Yunji, Wang Baohe, Mao Wansheng and Shanyuantai appeared in the Ming and Qing, and Shen Yonghe was founded in 1664; these names later became basic coordinates in the history of huangjiu brands.

On the sales side, cross-regional wine houses and grading appeared. Wine supplied to Beijing was called 'Beijing parcel huangjiu', that supplied to Guangdong 'Guangdong parcel huangjiu'; there were also channels supplying medicinal wine to the Huqingyutang pharmacy in Hangzhou and the Tongrentang pharmacy in Beijing. The Beijing and Guangdong parcels were not simple transport labels but product tiers shaped by destination taste, packaging and use. Breweries opened shops, taverns or wine houses in Hangzhou, Shanghai, Beijing, Tianjin and Hankou, running retail and wholesale and bringing the producing-area brand directly to consumer cities.

Transport depended on water. The Grand Canal and coastal routes carried Shaoxing wine jars north and to southern ports; the jar was both container and unit of ageing and transport. Liang Zhangju's 'shipped across the whole realm and even as far as the far borders of Xinjiang' more likely reflects merchants carrying, gifting or reselling wine than a stable market size.

## Influence: famous-wine status and standardised technique

First, it established the way 'famous wine' was described. Producing area, water system, ageing, taste difference and gentry judgement combined into a repeatable discourse of famous wine; later huangjiu brand narratives largely followed this structure.

Second, it promoted the stratification of technique and product. The appearance of Beijing parcels, Guangdong parcels and medicinal wine shows that wine from one area was differentiated by use and market; the price gap between aged and ordinary wine was also institutionalised in this period.

Third, it bound Shaoxing to huangjiu. To this day 'Shaoxing wine' remains the best-known origin label for huangjiu; the binding came both from quality and from Qing distribution and literature.

Fourth, it left a network of old names and breweries. Shen Yonghe and others span the Qing, the Republic and the present, a rare continuous thread in the industrial history of huangjiu.

## Sources and disputes

First, 'travels the world' is literary and commercial rhetoric, not sales statistics. The Qing had no national wine output or sales statistics, and Liang Zhangju's 'across the whole realm' and 'as far as Xinjiang' are observation and embellishment, not evidence of market coverage.

Second, rankings of famous wine came from individual judgements. Yuan Mei, Liang Zhangju and Tong Yuejian each had a standpoint, and none was a judging body; 'Shaoxing wine is the orthodox' was a gentry consensus, not a universal consumer choice.

Third, Qing huangjiu is not the same as Shaoxing wine. Jinhua wine, Fujian red-qu wine and Jimo aged wine still held local markets; writing the history of huangjiu as the history of Shaoxing wine would ignore other regions' techniques and consumption.

Fourth, the text of the 1681 Kuaiji gazetteer still needs checking. This essay relies on a research-institute quotation and has not checked the original Kangxi woodblock; the exact chapter and context of 'Yue wine travels the world' require edition comparison.

## Timeline

- 1644: the Qing enters the passes, and the Ming pattern of wine continues into the Qing.
- 1664: the Shen Yonghe brewery is founded, one of the huangjiu old names still in use.
- 1681: the Kuaiji county gazetteer records 'Yue wine travels the world; its kinds are many, and the old wine is especially current'.
- Daoguang reign: Liang Zhangju's Langji Xutan records Shaoxing wine as 'current throughout the seas' and 'as far as the far borders of Xinjiang'.
- 1911: the Qing ends, and the private brewery system enters the Republic.

To read Qing Huangjiu, start with the date and setting, then the documented consequences, then how later accounts treat it; no single check settles it.
