# The rise of Chinese craft beer around 2015

Around 2015 craft beer in China moved from a very small circle into more cities. Small breweries, taprooms, craft bars and beer festivals appeared, and domestic brands coexisted with imported craft beer. It was not a centrally planned movement but a dispersed growth driven by practitioners, consumers and urban commercial space. "Rise" is more accurate than "invention", because home-brewing communities, importers and small breweries had been accumulating experience for years.

## Background

The Chinese beer market had long been dominated by standardised pale lager from large plants, with narrow flavour, low prices and channels controlled by a few national and regional brands. In 2013 national beer output reached 50.615 million kilolitres, a peak for the period; in 2014 output fell 0.96%, the first decline in ten years. Once total volume peaked, the industry began looking for new growth. At the same time urban middle-class and younger consumers demanded more variety, and imported craft beer, home-brew bars and foreign beer books provided the first examples. The [American](ref:usa) craft movement travelled through competitions, media and social networks, and the Brewers Association definition of small, independent and traditional was widely cited.

Craft beer had a relatively low entry threshold: small equipment and small premises were enough to start, and a taproom could sell directly to consumers. That allowed it to bypass the traditional distributor system, but it also brought complexity in licensing, taxation, fire safety and food-safety management.

## Course and timeline

- 2008: Gao Yan founded the local craft brewery Aofei Beer in Nanjing, one of the early Chinese craft experiments.
- 2010: Great Leap Brewing opened its first outlet in Doujiao Hutong, Beijing, emphasising local ingredients and Chinese flavours.
- 2011: Gao Yan published an introductory beer book, and home-brewing and craft knowledge began to spread systematically.
- 2012: Nanjing Master Gao Beer Co. was established, and Beijing brands such as Jing-A entered the market.
- 2013: Panda Brew was founded in Beijing, and the number of domestic craft brands increased.
- 2015: media and capital attention rose noticeably; in October the revised Food Safety Law took effect, and the food production licence (SC) system set clearer requirements for small producers; imports reached 538,300 kilolitres, up about 59%.
- 2016–2019: craft bars, beer festivals and domestic brands continued to grow, and some large breweries launched craft-style product lines.
- After 2020: the pandemic, rising costs and intensifying competition brought an adjustment period in which openings were accompanied by closures and acquisitions.

## Key people and institutions

Gao Yan is one of the most frequently cited early promoters; his brewery, books and training influenced many practitioners. Great Leap Brewing, Panda Brew, Jing-A and NBeer represent practice in Beijing, Nanjing and other cities. On the institutional side, the China Alcoholic Drinks Association and local beer associations provide statistics, standards and exchange platforms; the National Bureau of Statistics and the General Administration of Customs provide output and import data; local market regulators handle production licences and food safety. The Brewers Association definition and competitions provided an international reference.

## Concrete changes for the industry

First, the range of flavours expanded. IPA, wheat beer, sour beer and stout entered the view of ordinary consumers, making "beer has more than one taste" an experience. Second, drinking spaces changed. Craft bars emphasise tasting, conversation and local community, unlike traditional street stalls and nightlife venues, and taprooms put production and consumption together. Third, local ingredients and flavour experiments advanced, with tea, Sichuan pepper, fruit and honey used in beer, creating an exploration of "Chinese-style craft". Fourth, new regulatory questions arose: small production licences, cross-regional sales, labelling and alcohol-content marking all needed adaptation. Fifth, large breweries upgraded their product mix, with some national brands launching wheat beer, IPA and craft-style lines. Sixth, the movement laid groundwork for later domestic brands, exports and beer tourism. ## Disputes and later assessments

First, scale. Craft beer has no separate official statistics, and common market-size figures come from industry bodies or research firms with very different definitions; the "craft share" often quoted for 2015 is hard to verify. Second, the definition of craft. The Brewers Association sets "small" at annual output of no more than 6 million barrels and "independent" at less than 25% ownership by a non-craft beverage alcohol company, but Chinese practitioners disagree about its applicability. Third, the timing. Treating 2015 as the starting point ignores earlier home-brewing communities and small breweries; it is more an accelerator than a zero point. Fourth, success. Media report successful brands more often than closures and transfers, producing survivorship bias. Fifth, later assessments diverge: some argue craft beer drove premiumisation and flavour education, while others say it was packaged by capital and the "handmade" label, with prices detached from value.

## Common misreadings

First, craft beer is not synonymous with imported or expensive. Domestic small breweries are craft too, and price depends on ingredients, scale and channel. Second, 2015 was not a start from zero. Breweries, home-brewing communities and importers existed after 2008; 2015 was a point of concentrated media and capital attention. Third, craft does not mean every small brewery makes good beer; small scale does not guarantee quality, and equipment, hygiene and consistency matter. Fourth, craft does not have to be independent. Whether an acquired brand still counts as craft depends on the definition used, not on a single moral judgement. Fifth, China has no unified "craft beer law"; requirements are spread across food safety, production licensing, advertising and tax rules.
