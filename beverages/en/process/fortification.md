# Fortification

Fortification is the addition of distilled spirit to wine. The spirit is usually grape-derived alcohol or brandy, and the aim is to raise the alcohol of the finished wine, suppress microbial activity, stabilise the wine and, in the process, retain sugar or shape flavour. Fortified wine is a category of its own precisely because this step rewrites where fermentation ends, rather than merely raising an alcohol number.

## Place in the process

Fortification happens during alcoholic [fermentation](ref:fermentation) or after it, and before ageing. It differs from [distillation](ref:distillation): distillation concentrates alcohol from a fermented liquid, while fortification adds that distilled spirit back to wine in a set proportion. It is also not compounding, which adds spices, sugar or other ingredients. The three can follow one another but sit at different conceptual levels. Fortified wine is still wine; fermentation has been stopped or reinforced by hand. Most regions require the added spirit to be grape-derived and set a minimum alcohol and a permitted sweetness range, so fortified wine is governed both by drinks law and by appellation rules.

## Steps and parameters

In Port, for example, grape spirit at about 77% alcohol is added when fermentation has reached roughly 5–9% alcohol, bringing the finished wine to 19–22% alcohol and leaving 90–140 g/L residual sugar. In dry Sherry, the base wine ferments to completion and is then raised from about 11–12.5% to 15–15.5% for Fino or 17–18% for Oloroso. EU rules frame fortified wine alcohol between 15% and 22%. These ranges follow the common descriptions in the OIV material, regional association information and wine reference work listed on this page, and individual products remain bound by appellation rules.

## Mechanism: alcohol inhibition and extraction

The core of fortification is ethanol toxicity to yeast. Wine yeast usually tolerates 15–16%; when added spirit pushes the concentration up quickly, membrane fluidity changes and sugar transport and glycolysis are blocked, so fermentation stops and residual sugar remains. Ethanol is also a good solvent: it raises the partition coefficient of volatile aroma compounds, making aromas more forceful, and changes viscosity and the sense of sweetness. In dry fortified wines, spirit is added after fermentation, residual sugar is low, and oxidative ageing then drives the nutty, caramel and saline character.

## Equipment and variants

Equipment includes storage tanks, dosing pumps, flow meters, in-line mixers, temperature control and nitrogen blanketing. Traditional cellars measure and blend by hand in wood; modern plants use automatic dosing with batch traceability. Variants include sweet styles fortified mid-fermentation, dry styles fortified after fermentation, oxidative styles such as Sherry and Madeira, and aromatised products such as vermouth that add spices or sugar after fortification. Vessel and temperature affect mixing homogeneity and volatile loss, and the added spirit should be kept from aeration.

## Failure modes and diagnosis

First, under-fortification: surviving yeast keeps working, causing refermentation in bottle with haze, pressure or pushed corks. Second, over-fortification: the alcohol feels hot, fruit is suppressed and balance is lost. Third, poor spirit quality: solvent, sulfur or coarse spirit notes enter the wine. Fourth, tartrate and protein instability: fortification changes solubility and deposit appears after bottling. Fifth, label or regulatory breach: alcohol, origin or category claims fall outside what is permitted. Diagnosis should combine alcohol, residual sugar, sensory checks, stability trials and a regulatory review.

## Effect on flavour

Fortification lifts alcohol, bringing more warmth and a longer finish. Sweet fortified wines, with residual sugar retained, feel fuller and more viscous; dry fortified wines emphasise the nutty, caramel, saline and spicy notes of oxidative ageing. In [fortified wine](ref:fortified-wine), fortification and ageing together define the style: Port leans on sweetness and fruit, Sherry on oxidation and a dry structure, Madeira on heat and oxidation. Compared with ordinary [wine](ref:wine), fortified wine is stronger and more stable, but the clarity of fruit can be masked by alcohol and oxidation.

## Effect on stability

Higher alcohol suppresses yeast and most bacteria, so fortified wine keeps better after opening than ordinary wine and tolerates oxygen better. High alcohol is not absolute stability, however: acetic acid bacteria can still act when oxygen is present, and residual sugar and nutrients can still support microbes. Fortification lowers the solubility of potassium bitartrate and pigment-protein complexes, so cold and protein stabilisation are needed before bottling. In sweet styles, the balance of residual sugar and acidity sets both palate and bottle life. How fast an opened bottle oxidises depends on alcohol, residual sugar, acidity and whether the wine was oxidatively aged, so no single rule applies.

## Common misconceptions

First, calling all fortified wine "spirits" and forgetting it is still wine. Second, assuming earlier fortification always means sweeter, when timing depends on target residual sugar and alcohol. Third, watching only alcohol and ignoring the purity and aroma of the added spirit. Fourth, believing fortified wine cannot spoil, when it still oxidises and acidifies after opening. Fifth, equating fortification with compounding and ignoring that spices and sugar are a separate layer of process. Sixth, treating fortification as a simple alcohol boost and ignoring how it dilutes aroma, acidity and texture.
