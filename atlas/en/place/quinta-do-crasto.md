# Quinta do Crasto

Quinta do Crasto lies on the right bank of the Douro at Gouvinhas, in the municipality of Sabrosa, northern Portugal. The name comes from the Latin castrum, meaning "Roman fort", and the earliest document mentioning wine production at the estate dates from 1615. Today it is run by the Roquette family and produces Douro DOC reds and whites as well as Port and olive oil, with a wine-tourism programme that includes tours, tastings and meals.

## Place and history

The history can be read through three dates. 1615 is the first documentary mention of Quinta do Crasto and its wine; the estate was later granted Feitoria status, one of the highest vineyard classifications of the time in the Douro. In 1758 the Marquis of Pombal ordered 335 granite markers, each two metres high, to define the world's first demarcated wine region; one of these "marcos pombalinos" still stands beside the century-old farmhouse at Crasto. The old iron gate carries two dates, 1615 and 1918: the first is the origin, the second the family's arrival.

In 1918 the Port merchant Constantino de Almeida bought the estate. He ran the Constantino wine house, which produced and exported Port and brandy; after his death in 1923 his son Fernando Moreira d'Almeida took over and continued making high-quality Port. In 1981 Leonor Roquette, Fernando's daughter, and her husband Jorge Roquette acquired majority ownership and management, becoming the fourth generation of the family to run the estate. They began restoring and extending the vineyards and producing the Douro DOC wines for which Crasto is now known internationally, rather than relying on Port alone.

This explains a common misunderstanding: Crasto is not a company founded in 1615 and continuously owned by the same family since then. 1615 is the documentary starting point, 1918 the family's entry, and 1981 the beginning of the modern commercial structure. Mixing the three dates overstates the corporate continuity and understates how much the Roquettes changed the vineyards and the range over the last four decades.

## The estate and the wines

The vineyards sit on the right bank of the Douro, on schist soils, with traditional terraces (socalcos) supported by stone walls; some old parcels still contain mixed plantings. Summers are hot and dry, schist holds limited water, and old vines root deeply and crop low, which concentrates flavour. This is one reason Douro wines often show dark fruit, mineral notes and spice. Reds are based on Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Barroca and Tinta Roriz, among others; whites use local varieties such as Viosinho and Rabigato.

The buildings are grouped around the century-old farmhouse: the old iron gate, the Pombaline marker, the main house, the renovated winemaking cellars, the barrel cellar and the tasting spaces form a short route. A tour usually starts at grape reception, moves through the winery and then into the barrel cellar where the premium wines age; this shows how parcels and varieties are handled separately. The old buildings have not been turned into a stage set: tanks, pipes and barrels are still in use, and visitors should keep out of the staff's way.

The range can be remembered in three lines. First, Douro DOC wines: Quinta do Crasto red and white, Reserva, and single-vineyard or old-vine labels such as Touriga Nacional, Vinha Maria Teresa and Vinha da Ponte. Second, Port: LBV, Vintage and Tawny. Third, olive oil. Crasto's strength is the balance between Douro's ripe fruit and schist-driven minerality rather than sheer power. On a first visit, start with the white and the basic red, move to the Reserva and single-vineyard wines, and finish with an LBV or Tawny; the reverse order flattens the simpler wines.

Wine tourism is a second product line. In November 2026 the official page listed a guided tour and tasting of five wines at €41.96 per person, 90 minutes, 1–10 people, 18 and over; a superior tasting of five wines at €79.71; a guided visit with a paired lunch at €103.04, about three hours, up to 30 people; dinner at €62.13, about two hours; and a wine dinner at €184.01, up to 20 people, 18 and over. Prices include tax and change with season and wines; use the official page when booking.

## How to visit

All tastings and meals must be booked in advance. The booking link is on the estate's Wine Tourism page; you can also contact enoturismo@quintadocrasto.pt or telephone +351 254 106 086 or +351 937 760 572. Reception and the wine shop are open daily from 10:00 to 18:00, but tours run to booked time slots, and walk-ins may not be able to join.

Choose the format according to how long you want to stay. A 90-minute tasting is enough to see the cellar and try five wines; the three-hour lunch is for sitting down to a meal based on local produce; dinner and the wine dinner suit an evening when the heat has gone. Families should note that tasting programmes are marked 18 and over, while lunch and dinner are open to all ages, though minors are not served alcohol.

Arrive ten to fifteen minutes early and confirm the booking and language at reception. The route includes outdoors and indoors; the slopes are exposed in summer and windy in winter, so wear comfortable shoes and bring a hat and water. Spitting is acceptable, especially if you are driving; Douro roads are steep and winding, so do not drive after drinking. During harvest, usually September to October, the estate is busy and some areas may be closed; confirm when booking.

## Etiquette and common mistakes

The first mistake is to equate the Douro with Port. Dry Douro DOC reds and whites have improved enormously over the last few decades, and Crasto built its international reputation on them; ordering only Port misses half the range. The second is to treat 1615 as the company's founding year. It is the documentary starting point; the family arrived in 1918 and the modern structure dates from 1981.

The third is to treat the wine-tourism programme as an ordinary restaurant. Lunch and dinner are booked estate experiences with a set menu and wine pairings, not à la carte service; walk-ins can usually use only the shop and cannot join a tasting. The fourth is to underestimate time and cost: premium tastings and dinners are priced per person, lunch takes three hours, and trying to reach another estate afterwards will be rushed.

Tasting etiquette follows the European winery norm: look at colour and aroma first, then sip; spitting is normal and finishing every glass is not required. Avoid strong perfume and do not touch barrels or bottles. Follow local photography notices and keep other guests out of your pictures. Tipping is not compulsory in Portugal and service is usually included; if you are happy, round up or leave 5%–10%.

## Around and routes

Crasto works well as part of a day in the Pinhão area. Pinhão station is known for its azulejo panels, and river cruises leave from the quay; nearby estates such as Quinta do Bomfim and Quinta da Roêda also receive visitors by appointment. The schist slopes and terraces along the river are themselves part of the Douro landscape.

Sabrosa is the birthplace of the navigator Ferdinand Magellan, and the town has monuments connected with him. West lies Vila Real and the Casa de Mateus; south is Lamego, with the Nossa Senhora dos Remédios church and old town. With more time, continue east into the Douro Superior, where the landscape is drier and closer to the Spanish border.

For routing, staying in Pinhão or Vila Real is more relaxed than a day trip from Porto. Two estates and a lunch are enough for one day; four estates from Porto is a driving exercise, not a visit. The Douro line from Porto to Pinhão follows the river and is scenic, but departures are infrequent, so check the return train before committing.
