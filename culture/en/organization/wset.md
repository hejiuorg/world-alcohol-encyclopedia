# WSET: turning tasting into an examinable qualification

WSET is often treated as a certificate, but it is first a graded course and only then an awarding body. Its legal identity, its governance and its examination structure are more useful to understand than the acronym, because they explain both what it can do and what a certificate cannot do: it can make tasting teachable, but it cannot turn a certificate into a licence to practise.

## From London in 1969

In 1969 much of the British wine trade's training still passed from master to apprentice and through in-house merchant courses, with inconsistent standards. WSET was founded in London to serve the local trade: merchants, restaurants, hotels and importers needed a shared language for judging wine, describing it and explaining it to customers. It broke tasting, regions, winemaking and regulation into units that could be taught at levels, and that is what later allowed it to expand into other countries and other subjects.

## An awarding body, a charity and a regulated qualification

WSET has two legal layers. First, it is an awarding body: it designs and issues its own qualifications. Second, it is a registered charity, run on a not-for-profit basis, with income spent on its educational mission rather than distributed to shareholders.

Its qualifications are recognised in the United Kingdom by Ofqual, the office regulating qualifications and examinations, so some sit within the regulated qualifications framework; it also runs a quality management system to ISO 9001:2015. Recognition and certification mean that processes are externally audited. They do not mean that government runs the school or endorses every line of the syllabus.

## Governance: how a foundation without members decides

Unlike a trade association, WSET has no layer of member companies. It is governed by a board of trustees, with an executive team running day-to-day operations and academic teams, question-setting and moderation controlling the courses and examinations. That structure shapes its stance: it does not have to balance growers against merchants, and it does not speak for any region. It answers to students, approved course providers and the requirements of its recognition.

Course revisions therefore tend to be justified by teaching standards, examination validity and industry demand rather than by the short-term interest of one country's trade. That logic is visibly different from an interprofessional body's.

## How the four levels and their examinations work

Courses are graded by difficulty. The first level is an introduction to the main drinks and basic tasting; the second adds detail on regions, varieties, production and labels; the third demands stronger analysis and systematic tasting; the fourth is the Level 4 Diploma in Wines, close to expert depth. Spirits, sake and other subjects have separate branches.

Examinations use multiple-choice and short-answer papers, and some levels require blind tasting. Blind tasting is not a guess at the name of the wine; it is a sequence of appearance, aroma, palate and conclusion that turns observation into a checkable description. That method later became a shared trade language in many markets.

## Candidate numbers and reach that can be checked

Several figures can be checked: courses are offered in more than 15 languages and reach more than 70 countries and regions; in 2024, the year of WSET's 55th anniversary, there were about 134,000 candidates, the second-highest total in its history; and 1,962 Diploma candidates came from 19 countries, up about 15%.

These figures measure different things: languages and countries describe reach, the candidate total describes annual scale, and the Diploma number describes the advanced group. Each should travel with its year rather than being compressed into “over a hundred thousand students worldwide”.

## Why the system carries influence

The hard part of wine is not drinking but saying what was tasted. WSET supplies a teachable descriptive framework, so that people of different nationalities and first languages can discuss the same glass. For merchants and restaurants this gives recruitment and training a common reference; for regions and importers it means that a market can be educated with ready-made courses.

The influence is infrastructural. It does not change the quality of a wine, but it changes how efficiently the trade talks about it. Once a vocabulary is widely adopted, the cost of discussion falls and comparison becomes easier.

## Boundaries and controversies

First, a course is not a region's law. WSET teaches a general framework, while the rules of a particular appellation still require local official material. Second, a certificate is not ability: passing shows that a student commands the descriptions and knowledge, while real judgement still needs repeated practice. Third, standardisation has a price: a common language helps communication and may also flatten local knowledge. Fourth, cost and access: courses and examinations take time and money, and access differs between markets. Fifth, commercial relationships: WSET sits in a chain with approved course providers and textbook publishing, so readers should separate the educational standard from market promotion.

## Common misreadings

Taking WSET for a government or international organisation; taking the certificate for a taster's licence; taking blind tasting for guessing; taking the course for a regional guide. All four come from one confusion: treating an educational standard as a legal qualification. A certificate proves training; it does not authorise practice.
