# Mendoza

Mendoza is Argentina's largest wine region, with vineyards on the dry oases of the eastern foothills of the Andes, known for high altitude and Malbec.

![Mendoza photograph](/media/photos/q2-region-mendoza-043c2ae0.jpg)

Figure: Mendoza. Photograph: Mendoza Vineyard. Credit: Justin De La Ornellas, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons ([source](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?curid=25786871)).

## Definition and scope

Mendoza is a wine region in Mendoza Province, covering the oases around the provincial capital and San Rafael, Malargüe and General Alvear in the south. In the 2021 INV count the province had about 146,815 hectares of vineyards, 75.8% of Argentina's vineyard area and 79.8% of the Cuyo region.

Inside the region, production is divided by oasis and river valley. Vineyards are not continuous but separated by dry mountains; oases within the same province differ greatly in altitude, soil and water source.

## History

Viticulture in Mendoza began in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, introduced by missionaries and colonial settlers and based mainly on local Criolla varieties. Industrial production began in the 1850s, when European varieties were introduced on a large scale; the arrival of the railway and European immigrants in the 1880s further expanded vineyards and wineries.

Argentine wine consumption peaked at about 90 litres per person in the 1970s; habits then changed and the industry went through a crisis in the 1980s. After liberalisation in the 1990s, Mendoza turned to exports with international advisers and vineyard replanting, and Malbec became the Argentine variety best known abroad.

Malbec originates in south-west France and arrived in Mendoza with European varieties in the mid-nineteenth century. Its behaviour in Argentina differs from that in its home region of Cahors, and it became the country's signature red grape.

## Geography and climate

Mendoza lies on the eastern foothills of the Andes; Aconcagua, at 6,959 metres the highest peak in the Americas, is in the province. The Andes block moist Pacific air and the distance from the Atlantic makes the region semi-arid, with about 220 mm of rain a year, more than 300 sunny days and hot summers and cold winters.

Vineyards depend almost entirely on Andean snowmelt. Water from the Tunuyán, Mendoza and Diamante rivers reaches the oases through reservoirs and canals, and modern vineyards mostly use drip irrigation. Water management is therefore a central variable, since shortage limits yield and ripening while excess dilutes flavour.

Soils are mainly alluvial, including gravel, sand, silt and clay; the Valle de Uco has more calcareous and stony soils, while Maipú has deeper, clay-rich soils with better water retention. Differences in drainage and water holding create [terroir](ref:terroir) differences within the oases.

## Altitude and ultraviolet light

Mendoza vineyards run from about 650 to 1,500 metres: Maipú at about 650–850 metres, Luján de Cuyo averaging about 900–1,100 metres, and the Valle de Uco at about 1,000–1,500 metres. Altitude is not a single variable, but at the same latitude it gives an otherwise warm region a longer ripening season and a larger day-night range.

Altitude works through two mechanisms. The first is temperature; air cools with height and nights are colder, so grapes respire less at night and retain more [acidity](ref:acidity). The second is radiation; the atmosphere is thinner with height, ultraviolet light, especially UV-B, is stronger, and the sky is clearer. UV-B induces flavonoid and anthocyanin synthesis in the skins and thickens the cuticle; combined with a large day-night range, this often shows as deeper colour and more complete tannin.

Ultraviolet light is also a stress factor. Too much radiation combined with heat causes sunburn, skin dehydration and loss of aroma compounds. The region manages light through canopy management, leaf removal, row orientation and shade cloth rather than simply seeking higher ground. The idea that higher altitude always means better quality does not hold, and style depends on site, variety, water and vintage.

## Sub-regions

Mendoza is usually divided into five sub-regions:

- Valle de Uco, made up of Tunuyán, Tupungato and San Carlos, at about 1,000–1,500 metres, with stony and calcareous soils, the area most concentrated in recent premium wine and experimental planting. About 29,105 hectares, of which Malbec accounts for about 16,051.
- Primera Zona, the Luján de Cuyo and Maipú areas around Mendoza city, at lower altitude and with the longest history. Luján de Cuyo has about 15,545 hectares and Maipú about 10,931.
- Northern oases, Lavalle and Las Heras.
- Eastern oases, San Martín, Rivadavia, Junín, Santa Rosa and La Paz, with large volumes and a history of bulk wine and domestic consumption.
- Southern oases, San Rafael, Malargüe and General Alvear.

## Varieties and raw material

In the 2021 count Mendoza had about 93,051 hectares of red varieties, 63.4%, about 21,998 hectares of white varieties, 15.0%, and about 31,766 hectares of pink-skinned varieties, 21.6%. The pink-skinned group is mainly Cereza, about 13,492 hectares, and Criolla Grande, about 12,411 hectares, mostly used for domestic and bulk wine and an easily overlooked base of production.

Malbec covers about 39,248 hectares, 26.7% of the provincial vineyard and 20.2% of the national Malbec area, the largest single variety. It is followed by Bonarda at about 14,824 hectares, Cabernet Sauvignon at about 10,494, Syrah at about 8,191, Tempranillo at about 5,047 and Merlot at about 3,722. Among whites, Pedro Giménez covers about 7,086 hectares, Chardonnay about 4,711, Torrontés Riojano about 3,360, Sauvignon Blanc about 1,446 and Chenin about 1,435. The raw material is [grape](ref:grape).

Malbec has deep-coloured skins and medium to full tannin, and in Mendoza usually shows blackberry, plum, violet and spice; high-altitude sites have higher acidity and clearer floral notes, while warmer low sites are riper and broader. Bonarda is deep in colour with higher acidity and has long been used for blends and domestic wine. Cabernet Sauvignon performs reliably in Luján de Cuyo and the Valle de Uco and is often blended with Malbec and Cabernet Franc.

## Winemaking

Red grapes are destemmed, sometimes partly as whole clusters, and macerated according to variety and style. Malbec often uses gentle pumpovers and a shorter maceration to preserve fruit, while structured wines macerate longer and are [aged](ref:oak-ageing) in oak; the share of new barrels varies by winery, with 12–18 months common for top wines.

White grapes are [fermented](ref:fermentation) cool, and most wines avoid malolactic fermentation to keep acidity. Torrontés preserves its floral character through low temperatures and early picking; some Chardonnay is fermented in oak with lees stirring. Pedro Giménez and the Criolla group mostly go into volume wine, with fermentation and clarification aimed at stability.

## Origin labelling

Argentina's origin system was established by the 1999 wine law, Ley 25.163, with three categories managed by the INV: Indicación de Procedencia (IP), Indicación Geográfica (IG) and Denominación de Origen Controlada (DOC). IP is the broadest and DOC the strictest, setting area, permitted varieties, yields and winemaking rules.

Mendoza has two DOCs. Luján de Cuyo was Argentina's first DOC and San Rafael the second. There are many more IGs, including Agrelo, Las Compuertas, Lunlunta, Barrancas, El Paraíso and Russel in the Primera Zona, and La Consulta, Paraje Altamira, Los Chacayes, San Pablo, Vista Flores, Tupungato, Tunuyán and San Carlos in the Valle de Uco.

IGs and DOCs describe origin and rules, not a quality ranking. Wineries, vintages and sites within the same IG still differ clearly, and a place name on a label must match the registered right of use for that product.

## Labels and serving

The label should be checked for the DOC or IG name, variety, [vintage](ref:vintage), [alcohol](ref:abv), volume and bottler. A wine labelled as a [single variety](ref:single-varietal) must meet the legal variety share; blends must list their varieties under the rules. Malbec and Cabernet Sauvignon are served at about 16–18 degrees Celsius, as is Cabernet Franc; Chardonnay at about 10–12 degrees Celsius, and Sauvignon Blanc and Torrontés at about 8–10 degrees Celsius.

Malbec suits grilled meat, red meat and hard cheese; high-altitude Malbec is tighter in structure and suits richer dishes. Torrontés suits spicy and Asian food. Refrigerate after opening and finish soon. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

## Common misconceptions

First, thinking Mendoza makes only Malbec. Malbec is about 26.7% of the vineyard, and Bonarda, Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah and pink-skinned varieties also cover large areas.
Second, taking Malbec for a native Argentine variety. It comes from south-west France and reached Mendoza only in the mid-nineteenth century.
Third, assuming higher altitude means better quality. Altitude brings a longer season and higher acidity, but also sunburn, frost and water-management problems.
Fourth, taking DOC and IG for quality grades. They set origin and rules and do not rank wines by body or price.
Fifth, assuming Mendoza relies on natural rainfall. It receives about 220 mm a year, and vineyards depend on Andean snowmelt.
