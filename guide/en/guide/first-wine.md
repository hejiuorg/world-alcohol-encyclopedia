# Choosing your first wine

The difficulty of a wine shelf is not that there are too many wines, but that the label carries too much information. For a first purchase you do not need every term. Filter by occasion, colour, sweetness, strength and budget, then use the label to confirm identity, and most pitfalls disappear.![Choosing your first wine thematic illustration](/media/photos/theme-wine-spirits.jpg)

## Fix the occasion first

Everyday drinking, food pairing, a party and a gift call for different choices. For everyday drinking, start with a single bottle in a everyday price band; for food, decide the dish first; for a party, consider whether the bottle will be finished; for a gift, consider packaging and a gift box. Once the occasion is fixed, colour and type become much easier.

For a first purchase, do not buy several different types at once. Buy one bottle, note what you like or dislike, then buy a second for comparison. This builds your own preference faster than buying a whole recommended list.

## Colour and type

[Wine](ref:wine) is commonly divided into [red wine](ref:red-wine), [white wine](ref:white-wine), [sparkling wine](ref:sparkling) and [fortified wine](ref:fortified). Red wine ferments with the skins and usually has [tannin](ref:tannin) and structure; white wine ferments without skins and tastes fresher; sparkling wine has bubbles and suits celebrations; fortified wine has higher alcohol and a denser flavour.

| Type | Typical flavours | Serving temperature | Suitable occasion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dry red | Black fruit, structure | 16–18°C | Red meat, stews, family meals |
| Dry white | Citrus, green apple, freshness | 8–12°C | Seafood, lighter dishes |
| Sparkling | Bubbles, acidity, lightness | 6–8°C | Celebrations, starters |
| Fortified | Dried fruit, nuts, richness | 14–18°C | After a meal, small glasses |

For a first bottle, choose either dry red or dry white. For dry red, look at [Cabernet Sauvignon](ref:cabernet); for dry white, look at [Chardonnay](ref:chardonnay). Both are widely planted and span many styles, which makes comparison easier.

## Sweetness, strength and volume

Sweetness is not a scale of quality. Dry, off-dry, semi-sweet and sweet are style choices; if unsure, dry or off-dry is safer for a first purchase, and sweetness should not be confused with quality. [Alcohol by volume](ref:abv) is usually 11%–15%; higher alcohol brings more heat, lower alcohol feels lighter.

The common volume is 750 mL, with small bottles also available. A small bottle suits one person, while a standard bottle suits a gathering. An opened red usually keeps for a few days, white and sparkling for less; sparkling loses its bubbles quickly and is best finished in one sitting.

## Five label checks

1. Check the type: red, white, sparkling or fortified, to fix the broad category.
2. Check the variety: Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay and others shape the flavour.
3. Check the region: country and region names offer style clues, such as [France](ref:france) and [Bordeaux](ref:bordeaux).
4. Check the vintage: it is the grape harvest year, not the time in bottle.
5. Check strength, net volume, producer and importer details.

The vintage only states the harvest year and does not mean older is better. Most everyday wines are best drunk within a few years, and only some structured wines reward long cellaring. For a first purchase, favour fresh bottles from reliable channels.

## Budget and buying

When the budget is limited, focus on the right type, good storage and a reliable channel rather than packaging. Reputable supermarkets, brand shops and authorised merchants are safer; protect bottles from light and heat, and lay cork-closed bottles down for long storage.

For a concrete reference, see [Penfolds Bin 389](ref:bin389): a Cabernet and Shiraz blend with strong structure that suits red meat and serves as a comparison point for "structure". It is not the only answer for beginners, only a coordinate for comparison.

## Temperature, glass and pairing

Red wine is served at about 16–18°C, white at about 8–12°C and sparkling at about 6–8°C. Too warm amplifies alcohol, too cold closes the aroma. Without a dedicated glass, a clean glass with a wide bowl and a slightly narrowed rim is enough, as covered in [how to choose a glass](ref:glass).

The principle of pairing is weight matching: light dishes with light wines, rich dishes with structured wines. Dry red suits red meat and stews, dry white suits seafood and poultry, and sparkling suits starters and fried food. For a first attempt, do not chase complex pairings; keep the intensity of the wine and the dish close.

## How to drink the first glass

Look at the colour, smell, then taste. Smell at rest first, then swirl gently; let the wine cover the tongue and note acidity, tannin, body and finish. You can record it with the method in [the first sip](ref:tasting) without rushing into terminology.

Do not fill the glass for the first pour; keep it below the widest part of the bowl so there is room to swirl and gather aroma. Before drinking, check for off-notes such as wet cardboard, vinegar or obvious oxidation; if in doubt, taste a small amount rather than finishing the glass.

## Common mistakes

First, treating price as quality. Second, treating older vintages as better. Third, assuming sweet wine is inferior. Fourth, using a red-wine glass for everything. Fifth, leaving an opened bottle at room temperature for a week. Sixth, reading only the front label and ignoring the producer and importer on the back.
