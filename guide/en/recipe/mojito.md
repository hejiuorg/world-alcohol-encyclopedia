# Mojito recipe (IBA version)

This is one [Mojito](ref:cocktail) recipe with a stated source. The Mojito is a long drink built in the glass: press the mint gently, dissolve the sugar first, use plenty of ice and add the soda water last. Get the order wrong and the drink is either too sweet or has lost its bubbles. This page records the public IBA version and explains the purpose of each step.![Mojito recipe (IBA version) thematic illustration](/media/photos/theme-ingredients.jpg)

## A recipe with a stated source

The public IBA (International Bartenders Association) recipe uses 45 ml Cuban white rum, 20 ml fresh lime juice, six mint sprigs, two teaspoons of white cane sugar and soda water, built in the glass and garnished with a mint sprig and a lime slice. This page follows that version and explains the purpose of each step so that a problem at home can be traced to a specific stage.

The recipe version is recorded separately from the cocktail identity so that it stays traceable. Bars make Mojitos very differently: some shake and strain, some use syrup, some pile in crushed ice, some use other citrus. These differences are not about who is "authentic"; they are different versions and should carry their own sources.

## Ingredients and ratio

- Cuban white rum: 45 ml
- Fresh lime juice: 20 ml
- Mint: 6 sprigs
- White cane sugar: 2 teaspoons
- Soda water: about 60 ml
- Ice: as needed

There is no fixed decimal ratio here because it depends on how fresh the mint is and how acidic the limes are. These numbers are the IBA baseline: rum as the body, lime for acidity, sugar for sweetness, soda for bubbles and volume. Push any one too far and the drink changes direction.

Quantities are a starting point, not a law. Glass capacity, ice amount and mint variety all change the result. Make the first drink to the numbers here, then adjust to the balance on the palate, changing one variable at a time.

## What each ingredient does

[White rum](ref:rum) supplies cane sweetness and the alcoholic frame. It is usually filtered, pale and light in flavour, which suits lime and mint; aged rum carries more barrel flavour and changes the direction of the drink.

Mint supplies the cooling sensation and aroma that make the drink recognisable. Stale mint has little aroma and can even taste grassy; pressing gently releases only the oils on the surface, while crushing hard brings out bitterness and chlorophyll.

Lime juice supplies acidity and freshness, and white cane sugar supplies sweetness to balance it. The sugar must dissolve before the ice goes in, or it sinks and the last sips turn suddenly sweet. Soda water supplies bubbles and lift and also dilutes; it goes in last so the bubbles are not stirred away.

## Method

Put the mint, sugar and lime juice in a highball glass and press gently a few times; stop as soon as the leaves begin to break and the aroma comes up. Then add a little soda water and stir to help the sugar dissolve; a small amount of liquid is enough, there is no need to fill the glass yet.

Fill the glass with ice, pour in the white rum and top up with soda water, then stir gently a few times with a bar spoon so the rum, lime, sugar and soda mix while as many bubbles as possible stay. Garnish with a mint sprig and a lime slice and add a straw. For the general principle of building and stirring, see [cocktail basics](ref:cocktails-guide).

## Ice and bubbles

A Mojito is a long drink, so the amount of ice decides temperature and dilution. Too little ice and the soda warms quickly and the texture goes flat; too much ice in a small glass leaves no room to stir. Use plenty of ice, ideally large cubes rather than crushed, so the drink stays cold without thinning too fast.

Bubbles are part of the structure, not decoration. Adding soda last and stirring gently limits bubble loss; shaking brings in air and crushed ice and drives carbonation off quickly. For a fresher drink you can raise the soda share, but adjust sugar and lime at the same time so the flavour is not washed out.

The real strength of the drink depends on the [alcohol by volume](ref:abv) of the white rum, the amount of sugar and the dilution from soda, not on the millilitres alone. For ice shapes, see [ice and temperature](ref:ice).

## Glass and temperature

Traditionally the drink is served in a [highball glass](ref:vessel): straight-sided and large enough to hold ice, mint and soda. The rim should not be too narrow or the mint sprig will not fit; the wall should not be too thin or the drink warms quickly in the hand. Chill the glass first so the ice melts more slowly.

Without the proper glass, choose any straight-sided glass with enough capacity; the key is room for plenty of ice and space to stir. Too small a glass limits the ice; too large a glass thins the carbonation. The glass is not part of the definition, but it shapes the experience.

## Garnish

The mint sprig gives visual recognition and extra aroma. Use fresh mint with intact leaves and set it in the rim rather than crushing it at the bottom; nosing near the rim brings out the cool sensation. The lime slice adds citrus aroma and visual contrast.

Add the garnish last, just before serving. Mint left in too long goes black and loses aroma; a lime slice soaked too long releases extra bitterness. The garnish complements rather than covers; more mint is not better, since too much tastes grassy and blocks the straw.

## Common mistakes

First, crushing the mint. Over-pressing releases bitterness; a gentle press is enough.

Second, not dissolving the sugar. Sugar at the bottom makes the last sips too sweet and throws off the balance.

Third, too little ice. The soda warms quickly, the bubbles fade and the texture goes flat.

Fourth, shaking instead of stirring. Shaking destroys the bubbles and the ice layer; a long drink does not need it.

Fifth, adding soda before the ice. The bubbles are consumed by the impact of ice and by stirring; top up last.

## Version differences and variations

Aged rum instead of white rum brings barrel flavour and a heavier body; less sugar or more lime changes the sweet-sour balance; adding fruit such as strawberry or passion fruit makes a different variation; another carbonated drink instead of soda changes sweetness and carbonation; shaking and straining gives a more aerated, more diluted texture.

## Limits

This page records one source version only. It does not rank brands and does not present one mint or sugar as the only correct choice. A recipe is a starting point, not a law; any change should be labelled as a difference. A long drink is easy to finish without noticing, so watch the total and the pace. Do not drink while driving, taking medication or pregnant, and follow local law.
