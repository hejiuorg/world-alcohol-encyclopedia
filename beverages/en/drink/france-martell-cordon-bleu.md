# Martell Cordon Bleu

Martell Cordon Bleu is a Cognac created by [Martell](ref:pernod-ricard) in 1912 and belongs to the Cognac type within [brandy](ref:brandy). Borderies eaux-de-vie are its signature component, and it is commonly 40% ABV in 700 mL bottles, with the blend proportions and age composition undisclosed.

## Definition and position

Separate the levels first: brandy is the broad family of fruit distillates, Cognac is a legally defined type from the French Cognac region, Martell is the brand and company, and Cordon Bleu is a specific product. Keeping the four apart prevents confusion between regional rules, brand history and the age of the liquid.

Cordon Bleu is not a legal grade and not a vintage statement. The legal Cognac grades are VS, VSOP and XO, among others; Cordon Bleu is the brand's own product name and range. It presents a style through a blend of eaux-de-vie, and no single vintage is stated on the bottle.

## Producer and brand

Martell was founded by Jean Martell in 1715, the earliest of the major Cognac houses, and now belongs to the [Pernod Ricard](ref:pernod-ricard) group. The brand has long made Borderies eaux-de-vie central to its style, emphasising softness, fruit and a fine structure.

Cordon Bleu was created in 1912 by Édouard Martell, and the name comes from the blue ribbon (Cordon Bleu); the exact naming background should be checked against official brand material, and this page does not treat it as a legal regional or grade name. Brand history and product age are separate dimensions, and a founding year cannot replace bottle age.

## Region and ingredients

Cognac must come from the delimited [French](ref:france) Cognac region, whose main sub-areas include Borderies, Grande Champagne, Petite Champagne and the surrounding areas. Cordon Bleu features Borderies eaux-de-vie, from soils known for clay and limestone, which typically give fine aromas and a soft entry.

The raw material is [grape](ref:grape), and Cognac mainly uses white varieties such as Ugni Blanc; a page for that variety is not yet available, so it is mentioned in text only. The grapes are pressed and fermented into a low-alcohol liquid before distillation. Soil and fruit shape the backbone, but distillation and ageing decide the final style.

## Production and blending

Cognac is [distilled](ref:distillation) twice in copper pot stills to produce high-strength eaux-de-vie, which are then [aged](ref:oak-ageing) in oak barrels. During ageing the liquid interacts with the wood, darkens in colour and moves from fruit notes toward dried fruit, spice and wood.

Cordon Bleu combines eaux-de-vie from several crus and vintages, and [blending](ref:blending) sets the final balance. The number of components, their proportions and the age of the oldest component are not disclosed; official material usually describes style rather than figures, so a single number should not be used to infer the age of the whole bottle.

## Specification and label

The common [alcohol by volume](ref:abv) is 40% ABV, with 700 mL bottles plus gift and market-specific versions. The label should be checked for brand, product name, Cognac type, ABV, net content, bottler and batch details; the local-language back label and importer information also support identity checks.

Cognac labels follow regional rules, and VS, VSOP and XO have legal meanings, but Cordon Bleu is a brand name and takes no part in the legal grading. Label language, ABV and volume can differ between markets, so the actual bottle label governs.

## Flavour and tasting

Typical aromas include plum, peach, candied fruit, vanilla and sandalwood with gentle spice; the palate is soft, with fruit and woody sweetness interwoven, a medium-to-full body and a long finish. The fine fruit character of Borderies eaux-de-vie is a recurring feature of the style.

Taste it neat at room temperature in a tulip-shaped glass that gathers the aromas; a few drops of water open the nose but change the balance. Check for sharp alcohol or excessive bitterness, which may relate to the batch or storage conditions. Ice lowers the temperature and dilutes the flavour.

## Drinking and pairing

Neat is the usual serve, and a little water or ice also works. Dark chocolate, dried fruit, cigars and after-dinner desserts pair well; fruit and sweetness contrast with salt and fat. Avoid very spicy dishes at the same time, since they can mask the finer aromas.

It can also be used in mixing, but an aged Cognac is costly, so that is a personal trade-off. After opening, store upright in a dark, cool place to limit aroma loss and oxidation; refrigeration is unnecessary. Excessive drinking is harmful to health, and minors should not drink.

## Buying and storage

Buy through regular channels and check anti-counterfeit marks, the local back label, importer and batch number. Prices move with market, channel and batch, so this page gives no figures; gift boxes and limited packaging do not change the identity of the liquid, and the label remains the basis for judgement.

An unopened bottle can be kept for years in a dark, cool place, standing upright to limit contact between liquid and cork. Once opened, the Cognac oxidises slowly and its aroma shifts month by month, so it is best finished within a few months. Strong odours in the storage environment can also reach the liquid.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating Cordon Bleu as a legal grade. It is a brand product name outside the VS, VSOP and XO grading.
Second, treating the founding year as bottle age. 1715 is company history, not the age of this bottle.
Third, assuming Borderies eaux-de-vie are the whole blend. They are a signature component, and the recipe includes other crus.
Fourth, assuming older Cognac always tastes better. Age affects style and price, not personal preference.
Fifth, judging authenticity by packaging or gift boxes. Channel, anti-counterfeit marks and label information are more reliable.

For Martell Cordon Bleu, begin with the base and the stated strength, then the sweetness or bitterness, and finish with how it is served; that order keeps the account grounded.
