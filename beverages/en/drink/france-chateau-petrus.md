# Pétrus

Pétrus is a Merlot red wine from Pomerol in Bordeaux, France, its vineyard lying on the eastern plateau of Pomerol with about 11.4 hectares planted almost entirely to [Merlot](ref:merlot). Pomerol has no official classification, and Pétrus owes its standing to the site, low yields and long, stable ageing. It is commonly 750 mL at about 13%–14% [alcohol by volume](ref:abv).

## Definition and position

Start with the levels: [red wine](ref:red-wine) is the category, Merlot is the variety, Pomerol is the region, and Pétrus is a specific estate and product. Keeping the four apart is what stops a brand name from being read as a grape or as a grade.

Note especially that Pomerol has no official classification comparable to the 1855 list, so the question "which growth is Pétrus" does not really exist. Its market standing comes from long reputation and scarce production, not from a grading certificate. Applying the left-bank classification system to Pomerol is a common mistake.

## Producer and brand

The estate sits on the Pomerol plateau, with a history reaching back to the early twentieth century; the Jean-Pierre Moueix family became involved in management from the 1960s and acquired the estate in 1969. The family has owned and run it since.

Estate, brand and product are three levels: the estate answers for the vineyard, team and technique, the brand answers for market position and scarcity, and the product answers for vintage and specification. Pétrus has no grand château building and its label is relatively plain, so identity should be checked through the label, vintage, importer and channel rather than through packaging.

## Region and ingredients

Pomerol lies on the right bank of [Bordeaux](ref:bordeaux) in [France](ref:france). It is small and has no formal classification. Pétrus is on a high point within the appellation, with clay at the surface and iron-rich deposits below, and the balance of drainage and water retention shapes how Merlot ripens.

The raw material is [grape](ref:grape), almost entirely [Merlot](ref:merlot). Merlot has medium berries, thinner skins and early ripening, with moderate tannin and acidity, and on clay it readily gives full fruit and a supple texture. Pétrus is not a "high-tannin power wine" so much as a wine of concentration, texture and complexity after ageing.

## Winemaking

After harvest the fruit is sorted, and fermentation runs in small temperature-controlled vessels, where maceration time and temperature shape the extraction. Yields are low and selection is strict, which sets the concentration of the wine.

The wine then ages in [oak](ref:oak-ageing), with a high proportion of new barrels whose exact share changes by vintage. Oak brings vanilla, toast and spice, and slow oxygen exposure integrates tannin. Clarification and filtration before bottling vary by year, and the estate's published information governs.

## Specification and label

The common volume is 750 mL, with [alcohol by volume](ref:abv) at about 13%–14% vol, varying by vintage. Production is limited, and public sources often mention around 30,000 bottles a year, though this fluctuates; the label and batch information govern when buying.

The label should be checked for estate name, vintage, region, net volume, bottler and importer. Pétrus has no second wine, and that fact is often used to spot counterfeits; be wary of any product claiming to be a "Pétrus second wine".

## Flavour and tasting

Typical aromas are black cherry, plum, violet, truffle and earth, developing leather, cigar box and spice with age; the palate has fine tannin, moderate acidity, a supple but weighty texture and a long finish. Young wines are relatively closed and need time to open.

For tasting, serve at about 16–18 degrees Celsius in a larger glass. Young wines can be decanted for 1–2 hours, while older wines should be decanted briefly after a small first taste so fragile aromas are not lost. Too warm a temperature pushes alcohol forward and hides the finer layers.

## Drinking and pairing

It suits red meat, roasted dishes, mushroom dishes and hard cheese; fat and protein buffer the tannin and round the wine. Truffle and mushroom aromas often echo an aged Pétrus. Avoid very sweet or sharply acidic dishes.

For Pétrus, most vintages are for drinking after 10 years or more, and good years can last longer; the window depends on vintage and storage. After opening, refrigerate and drink soon. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

## Buying and storage

Buy from reputable channels and check vintage, label, back label, importer and anti-counterfeit information. Pétrus is expensive and scarce, so counterfeit risk is relatively high; auction and secondary-market purchases should come with provenance and storage records.

For Pétrus, unopened bottles should be kept away from light, lying flat in a cool place at an ideal 12–15 degrees Celsius, avoiding heat and sharp temperature swings. Cork-sealed bottles need humidity and can be stored lying down. After opening, use a vacuum or inert gas and refrigerate rather than warming and cooling repeatedly.

## Common misconceptions

First, asking which growth Pétrus is. Pomerol has no official classification.
Second, treating Pétrus as a grape variety. It is an estate and a wine, and the variety is Merlot.
Third, assuming Pétrus has a second wine. It does not, and claims to the contrary deserve caution.
Fourth, believing right-bank wine must be low in tannin. Pétrus has real concentration and structure.
Fifth, judging authenticity by packaging and price. Channel, label and provenance records are more reliable.

For Pétrus, begin with the base and the stated strength. Then look at the sweetness or bitterness, and finally ask how it is served. The answers point to different choices, not to a single score.
