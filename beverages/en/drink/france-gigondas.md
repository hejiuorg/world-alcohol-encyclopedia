# Gigondas

Gigondas is a [red wine](ref:red-wine) appellation in France's Southern Rhône, with an AOC that covers only the village of Gigondas in the Vaucluse. It is built on [Grenache](ref:grenache), usually blended with [Syrah](ref:syrah), Mourvèdre and Cinsault. The legal minimum natural alcohol is 12.5%, the finished wines usually sit between 13.5% and 15%, and the normal bottle is 750 mL. [Alcohol](ref:abv) is a poor guide to quality here; altitude and vintage matter far more.

## A single village under a limestone ridge

The appellation's natural boundaries are unusually clear. To the east rises the limestone spine of the Dentelles de Montmirail; to the west runs the Ouvèze; to the north, the Trignon torrent. The village sits at about 282 metres, the vineyards climb from roughly 100 metres on the terraces to 500 metres on the hillside, and the ridge tops out at 732 metres. That vertical span is the starting point for everything that distinguishes Gigondas from its neighbours, and it is why this entry keeps returning to geography rather than to a generic "Southern Rhône" formula.

## Before and after the 1971 promotion

On 6 January 1971 Gigondas obtained an AOC in its own right. Until then it was one village inside Côtes du Rhône Villages; afterwards it became the first village in the southern Rhône hierarchy to be promoted to independent cru status. The change was not cosmetic. An independent AOC brought its own parcel boundaries, variety proportions, yield ceiling and minimum alcohol, and it allowed the label to read simply "Gigondas" without the Côtes du Rhône Villages prefix. The date also explains why older reference books describe a different appellation map.

## Altitude in the Dentelles: 100 to 500 metres

Altitude is not an abstract number here. Plots around 100 metres are warmer and ripen earlier; parcels at 300–500 metres are cooler at night, retain more acidity, and are usually picked several days to two weeks later. The mass of the Dentelles blocks part of the northern wind and bounces light onto the slopes, and the appellation records about 2,800 hours of sunshine a year. The result is two styles inside one AOC: lower sites give broader, sweeter fruit, while higher sites give tighter, saltier wines. Anyone who says "Gigondas is heavy" has usually been drinking from the bottom of the slope.

## Three geological eras in one appellation

The appellation is small but spans three geological periods: Jurassic grey limestone, Tertiary marl and sandstone, and Quaternary alluvial terraces from the Mindel glaciation. The terraces carry red clay mixed with stones, draining fast and holding little water; on the slopes, limestone scree covers marl and forces the roots downwards. Official material groups the soils into six classes, but the common factor is limestone almost everywhere. That is what gives the wines their tension, saline edge and fine-grained tannin rather than sheer power.

## The rulebook: 50%, 15%, 36 hl/ha

For reds and rosés, Grenache must be at least 50%; Cinsault, Mourvèdre and Syrah together must reach at least 15%; and the main and accessory varieties together must account for at least 90% of the planting. The minimum natural alcohol is 12.5%, and the maximum yield is 36 hl/ha, with a ceiling of 40 hl/ha. In 2025 the appellation covered 1,184 hectares and produced 30,162 hl, an average of about 25 hl/ha, with exports around 45% of the total. White wine follows a separate rulebook: permitted from the 2023 vintage, made mainly from Clairette (at least 70%), with accessory varieties capped at 5%, a minimum of 12% alcohol and a maximum yield of 40 hl/ha. Figures change with each revision of the cahier des charges, so check the current version when buying.

## White wine since 2023: the quiet rule change

Many Chinese-language sources still state that Gigondas makes no white wine. That was true before the 2023 vintage and is now out of date. The new rules allow a Clairette-led white with a more generous yield ceiling. Production is still tiny and the wine is rarely seen in export markets, but the change shows that an AOC is not a fossil: climate change, market pressure and lobbying by growers all move the legal boundary. Treating an old conclusion as a permanent fact is one of the most common mistakes in appellation writing.

## Three estates, three readings of the same hill

Domaine des Bosquets farms 26 hectares in Gigondas, with about 70% Grenache, 20% Syrah, 8% Mourvèdre and 2% Cinsault. The average vine age exceeds 50 years, estate records mention the "Les Bosquets" parcel as early as 1376, and Julien Brechet has run the property since 2009, fermenting and ageing parcel by parcel. Château de Saint-Cosme has belonged to the same family since 1490 and is now in its fifteenth generation; it holds about 25 hectares of old vines in Gigondas, with an average age of 60 years, and its named parcels include Le Poste, Le Claux and Hominis Fides. The estate sits at the meeting point of two geological faults, which is why it keeps returning to the idea of a geological jigsaw. Domaine Santa Duc's Les Hautes Garrigues takes a different route: later picking and a higher proportion of new oak, for a more extroverted style.

## Why it tastes cooler than Vacqueyras

[Vacqueyras](ref:vacqueyras) keeps most of its vineyards on river terraces at 60–160 metres, flatter and closer to the valley, so its wines are usually broader and approachable earlier. Gigondas climbs to 500 metres, has a higher share of limestone and cooler nights, which gives finer tannin, a straighter acid line and fruit that moves from cooked jam towards black cherry, juniper and dried herbs. The difference is not strength but temperature and altitude. Against [Châteauneuf-du-Pape](ref:chateauneuf), Gigondas has less oily roundness and more of limestone's angular edge.

## Matching, price bands and buying notes

Structured vintages suit braised lamb, wild boar stew, herb-roasted rack of lamb and hard cheese; wines from the higher plots can handle truffle dishes. Retail samples in France and Italy put entry and mid-range bottles at roughly €16–35, while flagship wines from leading estates reach €45–72, such as the top Saint-Cosme parcels. These figures move with vintage, channel and exchange rate and are only an order of magnitude. [Fermentation](ref:fermentation) and [oak ageing](ref:oak-ageing) vary by estate: whole-cluster work and large casks are common, and new oak is used with restraint so that limestone and altitude remain visible. The raw material is [grape](ref:grape), and the appellation sits inside the broader [Rhône Valley](ref:rhone) framework.

## Three misconceptions worth retiring

First, "Gigondas is just a stronger Côtes du Rhône Villages". It has been an independent AOC since 1971, with its own parcels and rules, not a village wine made more concentrated. Second, "it makes no white wine". White wine has been permitted since the 2023 vintage, even if volumes remain small. Third, "it must be 15% alcohol". The legal minimum is 12.5%, and cooler high-altitude sites can finish just above 13% in fresh years. Treating alcohol as the appellation's identity misses its real differences: altitude and limestone. The appellation also lies within [France](ref:france), whose broader appellation system shapes every label.
