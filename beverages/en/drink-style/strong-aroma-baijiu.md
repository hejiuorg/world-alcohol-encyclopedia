# Strong-aroma baijiu

Strong-aroma baijiu is one style within [baijiu](ref:baijiu) and one of the most widely produced and consumed types in the Chinese market. GB/T 10781.1-2021, Quality requirements for baijiu — Part 1: Strong-aroma baijiu, sets out terminology, requirements and testing rules for this category. Understanding the style means understanding the combination of mud pit, continuous grain topping and mixed steaming, not simply memorising the phrase "rich pit aroma".![Strong-aroma baijiu image](/media/photos/drink-style-strong-aroma-baijiu-1c3af236.jpg)

Figure: Strong-aroma baijiu. Image: Xiaomage de, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons ([source page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Vertreter_der_vier_Baijiu_Kategorien.jpg)).

## Definition and standard

Strong aroma is a style category defined by aroma, not an origin or a brand. The national standard specifies sensory requirements, physicochemical indices and test methods, and the standard number on the label is the direct evidence of category. A compliant product may print "strong aroma" on the label, but "strong aroma" itself is not a quality grade.

Geographical-indication standards and enterprise standards sit alongside the national standard. A geographical-indication standard usually adds more specific requirements for region, raw material and process; an enterprise standard may be higher than, or different from, the national standard. Buyers should read the standard, the producer and the region together rather than the style name alone.

## Process characteristics

Strong-aroma baijiu commonly uses mud pits for solid-state fermentation, continuous grain topping across multiple rounds, and mixed steaming during distillation. "Continuous grain topping" means keeping part of the fermented grain from the previous round and mixing it with new grain and new qu before the next round, so that the microbiology and flavour compounds in the pit accumulate.

"Mixed steaming" means grain cooking and fermented-grain distillation are coordinated in one flow, extracting alcohol and aroma while preparing material for the next fermentation. The process demands tight control: pit-entry temperature, acidity, moisture and starch concentration all affect yield and flavour. Regions, producers and products vary in their qu, pit management, fermentation length and blending.

## Raw material and qu

Strong-aroma baijiu usually uses sorghum as the main grain, often with wheat, maize, rice and glutinous rice in a multi-grain or single-grain recipe. Multi-grain recipes can add aromatic layers and sweetness, while single-grain recipes show the main grain more clearly. Wheat is common for making qu, and temperature and humidity during [qu making](ref:qu-making) decide the saccharifying power, fermenting power and aroma contribution of the qu.

Qu is one of the important sources of strong-aroma flavour. Medium-to-high-temperature qu brings a rich enzyme system and microbiology that participate in the formation of esters, acids and alcohols. If qu quality is unstable, later process control cannot fully compensate, which is why qu making is treated as the first gate of strong-aroma production.

## Pits and microbiology

The mud pit is the most recognisable facility in strong-aroma production. After long use, the pit wall develops a stable microbial community, especially the enrichment of ester-producing organisms such as caproic-acid bacteria. This is the value of an old pit: the longer it is used continuously, the more mature the community and the more stable the main aroma.

Pit management includes maintaining the pit mud, controlling entry moisture and temperature, and managing the fermentation period. Fermentation usually lasts tens of days, depending on region, season and product positioning. An old pit is not a simple formula for quality; a neglected old pit degrades too, and hygiene, recipe and temperature control are the real basis for stability.

## Flavour structure

The main aroma of strong-aroma baijiu is usually summarised as ethyl caproate, supported by ethyl lactate, ethyl acetate and other esters, together with alcohols, acids and aldehydes. The nose combines pit aroma, grain aroma, qu aroma and aged aroma; common sensory descriptions are sweetness on entry, a smooth body and a clean finish. Products differ markedly in how they balance sweet, smooth, clean and aromatic.

These descriptions are communication tools, not scoring formulas. A proper record names the taster, the product, the temperature, the glass and the resting time, and separates subjective impressions from verifiable facts. Aroma intensity, body weight and finish length should be judged together with product positioning and price band.

## Regions and representative products

Strong aroma has strong regional clusters in Sichuan, Jiangsu, Anhui and Henan. The Luzhou and Yibin areas of Sichuan are known for old pits and multi-grain techniques; [Luzhou Laojiao](ref:luzhoulaojiao) and [Wuliangye](ref:wuliangye) are product pages representing this style. Yanghe in Jiangsu and Gujing Gongjiu in Anhui have formed their own expressions at different price points.

Regional differences are not only geographical but also about craft tradition and product structure. When comparing strong-aroma products from different regions, read the grain recipe, fermentation length, strength and price band together, and avoid judging a whole region from a single glass.

## Tasting method

For strong-aroma baijiu, use a small glass, a small amount and room temperature. Smell the liquid at rest, then swirl gently to compare pit, grain and qu aromas; take a sip and let it cover the tongue to feel sweetness, body and alcohol heat; after swallowing, check whether the finish is clean and the return of sweetness is natural.

Comparative tasting is more reliable than judging one glass: choose two or three products in the same price band and record aroma, entry, finish and the smell of the empty glass. Empty-glass aroma is a common observation for strong aroma, but how long it lasts depends on strength, glass and environment, so it should not be a quality conclusion on its own. The strength is often 52% ABV, and products also exist between 38% ABV and 52% ABV; the difference in strength directly changes palate intensity.

## Buying and common misconceptions

First, treating "strong aroma" as a quality grade. It is only a style category; quality also depends on the standard, the grade marking and sensory performance.
Second, assuming older pits are always better. Old pits need continuous maintenance, and degraded pit mud directly affects flavour.
Third, choosing only by strength. Stronger products concentrate the aroma but also hit harder; lower-strength products are easier to drink but are not easier to make well.
Fourth, treating "rich pit aroma" as the only criterion. Strong aroma also demands harmony, cleanliness and a finish.
Fifth, ignoring the label. The standard, raw materials, producer and region are the basic evidence for whether a product meets expectations.

**Short definition:** A baijiu style associated with mud-pit solid-state fermentation, continuous grain topping and mixed steaming. Style is not a quality score.
