# The Eyrie Vineyards

The Eyrie is the beginning of modern Pinot Noir in the Willamette Valley: in 1965, 25-year-old David Lett arrived in Oregon with 3,000 grape cuttings and planted the valley’s first Pinot Noir in Dundee Hills. The 1975 Reserve Pinot Noir later beat several Burgundian wines in blind tastings in Paris and Beaune, putting Oregon on the world Pinot map.

## Position and history

The Eyrie is in Oregon’s Dundee Hills, with the winery on Kutch Street in McMinnville and the vineyards on south-facing slopes in Dundee Hills. In February 1965, David Lett planted 3,000 cuttings from UC Davis in a nursery near Corvallis while searching for a permanent site. The material included the Wädenswil Pinot Noir clone that later became central to Oregon, and the first commercially planted Pinot Gris in the United States. In 1966 he and his new wife Diana bought twenty acres in Dundee Hills and spent their honeymoon planting the first five acres.

The name comes from the red-tailed hawks nesting in the fir trees above the vineyard. David and Diana called the place “Eyrie”, both the hawk’s nest and a hint of looking at the world from a height. Their first vintage came in 1970. The 1975 Reserve Pinot Noir placed in the top ten at the 1979 “Wine Olympics” in Paris, then finished second at a rematch in Beaune in 1980, only two-tenths of a point behind Robert Drouhin’s 1959 Chambolle-Musigny and ahead of a 1961 Chambertin-Clos de Béze. That tasting is often cited as the moment that persuaded Drouhin to buy land in Oregon.

David Lett was known locally as “Papa Pinot”. He insisted on light extraction, early picking and moderate alcohol, and argued that colour density was not a measure of Pinot quality. That stance looked stubborn during the 1990s fashion for darker, bigger wines, but it made Eyrie a reference for old-school Oregon Pinot. David died in 2008; in 2005 his son Jason Lett took over winemaking and ownership, keeping the old vines and light touch while adding Trousseau, Melon de Bourgogne, native fermentations and some no-sulphur trials.

## Vineyards and terroir

The Eyrie estate consists of five Dundee Hills vineyards, from Sisters at 220 feet to Daphne at about 860 feet, all south-facing, own-rooted, non-irrigated and certified organic. The core is The Eyrie / Original Vines: seven acres of Pinot Noir planted between 1966 and 1974 at 260–410 feet, the first commercial Pinot planting in the Willamette Valley and the source of the estate’s most fragrant, complex fruit.

Sisters was planted in 1989 and originally named after the three Pinot “sisters” — Noir, Blanc and Gris. Today it holds eight varieties and serves as the estate’s test site for new rootstocks, varieties and clones. Outcrop was bought from neighbours in 2011 with five acres of Pinot planted between 1982 and 2000; Roland Green was planted in 1988 and named after David’s father; Daphne, planted between 1974 and 1984 at almost 900 feet, is one of the oldest and highest plantings in Dundee Hills, with small berries and intense flavour.

Dundee Hills soils are Jory and Nekia series, formed from volcanic lava about 15 million years ago: red volcanic clay loam over basalt cobble, with ice-age flood deposits at lower elevations. Jory drains quickly, but the cobble cracks hold enough water through Oregon’s dry summer to keep non-irrigated old vines alive. The estate’s 67 acres of vines are farmed without irrigation, tillage, herbicides, insecticides or systemic chemicals, relying on cover crops, organic matter and biodiversity.

## Winemaking and wines

Eyrie’s approach is “do little, but watch closely”. Pinot Noir is usually fermented in small lots with native yeast; the single-vineyard wines share the same fermentation and ageing framework, with about 12% new oak so that wood does not bury site differences. Pressing and ageing are gentle, and the reds are not extracted for density. The whites — Pinot Gris, Pinot Blanc, Chardonnay and Melon de Bourgogne — emphasise acidity, mineral texture and early drinking rather than heavy oak.

The range is built around single-vineyard Pinot Noir: Original Vines, Sisters, Outcrop, Roland Green and Daphne are bottled separately, like a soil profile running from low to high elevation. Original Vines is the most perfumed and complex; Daphne is the most concentrated, with herbal and stony notes; Roland Green and Outcrop sit between; Sisters is earlier and red-fruited. Beyond Pinot, the estate makes small volumes of Pinot Gris, Pinot Blanc, Chardonnay, Trousseau and Melon de Bourgogne.

Eyrie’s “old school” is not nostalgia but restraint over ripeness and extraction: alcohols are usually moderate, colour is not always deep, and the wines can seem lean when young before opening into spice, earth, dried flowers and mushroom with age. Jason’s single-vineyard series and no-sulphur trials let the estate keep its old-vine style while responding to warmer vintages and a new generation of drinkers.

## Visiting and spaces

Tastings are mainly by appointment. The winery is on Kutch Street in McMinnville, while the vineyards are in Dundee Hills; visits usually centre on the winery, and vineyard access should be confirmed in advance. The estate is small and the tasting room is not designed for large tour groups; it suits careful comparison of the single-vineyard Pinot Noirs.

If time allows, taste the five single-vineyard Pinots together: from Sisters to Daphne, elevation, vine age and soil differences become visible in one set of wines. It is also worth comparing Original Vines with a younger single vineyard to understand old vines versus newer clones. Tasting fees and available wines change with stock, so check the website before going.

## Drinking and pairing

Eyrie Pinot Noir is best served slightly cool, around 14–16°C. Too warm and the alcohol and bitterness dominate; too cold and the red cherry, dried flowers and earth close down. Young wines need twenty to thirty minutes after opening, not aggressive decanting; old-vine single vineyards can be opened an hour ahead or given a short spell in a decanter. Eyrie wines usually have high acidity and fine tannin, so heavy sauces can overwhelm them.

For food, Pinot Noir suits duck, roast chicken, mushrooms, salmon, pork and earthy vegetables. Original Vines has the complexity for roast duck, mushroom risotto and soft cheese; Daphne has the concentration for roasted lamb, venison and hard cheese. Pinot Gris and Pinot Blanc suit seafood, salads and light starters; Trousseau, served slightly cool, works with sausages, roast meat and tomato dishes.

## Practical information

From Portland, take I-5 south to McMinnville and follow the booked directions to the winery; allow about an hour from the city and five minutes from McMinnville. There is parking, but tasting slots are limited, so book ahead at weekends and during harvest. A tasting needs about ninety to 120 minutes; if you add a Dundee Hills vineyard or another winery, stop at one more, not three.

Opening hours, tasting fees and available wines change, so check the estate website before travelling. With children or older relatives, confirm step-free access, restrooms and seating; the winery and vineyards are on slopes, and some paths are gravel or grass. If the estate is closed or stock is limited, check the website and phone, and keep McMinnville wineries or restaurants as a backup.

## Etiquette and common misreadings

Eyrie’s tasting room is small, so treat it as a working winery rather than a photo stop. Arrive at your booked time, wear shoes that can handle grass, stay out of the fermentation rooms while work is going on, do not touch equipment and do not take glasses home. Ask before bringing children or a large group. If you want to compare seriously, use the spit bucket; if you are driving, keep to one or two tastes or buy a bottle to take away.

The most common misreading is to treat Eyrie as only “the winery from the 1975 tasting”. The tasting made it famous, but what sustains it is five own-rooted, non-irrigated, organic old-vine sites and Jason’s continued work with single vineyards and native ferments. Another mistake is judging Pinot quality by colour; David Lett spent his life arguing against that, and Eyrie wines are often light in colour yet longer in aroma and ageing.

## Surroundings and routes

Eyrie works as the historical starting point of a Dundee Hills day: continue to Domaine Drouhin Oregon, Domaine Serene or Sokol Blosser, or return to McMinnville for lunch. With half a day, choose Eyrie, one contrasting winery and lunch, and do not cross to Eola-Amity Hills. With a full day, add McMinnville and Dundee, but allow for driving and bookings.

The best way to understand Eyrie is to compare Original Vines with Daphne on the same trip, recording how elevation, vine age and ripeness change aroma, acidity and tannin, then use Sisters as an entry reference. Once those differences are clear, comparisons with other Oregon estates become much easier.
