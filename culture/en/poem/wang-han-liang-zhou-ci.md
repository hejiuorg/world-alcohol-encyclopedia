# Liangzhou Ci: Wang Han Sets a Cup of Grape Wine Beside the Battlefield

This poem has only four lines, yet it brings together two things that do not belong together: grape wine in a luminous cup, and a battlefield where one may die at any moment. The first two lines are about drinking, the last two about death; there is no transition, and the reader is taken straight from the banquet to the field. Precisely because it puts the most splendid and the cruellest side by side, the cup becomes complicated: it looks like a hearty drink, and also like a farewell.

## Liangzhou Ci and the frontier

Liangzhou Ci is not the title of a single poem but a lyric written for the Liangzhou tune. Liangzhou was an important north-western garrison town of the Tang, its seat in present-day Wuwei in Gansu; tunes named after Liangzhou carried a frontier colour, and Tang poets often used them to write about the border. Wang Zhihuan's The Yellow River rises far away into white clouds and Wang Han's poem here are both famous pieces under the same title.

Wang Han, courtesy name Ziyu, came from Jinyang in Bingzhou, present-day Taiyuan in Shanxi. In the first year of the Jingyun reign of Emperor Ruizong (710) he passed the metropolitan examination and rose to be a vice director of the Equipage Bureau. His dates are given variously; Gushiwen gives c. 687–726, and some scholars regard these figures as no more than guesses. Old histories describe him as bold and unrestrained, and the tone of this poem suits such a character. But a poet's character cannot replace a close reading: a good poem is often more complex than its author.

## Text

> Grape wine, luminous cup, about to drink, the pipa on horseback urges.
> Do not laugh if I lie drunk on the battlefield; since ancient times how many have come back from war?

This is the received text. Grape wine is wine made from grapes; the luminous cup is a legendary cup of fine jade that glows at night; about to drink means the cup is being raised; the pipa on horseback urges means a pipa played on horseback is urging; the battlefield is the field of war; how many have come back asks how many can return.

## Grape wine and the luminous cup

The first line sets two things side by side: the wine and the cup. Grape wine came from the Western Regions, and the luminous cup was also said to be made of fine jade from those regions; both carry a colour of the exotic and the precious. According to records, after Emperor Taizong of the Tang subdued Gaochang, grape growing and winemaking spread further into the central plains, and grape wine was not rare among the Tang elite. For the craft lineage of grape wine, see [Fruit Wine](ref:fruit-wine); note that Tang grape wine and modern industrial fruit wine are not the same.

The luminous cup need not be pinned to a particular local product. It is first of all an image in the poem: a cup that glows at night lights up a military drinking scene and gives it a slightly unreal splendour. Later ages often speak of the luminous cups of Jiuquan, but that is another local tradition and cannot be treated directly as physical evidence for this Tang poem.

The more splendid the first line, the sharper the urging in the second. The wine has not reached the mouth before the urging begins; the brighter the cup, the more visible the pressure of time.

## The pipa on horseback: urging a drink or urging a march

About to drink, the pipa on horseback urges has long had two readings. One takes the urging as a call to mount and march: just as the wine is about to be drunk, the pipa sounds on horseback and a military order hastens the departure. The other takes it as music urging the drink: the pipa is played on horseback to liven the banquet. Both readings work, but they produce different effects.

If it is a call to march, the line is the turning point between the banquet and the battlefield: the cup has just been raised when the order to leave arrives, and the later image of lying drunk on the battlefield becomes heavy. If it is music urging the drink, the line is closer to the bustle of a military banquet, the pipa is an instrument of persuasion, and the final question sounds like an after-drink reflection. The coexistence of the two readings is exactly what leaves the poem open between drinking and war.

Either way, the pipa is essential. It is both an instrument and a sound of the army; in frontier poetry the pipa is often linked with war, parting and homesickness. Placing it on horseback joins the banquet to the march.

## Drunk on the battlefield: the cost behind the brave words

“Do not laugh if I lie drunk on the battlefield” is a line of persuasion and also a mockery of himself. To fall drunk on a field that is meant for fighting sounds bold; but the word battlefield reminds the reader that this is not a garden or a restaurant but a place where people may die. Behind the bold words is risk.

“Since ancient times how many have come back from war” closes with a question. It is not a boast in interrogative form but a sadness in interrogative form: since ancient times, how many of those who went to war have returned? The poem gives no answer, and the reader knows it. To read this line as pure willingness to die for a cause misses its acknowledgement of the cost of war.

It is also because of this final question that the grape wine before it is not mere pleasure. It is closer to a deep drink taken in limited time: the finer the wine, the shorter the time seems; the brighter the cup, the more it reveals the darkness of the battlefield. The poem does not preach against war, but it lets the reader hear unease inside the heroic tone.

## Textual variants

The poem is short, and its variants concern individual characters. One common case is that grape is written with an alternative character in some editions, both being different ways of writing the same fruit; some anthologies also punctuate differently after the luminous cup, without changing the sense. This page follows the received text given by Gushiwen and checks it against Quan Tang Shi. A quotation should state which edition it follows and should not mix the wording of different anthologies.

## Common misreadings

First, taking the luminous cup as a definite artefact excavated from a particular place in the Tang. It is first of all a legendary jade cup and a literary image in the poem.

Second, taking the pipa on horseback as urging a drink only. The readings of urging a march and urging a drink coexist and should not be reduced to one.

Third, taking drunk on the battlefield as praise of war. It writes a hearty drink and also the risk of the battlefield.

Fourth, taking “since ancient times how many have come back from war” as a heroic boast. It is a question with sadness in its tone.

Fifth, taking grape wine as an everyday Tang drink. It was then associated more with the Western Regions, the frontier and elite banquets, not with everyone's daily fare.

**Short definition:** Wang Han's Liangzhou Ci has only four lines: grape wine, a luminous cup, a pipa played on horseback, and “do not laugh if I lie drunk on the battlefield; since ancient times how many have come back from war?” It places the most splendid drinking vessel and the cruellest battlefield in one scene and writes the cost of war in the tone of a drinking invitation. The last line is a question, not a boast. That is how a practical judgment about Liangzhou Ci is built.
