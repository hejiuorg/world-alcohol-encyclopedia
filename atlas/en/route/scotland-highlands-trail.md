# Highlands whisky trail: Tain to the Drumochter pass

The Highlands is the largest of Scotland's five protected whisky regions, running from the North Sea coast to the central mountains. Size means variety: two Highland distilleries can produce entirely different bodies. This route takes two, Glenmorangie by the Dornoch Firth and Dalwhinnie south of the Drumochter pass. They sit about two hours apart on the A9, one built around sea air and very tall stills, the other around elevation, worm tubs and a cold climate.

## Route overview

- Stop one: [Glenmorangie](ref:glenmorangie), The Distillery, Tain, Ross-shire IV19 1PZ; about an hour north of Inverness on the A9.
- Stop two: [Dalwhinnie](ref:dalwhinnie), Dalwhinnie, Inverness-shire PH19 1AA; about two hours southwest of Tain on the A9.
- Mode: car is most flexible. Rail reaches both, usually with a change at Inverness, and services are infrequent; check Traveline Scotland on the day.
- Duration: a full day; by rail, stay overnight rather than returning the same day.

## Why follow the A9

The A9 is Scotland's main north-south road and a cross-section of the Highlands. Leaving Tain, it passes the coast and farmland around the Dornoch Firth, runs through Inverness, then climbs towards the Cairngorms and the southern side of the Drumochter pass. In two hours elevation, wind, vegetation and road surface all change, and so do the reasons for siting a distillery: the coast offered a port and springs, the pass offered rail and road. The route avoids treating the Highlands as one fixed flavour; a regional label describes geography, not the glass.

## Tain: tall stills by the sea

In 1843 the farmer William Matheson and his wife Anne converted a brewery in Tain into a distillery; the name comes from Gaelic and means a peaceful glen. It changed hands several times and is now part of LVMH. The official history often mentions the Sixteen Men of Tain, long-serving craftsmen; today the phrase has become the 24 Distillers of Tain.

The still house is known as the Highland cathedral. Its pot stills stand 5.14 metres tall, the tallest set in Scotland; the long necks let only the lightest vapour reach the top, with high reflux, so the new make is cleaner and more floral. Water comes from the Tarlogie Springs, and the sea air is a restrained salinity rather than Islay's iodine. The Classic Tour lasts about an hour and ends with two drams; groups are capped at 12. It opens daily from June to August and Monday to Friday from September to May.

To taste: The Original brings citrus, peach, vanilla and honey; Lasanta adds sherry-cask raisin and chocolate; Quinta Ruban uses port pipes; Nectar D'Or uses Sauternes casks. Start with The Original, then one wood-finished expression.

## Dalwhinnie: worm tubs at the pass

In 1897 John Grant, George Sellar and Alexander Mackenzie built a distillery here, first named Strathspey and designed by Charles Doig; money ran short the following year, new shareholders took over and the village name, Dalwhinnie, replaced it. The reasons for the site were water, peat and rail. It is now owned by Diageo.

Dalwhinnie stands about 355 metres above sea level and is described as one of the highest and coldest distilleries in Scotland. It sits south of the Drumochter pass, where the old military road, the A9 and the railway converge; winter snow can close the road. The equipment to look at is the worm tub, a coiled copper pipe in a water tank, with some tubs still wooden. Compared with shell condensers, worms cool more slowly and the new make is usually heavier. The tour lasts about 45 minutes and ends with three malts and chocolate; groups are capped at 16 and children under eight are not admitted.

To taste: the 15-year-old brings heather honey, green apple, vanilla and light smoke; the Distillers Edition adds Oloroso raisin and orange peel.

## Handing the keys to someone sober

By car, drive north from Inverness to Tain, then retrace the A9 south through Inverness and continue southwest to Dalwhinnie. The A9 has single-carriageway stretches and average-speed cameras, and the pass can close in winter snow.

Rail is workable with planning. Tain is on the Far North Line and Dalwhinnie on the Highland Main Line, and the usual connection is at Inverness; services are infrequent. Check times with Traveline Scotland on the day. Do not drive after drinking: Scotland's limit is 50 milligrams per 100 millilitres of blood, lower than England's 80, and Highland roads are narrow and fast-changing. Use a chauffeur or a designated driver; if you drive, spit every sample.

## Seasons, beds and the scale of the Highlands

High season runs from May to September, with long daylight and better roads, but tours and lodging tighten. Winter is quiet, daylight is short, the pass is less predictable and some distilleries open only on weekdays.

Stay in Tain, Inverness or Dalwhinnie village: Tain is quiet and coastal, Inverness has the widest choice, and Dalwhinnie is small, better as a stop than a base. A two-hour drive on the map becomes more than three hours with meals and fuel.

Under the Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009, a single malt must be made at one distillery from 100% malted barley, distilled in pot stills, matured in oak for at least three years, bottled at no less than 40% alcohol by volume, and distilled to less than 94.8% alcohol by volume. Both labels say Highland, but still shape, condensation, cask type and warehouse climate decide the style.
