# Yu Lin Ling: Liu Yong and a Farewell Cup He Could Not Drink

Farewell songs often contain wine, but few make the wine as awkward as this one: the wine is already set out, the guest has not yet left, and the cup cannot be raised. Yu Lin Ling opens on an evening when the autumn rain has just stopped, then moves to the farewell tent outside the city gate, to the boatman urging departure and the two people holding hands, and finally to the morning after the wine. Wine appears only twice in the whole song, once in “no heart for the tent farewell” and once in “where will I wake from wine tonight”, and neither occasion is a happy drink.

## The tune and its background

Yu Lin Ling is the name of the tune, also written with a homophone character. According to records such as Zheng Chuhui's Miscellaneous Records of Emperor Ming of the Tang, during the An Lushan rebellion Emperor Xuanzong fled to Shu and, on a mountain-plank road in the rain, heard bells and, missing Yang Guifei, made this tune. This is a legendary account: it explains the origin of the tune, not the date of Liu Yong's song. Liu Yong used an old tune to write about parting, and the exact year of composition is not recorded.

Liu Yong (c. 987–c. 1053), originally named Sanbian, courtesy name Jingzhuang, later renamed Yong with the courtesy name Qiqing, was the seventh in his family and was known as Liu Qi. He came from Chong'an, in present-day Wuyishan, Fujian. In the first year of the Jingyou reign (1034) he passed the metropolitan examination and served as a vice director of the Grain Tax Bureau, hence the name Liu Tuntian. His dates and some details of his official career are variously given, and this page uses the usual approximate figures. Liu Yong spent long periods among city audiences and song houses and was skilled in the long slow-ci form; Yu Lin Ling is a representative example of how he expands a single farewell into two connected scenes.

## Text

> Cold cicadas chirp sadly, facing the long pavilion at evening, the sudden rain has just stopped.
> Outside the city gate, no heart for the tent farewell; where I linger, the magnolia boat urges departure.
> Hand in hand we look at each other through tears, and cannot speak for sobbing.
> Thinking of going on and on, a thousand miles of misty waves, evening haze heavy, the southern sky wide.
> Since ancient times the passionate grieve at parting, and how much more at the cold, desolate clear autumn season!
> Where will I wake from wine tonight? A willow bank, morning wind, a fading moon.
> This time away will be year after year; all fine days and good scenes will be set out in vain.
> Even with a thousand kinds of feeling, to whom can I speak of them?

This is the received text. The long pavilion is the post pavilion where friends saw travellers off; the sudden rain is a squall; the city gate means outside the capital gate; the tent farewell is a farewell banquet held in a tent in the suburbs; no heart means no mood for it; the magnolia boat is an elegant name for a boat, not a claim about the timber; sobbing is choking on tears; going on and on is travelling stage after stage; evening haze is the mist of dusk; the southern sky is a general term for the sky in the south; year after year can also mean the whole year; feeling here means affection and things on one's mind.

## The upper half: no heart for the tent farewell

The upper half begins with weather. Cold cicadas chirp sadly is hearing, facing the long pavilion at evening is place and time, and the sudden rain has just stopped is the rain that has only now ended. Not one of the three lines writes a person, yet the mood of parting is already laid out: autumn, evening, rain and pavilion are all things that make people feel cold and separated.

“Outside the city gate, no heart for the tent farewell” brings the wine formally onto the table. A tent farewell was a banquet set up outside the city before departure; by the custom of the time, relatives and friends would prepare wine to see a traveller off. But the next three words are no heart: it is not that there is no wine or no food, but that there is no mood. The point of the line lies in no heart coming after the tent farewell: the more complete the banquet, the more the absent-mindedness shows.

Where I linger, the magnolia boat urges departure puts two kinds of time together. On one side the one seeing him off wants to stay a little longer; on the other the boatman is already urging. Lingering is subjective and can be extended indefinitely; urging departure is objective and has a sailing time. The hardest moment of a farewell is often when these two collide.

“Hand in hand we look at each other through tears, and cannot speak for sobbing” writes action and silence. Holding hands is the action of the hands, looking is the action of the eyes, tears are the result; by cannot speak for sobbing even speech fails. There is no grand speech and no urging to drink, only two hands held and a voice that cannot come out.

Thinking of going on and on, a thousand miles of misty waves, evening haze heavy, the southern sky wide pushes the camera into the distance. Going on and on is stage after stage, a thousand miles is distance, misty waves and evening haze are the blur in the line of sight, and the wide southern sky is the emptiness above. The traveller has not boarded, but his mind has already gone a thousand miles.

## The lower half: where will I wake from wine

The lower half opens with Since ancient times the passionate grieve at parting, and how much more at the cold, desolate clear autumn season, placing one person's parting inside a larger experience. Passionate people have always been hurt by parting, and this parting happens to fall in the cold, desolate clear autumn, so it is harder to bear. This does not claim that the pain is unique; it says that it has collided with the least suitable season.

“Where will I wake from wine tonight? A willow bank, morning wind, a fading moon” is the most famous passage. It does not write the pleasure of drinking but imagines the place and time after the wine: where will I wake tonight? The answer is a bank of willows, the wind of early morning, and a moon about to set. Wine here does not make the parting forgotten; it makes the parting something one must still face after being drunk. The moment of waking is clearer than the drunken hours.

This time away will be year after year; all fine days and good scenes will be set out in vain stretches time out. Year after year is one year after another; fine days and good scenes are worth enjoying, but without the other person present they become mere display. Set out in vain is a heavy phrase: the scenery is not bad, but there is no one to share it.

“Even with a thousand kinds of feeling, to whom can I speak of them” ends on a question. A thousand kinds of feeling is a great store of things on one's mind; to whom can I speak is the absence of anyone to tell. The line gives no answer and needs none; it supplies, at the end of the lower half, the empty place left by what the upper half could not say.

## What kind of farewell wine was the tent farewell

The tent farewell is a key to understanding this song. When people travelled far, relatives and friends would often set up a tent outside the city and prepare wine and food, a practice called a tent farewell or a parting sacrifice. It was neither a small drink at home nor a banquet in a restaurant, but a farewell place built for the occasion by the roadside. Once the tent was up and the wine laid out, the parting acquired ceremony.

But ceremony does not guarantee the drinker's mood. No heart shows that the wine was not enjoyed. One can imagine the cup merely held in the hand, or set down after a sip, because the boat was urging, because the words would not come, because tears fell first. Wine here is not there to raise spirits but to prove that this really is a farewell.

As for the kind of wine, farewells in the Song usually used grain-fermented wine, related in craft to today's [Yellow Wine](ref:yellow-wine) but not identical with it. The song gives no name, colour or taste for the wine, and this is not an omission but a deliberate one: at such a moment the taste of the wine no longer matters.

## Willow bank, morning wind, fading moon

Willow bank, morning wind, fading moon gives three things seen after waking: willows on the bank, the wind of early morning, and the moon still left at the edge of the sky. They are all light, yet together they fix a very clear time and place. Willows are often linked with parting in classical poetry, since the word for willow sounds like the word for stay, and breaking a willow branch was an old farewell custom; the morning wind says that day has just broken; the fading moon says that the night is ending. Together they are the moment when a person wakes from wine and finds that the other is no longer there.

The line lasts because it does not say directly that the poet is sad. It gives only the scene and lets the reader feel the cold. The clarity after wine is harder than the noise of drunkenness: while drunk one can forget for a while, but on waking one must remember again. This is why the song puts wine at a structural hinge: wine is not the background of the farewell but the switch that turns farewell into longing.

## Textual variants

Yu Lin Ling has some variant readings and differences in punctuation in its transmission. One common case is that some anthologies give the line rendered here as cannot speak for sobbing with a near-synonym character for sobbing; another is the punctuation of Since ancient times the passionate grieve at parting, and how much more at the cold, desolate clear autumn season, which some editions run together without the comma. These differences do not affect the overall reading, but a quotation should state which edition it follows. This page follows the received text given by Gushiwen and checks it against Complete Song Ci.

## Common misreadings

First, taking the tent farewell as casual drinking in a tent. It is a farewell banquet in the suburbs, and the point is seeing someone off, not drinking.

Second, taking no heart as meaning there was no wine. No heart means no mood; the wine and food were already prepared.

Third, taking the magnolia boat as a particular boat made of magnolia wood. It is an elegant name for a boat and need not be checked against timber.

Fourth, taking Where will I wake from wine tonight as a boast of heavy drinking. It writes the clarity of waking after wine and still having to face parting.

Fifth, taking the southern sky as a definite province. It is a general term for the southern sky and need not match modern administrative divisions.
