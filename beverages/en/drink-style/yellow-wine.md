# Yellow wine

Huangjiu is a traditional Chinese fermented grain wine. It uses rice (glutinous, japonica or indica) as the main material, with wheat qu, starter or red yeast for saccharification and fermentation, followed by pressing, clarification, pasteurisation and ageing. Alcohol is usually 14%–20% ABV; it is fermented, not distilled.

## Definition and classification

In beverage-alcohol classification, huangjiu is a fermented drink, as opposed to the distilled process used for [baijiu](ref:baijiu). By raw material and starter it divides into glutinous-rice, japonica-rice, broomcorn-millet and red-yeast huangjiu; by sugar it divides into dry, semi-dry, semi-sweet and sweet. The point of classification is to explain where flavour comes from, not to rank the wines.

The "yellow" comes from the colour formed by rice, qu and process together, and not necessarily from added caramel. Some products do use caramel to adjust appearance, and whether that is permitted and how it must be declared depends on the product standard and the label. Colour depth alone cannot tell you age or quality.

## History

Huangjiu's history is tied to Chinese rice agriculture and grain brewing. Early brewing used sprouted grain or mouldy grain to achieve saccharification, and in time the process settled into using qu to carry out saccharification and fermentation together. After the Song dynasty, the glutinous-rice process around Shaoxing matured, and wine taxes and licensed workshops pushed production towards a commercial scale.

From the 20th century, huangjiu moved from workshops to factories: strains were isolated and purified, temperature control became more stable and testing improved. At the same time, traditional steps such as ageing in clay jars and manual "kaipa" stirring were retained in some products. Understanding this change explains why one region can produce both "traditional" and "modern, stable" styles.

## Raw materials and qu

The main material is rice. Glutinous rice is high in branched starch and gives a fuller body; japonica and indica rice give lighter wines. Water is also an ingredient, and Shaoxing wine emphasises Jian Lake water while other regions have their own sources. Qu is the saccharification and fermentation agent: wheat qu cultivates mould on wheat, starter contains Rhizopus and yeast, and red yeast is dominated by Monascus and brings colour and a specific flavour.

[Qu making](ref:qu-making) decides saccharification efficiency and the flavour base. The microbial community in qu is complex, and temperature, humidity and the timing of turning all affect quality. Traditional production relies on experience, while modern production uses strain management and temperature control for consistency. Differences in qu are an important source of regional style.

## Main types

- **Shaoxing wine**: a protected geographical indication from Shaoxing, Zhejiang, brewed from glutinous rice, wheat qu and Jian Lake water; representative types include yuanhong, jiafan, huadiao, shanniang and xiangxue.
- **Fujian laojiu**: a traditional Fujian huangjiu, often made from glutinous rice and red yeast, with a sweeter, rounder flavour and the colour that red yeast brings.
- **Jimo laojiu**: a traditional Shandong huangjiu made from broomcorn millet, with a toastier, fuller style.
- **Other regional huangjiu**: Jiangsu, Shanghai, Jiangxi and other regions have their own glutinous-rice traditions, from light to rich.

The Shaoxing types differ in the proportion of ingredients and in sweetness: yuanhong is drier, jiafan uses more rice and is semi-dry, shanniang uses wine instead of water and is semi-sweet, and xiangxue uses distillers' spirit instead of water and is sweeter still. Huadiao refers more to jar packaging and ageing than to a separate sugar category.

## Production

The public process is: soak the rice, steam it, cool it, mix with qu, ferment in jars, stir ("kaipa") to control temperature, carry out a secondary fermentation, press, clarify, pasteurise, fill into jars and age. Kaipa is the key traditional action: stirring dissipates heat and provides oxygen, controlling fermentation temperature and microbial state. Fermentation usually takes place in clay jars or large vats and lasts from several days to several months.

Pasteurisation heats the wine to a set temperature to kill microbes and stabilise it, and temperature and time must be balanced: too little and the wine spoils, too much and aroma is lost. Ageing usually happens in clay jars, whose slight permeability allows slow oxidation and esterification that build aged aroma. Modern production also uses stainless steel, filtration and aseptic filling for stability.

## Flavour and tasting

Typical huangjiu aromas include rice, qu, alcohol and aged notes, and the palate runs from dry to sweet, usually fuller than wine, with a gentle alcohol feel. Dry wines emphasise umami and acidity, semi-dry are rounder, and sweet are fuller. Age brings oxidative notes like nuts and caramel, but too much tastes flat.

In tasting, look at colour and clarity, then smell: young wine leans rice and qu, aged wine leans nut, dried fruit and savoury notes. On the palate, check the balance of sweet, sour, umami and bitter, and whether the body carries the alcohol. Temperature changes the experience markedly, and the same wine smells very different at 20°C and 45°C.

## Drinking and pairing

Huangjiu can be warmed, served at room temperature or chilled. Warming is usually around 35–45°C, which releases aroma and softens the alcohol, but overheating damages the flavour. It suits hairy crab, braised pork and soy-braised duck. Adding preserved plum or ginger is common but masks the wine's own flavour.

At the table, semi-dry and dry styles suit savoury dishes, while sweet styles suit sweet food or sipping. Huangjiu can also be used in cooking, where heating changes the dish through its acidity and amino acids. After opening, refrigerate, keep out of light and finish within a few weeks, because low alcohol and nutrients both favour microbes.

## Common misconceptions

First, that huangjiu is always sweet. Dry and semi-dry products are just as common, and sweetness depends on process and ingredients.
Second, equating age with quality. Ageing changes flavour but does not guarantee everyone will like it, and storage conditions matter greatly.
Third, that darker means older. Colour may come from caramel, the starter or the vessel and cannot be used on its own.
Fourth, treating huangjiu as cooking wine. Drinking-grade and seasoning-grade huangjiu differ in standards and quality requirements.
Fifth, ignoring strength differences. Products range from the low teens to around 20% ABV, and the label is authoritative.
