# Regulation (EU) 2019/787 on Spirit Drinks

Regulation (EU) 2019/787 is the framework law for spirit drinks in the European Union. It does more than define whisky, brandy or rum: it controls whether those category names may appear on a label, how they may be combined with geographical indications, and what happens when a spirit-drink name is used in another food or in a mixed alcoholic beverage. Adopted on 17 April 2019 and published in the Official Journal (OJ L 130, 17 May 2019), it applies from 25 May 2021 and repealed Regulation (EC) No 110/2008. It is directly applicable, not a voluntary industry code.

## Where the regulation sits, and when it applies

The full title lists five areas: the definition, description, presentation and labelling of spirit drinks; the use of spirit-drink names in the presentation and labelling of other foodstuffs; the protection of geographical indications for spirit drinks; the use of ethyl alcohol and distillates of agricultural origin in alcoholic beverages; and the repeal of the earlier regulation. The aim is a single set of naming and labelling rules across the internal market, coupled with protection for traditional products and geographical indications.

Adoption was on 17 April 2019; publication on 17 May 2019, with entry into force seven days later; the main application date is 25 May 2021. Article 51 brought certain geographical-indication provisions into force on 8 June 2019. Transitional provisions cover old labels and existing stocks, so 25 May 2021 was not a cut-off for every bottle on a shelf. The accurate formula is “adopted in 2019, applicable from 2021”.

## What counts as a spirit drink

Article 2 defines a spirit drink as an alcoholic beverage intended for human consumption, with particular organoleptic qualities and a minimum alcoholic strength of 15% vol. The only exception is category 39 in Annex I, which has a lower threshold. It must be produced by distillation of fermented products, by maceration of plant materials in ethyl alcohol or distillates, by adding flavourings, colours, sweeteners or foodstuffs to those bases, or by blending spirit drinks, ethyl alcohol or distillates. It must not fall within the customs codes covering wine, beer and certain other fermented beverages.

That definition is wider than many drinkers assume. Liqueurs and cream liqueurs are spirit drinks even when much of their volume comes from sugar syrup, cream or water, and not every spirit drink is a pure distillate. The 15% vol figure is a legal floor, not a style target or a quality grade. When discussing [alcohol by volume](ref:abv), separate the statutory minimum from the actual strength of a given product. When discussing [distillation](ref:distillation), remember that maceration, blending and flavouring are also permitted production steps.

## Forty-four categories, two sets of production rules

Article 7 divides spirit drinks into 44 categories, with definitions, permitted raw materials, process limits and minimum strengths set out in Annex I. Categories 1 to 14 follow stricter rules: they must be produced by alcoholic fermentation and distillation, may use only the raw material specified for the category, may not have added alcohol or flavourings, may not be coloured except with caramel used to adjust colour, and may be sweetened only to round off the final taste and only within the category limit. Categories 15 to 44 may use any agricultural raw material listed in the Treaty, and may have added alcohol, flavourings, colour and sweetening, subject to category-specific rules.

A few thresholds show how Annex I works. [Whisky](ref:whisky) must be distilled below 94.8% vol, matured for at least three years in wooden casks of no more than 700 litres, and bottled at no less than 40% vol. Rum has a 37.5% vol minimum; [brandy](ref:brandy) 36% vol; vodka and gin are normally 37.5% vol. Category names are legal names, not marketing adjectives. A product that meets a category must use that category name; a product that meets none may only use the residual name “spirit drink”. EU “single malt” requires one distillery and 100% malted barley, but not pot stills; [Scotch whisky](ref:scotch) adds that requirement, so the terms differ.

## Legal names and labelling

Article 10 is the heart of the labelling rules. A spirit drink must carry its legal name. If it complies with an Annex I category, it uses that category name; if it complies with none, it uses “spirit drink”; if it complies with several categories, it may use one or more of those names. A geographical indication may supplement or replace the legal name, provided the product specification is met and consumers are not misled. The regulation also prohibits the use of category names or geographical indications on non-compliant beverages, including with words such as “like”, “type”, “style”, “made” or “flavour”. “Style of Cognac” does not make a non-Cognac product lawful.

“Dry” is specially controlled: apart from category 2, whisky, an unsweetened spirit drink may add it; category 33 liqueurs may use it under conditions even though sweetened. “Dry whisky” is not a free stylistic description. Under Article 13, a blend may list component category names or GIs only in the alcohol-ingredient list, in uniform type and descending order of pure alcohol. An age statement may refer only to the youngest component, and ageing must occur under excise supervision or equivalent guarantees.

## Geographical indications, enforcement and supervision

Chapter III covers product specifications, applications, examination, opposition, registration, amendment, cancellation and trade marks. A registered name is protected against direct or indirect commercial use, imitation or evocation, false or misleading origin indications, and translations or transliterations with terms such as “kind”, “type” or “style”. Scotch whisky, Cognac, Armagnac, Grappa and Ouzo are examples; they also connect to national [appellation](ref:appellation) systems, though the legal layers differ.

Enforcement is shared. The Commission keeps the register and runs the registration process; Member States designate authorities for checks, market surveillance and penalties, with sanctions set in national law. Articles 38 to 40 cover verification against product specifications and monitoring of names on the market, while Article 43 requires Member States to carry out checks. For exporters, this means that “sold in the EU” and “sold in a third country” may attract different rules. For writers, it means that the EU regulation cannot simply be applied to a product made in the United Kingdom, the United States or China.

## Disputed points and common misreadings

“Spirit drink” is not a sign of cheapness; it is the residual name for a drink fitting no Annex I category. EU “single malt” is not Scotch “single malt”: the EU rule requires one distillery and 100% malted barley, while the Scotch rule also requires pot stills (see [Scotch whisky](ref:scotch)). Vodka need not be made from potatoes; Annex I allows cereals or other agricultural raw materials. “Dry whisky” is not permitted, because Article 10 excludes whisky from that supplement. An age statement refers to the youngest component, not the oldest. And the 44 categories are naming and production frameworks, not quality tiers.

One more difference is easy to miss. Rum may be sweetened up to 20 grams of invert sugar per litre in the final product, whereas whisky may not be sweetened at all and may not be flavoured; only water and plain caramel colouring may be added. Applying one category’s sweetness rule to another is a common source of labelling errors.
