# The 1988 liberalisation of famous-wine prices

On 16 July 1988 the State Council issued the Notice on Doing a Good Job in Liberalising Famous Cigarette and Wine Prices and Raising Some Tobacco and Alcohol Prices (Guofa 1988 No. 44), deciding to liberalise the prices of thirteen famous cigarettes and thirteen famous wines. With State Council approval, the measure took effect nationwide on 28 July. Until then, ex-factory and retail prices of famous wines had long been fixed by the state; afterwards enterprises set prices according to supply and demand. It was a key step from planned pricing toward market pricing.

## Background

From the 1950s to the 1980s, tobacco and alcohol prices had been set by the state for more than thirty years. After reform and opening, demand for famous wines was strong, but planned prices were low. Queues, rationed approvals and black-market mark-ups became normal. The State Council's publicity outline said plainly that ordinary consumers had to pay one or several times the listed price on the black market, and that the difference flowed to speculators. At the same time, institutional consumption grew too fast and grain-based distilling expanded rapidly: in 1987 grain baijiu output reached 4.2 million tonnes, beer output 5.24 million tonnes, and the industry used 1400 ten-thousand-tonne units (14 million tonnes) of grain. In 1988, as price reform advanced and inflationary pressure rose, liberalising famous cigarettes and wines was presented as a way to use the law of value, attack the black market, save grain and increase fiscal revenue.

## Course and timeline

- 16 July 1988: the State Council issues Guofa 1988 No. 44, liberalising thirteen famous cigarettes and thirteen famous wines while raising some high- and mid-grade cigarettes and grain-based alcohol in a controlled way.
- Same day: the General Office of the State Council and the Central Propaganda Department issue Guobanfa 1988 No. 29, printing a publicity outline to explain the reform uniformly.
- 25 July 1988: with State Council approval, the nationwide implementation date is fixed.
- 28 July 1988: famous cigarette and famous wine prices are liberalised across the country.
- Late 1988: retail prices of famous wines rise quickly, some doubling; collective and private distributors enter alongside the state sugar-and-wine companies.
- 1989: the fifth national wine evaluation is held, and national evaluations are discontinued afterwards, completing the handover from evaluation prestige to market pricing.

## Key people and institutions

The decision and implementation involved the State Council, the State Price Bureau, the Ministry of Commerce, the Ministry of Finance, the General Office of the State Council and the Central Propaganda Department. The thirteen came from different regions and enterprises: Moutai, Wuliangye, Langjiu, Luzhou Tequ, Gujing Gongjiu, Yanghe Daqu, Shuanggou Daqu, Quanxing Daqu, Jiannanchun, Dongjiu, Fenjiu, Xifeng and Special Yellow Crane Tower. Behind them stood producers such as the Moutai distillery, the Wuliangye distillery, [Luzhou Laojiao](ref:luzhou), [Fenjiu](ref:fenjiu), the Gujing Gongjiu distillery and the Yanghe distillery. Firms gained pricing power but were pushed into competition over brands, channels and anti-counterfeiting.

## Concrete changes for the industry

First, the pricing mechanism shifted. Famous wines went from planned allocation goods to market commodities, and enterprises could adjust ex-factory and retail prices according to supply and demand; prices began to guide production and investment. Second, the identity of famous wines became commercial. The title was no longer only an evaluation label but a realisable premium, giving firms an incentive to protect brands and improve packaging. Third, channels diversified. Beyond the state sugar-and-wine companies, collective and private distributors and wholesale markets grew quickly. Fourth, the industry divided. Firms with famous-wine resources and strong brands expanded; smaller plants without brands were squeezed. Fifth, regulation became more visible. Higher prices encouraged counterfeiting; anti-counterfeit marks, specialised stores and market supervision were strengthened. Sixth, the change prepared the ground for the expansion, advertising wars and premiumisation of the 1990s; both [baijiu](ref:baijiu) and [yellow wine](ref:yellow-wine) were repositioned within this price and channel logic.

## Disputes and later assessments

First, the exact date. The document is dated 16 July and nationwide implementation began on 28 July; sources often pick one and produce a vague "July" date. Second, price-change data. Retail prices of the time lacked unified statistics, and figures in memoirs and reports are often inconsistent. Third, policy evaluation. One view holds that liberalisation straightened out prices, encouraged production and attacked the black market; another holds that it worsened inflation and speculation, with consumer prices rising about 18.8% in 1988 and public reaction strong. Fourth, the link with evaluations. The fifth evaluation in 1989 was the last, and market pricing and the end of evaluations are often told as one story, but direct causation needs careful wording; [the 1989 fifth national wine evaluation](ref:evaluation-1989) is an institutional marker, not the sole cause.

## Common misreadings

First, 1988 did not liberalise all alcohol prices. It covered thirteen famous wines, plus controlled increases for some high- and mid-grade cigarettes and grain-based alcohol; ordinary liquor, fruit wine and most local products remained under varying degrees of management. Second, liberalisation did not complete marketisation overnight; price supervision, monopoly management and filing requirements persisted for a long time. Third, the thirteen famous wines are not the eight famous wines of 1952, nor the thirteen famous wines of 1984; the lists differ in selection context and categories. Fourth, a higher price did not mean better quality; price reflected scarcity, brand and channel more than craftsmanship. Fifth, calling 1988 the start of completely free baijiu pricing ignores the repeated adjustments and regulation that followed.
