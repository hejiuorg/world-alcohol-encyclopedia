# US Prohibition: The Experiment and Repeal of 1920 to 1933

American Prohibition is often reduced to “the country banned drinking”. More accurately, federal constitutional law and statute for a period banned the manufacture, sale and transportation of alcoholic beverages, while retaining several exceptions, and enforcement was far from fully effective. It lasted from 1920 to 1933 and ended with a constitutional amendment written specifically to repeal an earlier one. The episode is a case study in alcohol regulation, and also in the relationship between state capacity, markets and crime.

## Prohibition did not appear suddenly

Prohibition had a long prehistory in the United States. Religious revivalism in the early 19th century encouraged temperance and reform societies; abstinence pledges had been introduced by churches around 1800, and earlier temperance organisations appeared in Saratoga, New York, in 1808 and in Massachusetts in 1813. By 1833 several states had thousands of local societies.

Legal experiments also predated the 20th century. In 1838 Massachusetts passed a law prohibiting sales of spirits in quantities under 15 gallons (about 55 litres); it was repealed two years later. In 1846 Maine passed the first state prohibition law, setting off a wave of state legislation. Some of these experiments succeeded and some failed, but all accumulated one lesson: legal restrictions on the liquor trade bring together enforcement costs, consumption habits and lost tax revenue.

By the early 20th century, temperance was no longer only a moral campaign but an organised political force with national reach. Groups such as the Anti-Saloon League shifted the goal from “persuading people to drink less” to “rewriting the Constitution”, which led to the Eighteenth Amendment.

## The Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act

The Eighteenth Amendment was promoted by Wayne Wheeler of the Anti-Saloon League and others. It passed both chambers of Congress in December 1917 and was ratified by the states in January 1919. The amendment stated a principle; Congress had to legislate its enforcement. That work was championed by Andrew Volstead, chairman of the House Judiciary Committee, and became the National Prohibition Act, usually called the Volstead Act.

The Volstead Act passed over President Wilson's veto. It made the scope of Prohibition concrete and defined enforcement bodies and penalties. It is important to note that “Prohibition” did not mean every alcoholic drink disappeared: wine for religious rites, whiskey sold on prescription, and industrial alcohol mixed with noxious chemicals to make it unfit for drinking all existed to varying degrees. These exceptions later became routes for evasion and smuggling.

## 1920 to 1933: enforcement, bootlegging and speakeasies

Prohibition took effect in 1920, but neither the Volstead Act nor the Eighteenth Amendment was enforced with great success. The encyclopaedia entry notes that entire illegal economies—bootlegging, speakeasies and private distilling—flourished instead. The earliest bootleggers brought foreign liquor across the Canadian and Mexican borders and landed it from ships along the coast; the Bahamas, Cuba and the French islands of Saint-Pierre and Miquelon were common sources.

Smugglers also anchored opposite Atlantic City, using the gap beyond the three-mile limit of US jurisdiction to unload, then ran the goods ashore in fast boats. When the Coast Guard extended its searches and acquired fast patrol boats, smuggling became costlier but did not disappear. Another channel was “medicinal” whiskey, sold in drugstores on real or forged prescriptions. Industrial alcohol, made undrinkable with noxious chemicals, was still illegally diverted in millions of gallons, “washed”, mixed with water and sold to speakeasies or individual customers.

By the late 1920s, corn moonshine had become a major source. The speakeasy was the most visible urban place of consumption: a concealed front, an introduction or password, and prices set by the operator. Prohibition did not abolish demand for drink; it pushed demand into a grey market with weaker supervision, higher prices and less reliable quality.

## Organized crime and the social landscape of Prohibition

The expansion of bootlegging and speakeasies gave organized crime room to grow. The distribution of liquor was more complex than other criminal activity: it required hidden stills or breweries, storage, transport, and then speakeasies, restaurants and nightclubs. Gangs that could control the whole chain could establish a distribution monopoly in a given area.

The encyclopaedia entry uses Chicago as an example: Johnny Torrio expanded his gang's operations into large-scale bootlegging in the early 1920s and handed the territory to Al Capone in 1925; Capone became the era's most famous gangster, with wealth estimated at close to $100 million in 1927. After the stock market crash of 1929, the US Department of Justice hired Eliot Ness to head Prohibition enforcement in Chicago; his team, known as the Untouchables for being unbribable, raided breweries and speakeasies, gathered evidence and in 1932 helped send Capone to prison for income-tax evasion.

This history is often told as “Prohibition created the gangster”. More accurately, Prohibition created a high-profit, lightly regulated illegal market in which existing criminal networks expanded; gangs, smuggling and corruption did not begin with Prohibition. Prohibition amplified and reorganised them.

## The Great Depression and the policy turn

The stock market crash of 1929 and the Great Depression changed the focus of policy debate. Britannica's entry on Prohibition notes that the crash seemed to increase rather than reduce demand for illegal liquor; more importantly, the economic crisis made “is Prohibition worth it?” a more urgent question: enforcement cost money, liquor tax revenue was lost, and the illegal market diverted transactions and tax revenue to criminal groups.

In 1932 Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected president. On 20 February 1933 Congress proposed the Twenty-first Amendment, aimed at repealing Prohibition. In April Roosevelt signed the Cullen-Harrison Act, amending the Prohibition-based Volstead Act to permit the manufacture and sale of beer and wine of lower alcohol content. This step first returned some legal alcohol to the market and prepared the way for full repeal.

The sequence is visible here: repeal was not a single sudden act, but a statutory change for lower-alcohol drinks followed by a constitutional amendment. Beer therefore became one of the first categories to return to legal channels at the end of Prohibition, as discussed in [Beer](ref:beer).

## 1933: the Twenty-first Amendment and repeal

The Twenty-first Amendment completed ratification on 5 December 1933, formally repealing the Eighteenth Amendment. It is unusual in two ways: it is the only US constitutional amendment that specifically repeals another amendment; and it is the only one ratified by state conventions rather than by state legislatures.

Section 1 repeals the Eighteenth Amendment outright. Section 2 provides that the transportation or importation of intoxicating liquors into any state, territory or possession, in violation of local law, is prohibited. This returned considerable regulatory power to the states. In other words, federal Prohibition ended, but alcohol did not return to a completely free market; states could decide whether to prohibit, how to license and how to tax, and could restrict cross-state shipment.

Many features of American alcohol retail and distribution today—state licensing, county and municipal “wet-dry” differences, restrictions on cross-state shipping—can be traced to this institutional arrangement. The end of Prohibition was not the end of regulation but a shift in the level at which regulation operated.

## What Prohibition left behind

First, it left a state-level framework of alcohol regulation. The federal government no longer banned alcohol nationwide, but states gained greater room to regulate, and licensing, tax rates, sales hours and channels diverged.

Second, it left a long lesson about enforcement and crime. When the legal market is closed, demand does not vanish; it moves to channels with weaker supervision, higher prices and less reliable quality. If enforcement resources cannot keep up, the illegal market fills the gap.

Third, it left cultural memory. The speakeasy became a symbol of urban nightlife and bar culture, as discussed in [Pub and the City](ref:pub-and-city); Prohibition-era gangster stories entered film, television and popular narrative. These cultural images, however, were heavily adapted and cannot be read directly as social history.

Fourth, it changed the fate of alcohol brands. Take American whiskey: production and sales were interrupted during Prohibition, and many distilleries shut down, converted or went out of business; after repeal the industry rebuilt under new licensing and tax rules. See [Whisky](ref:whisky) and [Jack Daniel's](ref:jackdaniels) for this thread, though specific distillery histories still need checking against company archives and local records.

## Common misreadings

First, understanding Prohibition as “all alcohol was banned”. Exceptions for religious use, medicinal whiskey and industrial alcohol did exist, although their scope and enforcement varied by place.

Second, treating Prohibition as completely ineffective. It did reduce some legal consumption and the number of alcohol businesses, and it changed sales channels; but it did not eliminate demand or effectively cut off supply.

Third, equating Prohibition with a worldwide policy. It was a US federal constitutional arrangement; other countries and regions had their own prohibition, monopoly or restriction systems, which cannot simply be mapped onto it.

Fourth, treating repeal as “no more regulation”. The Twenty-first Amendment gave substantial power to the states, and licensing, distribution and cross-state transport remained tightly regulated.

Fifth, attributing all gang activity to Prohibition. Prohibition increased the profits of the illegal liquor market, but organized crime, corruption and violence existed before and after it.

Placed back in drink culture, the episode raises questions that remain relevant: when a widespread demand is closed by law, how does the market reorganise? Should regulation concentrate on supply, demand, or the channels between them? Prohibition did not provide perfect answers, but it left a sufficiently long experimental record.
