# South Korea

South Korea lies in the southern Korean Peninsula, and its drinks landscape is best known for soju. Soju is a distilled spirit, rice wine is fermented, and beer and wine hold a place in the modern consumer market. Understanding Korean drinks means separating traditional fermented drinks, industrial distilled spirits and modern imported drinks rather than lumping them under one idea of "Korean alcohol".

## Geography and climate

South Korea is surrounded by sea on three sides, with mountains and hills dominating the terrain and plains concentrated in the west and south. The climate is temperate monsoon, with four distinct seasons, humid summers and cold, dry winters. Rice is the core traditional crop and the raw material for rice wine and some soju; barley, wheat and tubers are used in distilled spirits and beer.

Seasonal change affects fermentation management. Traditional rice wine ferments faster in warm weather and needs temperature control in winter; modern breweries use temperature-controlled equipment, so production no longer depends entirely on the season. Climate also affects raw-material quality and supply, especially the vintage differences of rice and barley.

## History

Traditional Korean drinks are tied to farming, ancestral rites and home brewing, with local traditions in rice wine, fruit wine and medicinal wine. In the twentieth century, soju became industrialised, creating a market dominated by diluted soju; the beer industry grew with modern urban consumption; wine relies more on imports and a small domestic production. Traditional drinks have regained attention in recent years, with small breweries and local brands reviving them.

## Soju

[Soju](ref:soju) is Korea's most representative drink, usually made from [rice](ref:rice), [barley](ref:barley) or tubers through [fermentation](ref:fermentation) and [distillation](ref:distillation). Traditional soju emphasises raw materials and distillation and is more intense; modern diluted soju first produces a high-strength alcohol, then adds water and flavourings, giving lower alcohol and a lighter palate.

Soju types and labels need to be distinguished. Raw materials, distillation method, alcohol content and additives all affect style, and different products may use different definitions and regulatory categories. The word "soju" alone is not enough to infer the process.

## Rice wine and other traditional drinks

Rice wine (makgeolli) is made mainly from [rice](ref:rice) through [saccharification](ref:saccharification) and [fermentation](ref:fermentation), with lower alcohol and rice aroma with slight acidity. Saccharification and fermentation often run in parallel, and the choice of starter and yeast affects sweetness, acidity and fizz. Rice wine is usually kept chilled and should be drunk soon after opening.

Korea also has fruit wines, medicinal wines and regional traditional drinks. They share a fermentation tradition with rice wine but differ in raw materials and recipes. Traditional drinks are judged more by raw materials, fermentation management and local style than by alcohol content alone.

## Beer and wine

[Beer](ref:beer) is an important modern category in Korea, with large brands and small craft breweries. Beer is made from barley, water, hops and yeast, with styles from light lager to ale and stout. The Korean craft market has grown over the past decade, with local breweries gaining attention through flavour innovation and restaurant settings.

[Wine](ref:wine) in Korea is mainly imported, with restaurants and the gift market as important settings; domestic wine production is small and limited by climate. Vintage, region and variety information is central to reading a wine label.

## Regions and styles

Korean drinks production is not centred on grape regions but on raw materials and brewery locations. Rice-growing areas and urban breweries shape rice wine and soju production; beer breweries cluster near large cities and logistics hubs; wine regions are limited. Applying the "wine region" idea directly to Korean drinks ignores the role of raw materials and industrial location.

## Regulation and labelling

Korean food and alcohol labelling is administered by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, with requirements for alcohol content, ingredients, producer and warnings. Alcohol duty and sales rules are set by relevant regulations, and different drink categories have different definitions and label terms. Soju, rice wine and beer are not interchangeable categories.

When reading a label, distinguish brand, producer, type, ingredients and alcohol content. Traditional and modern industrial products carry different amounts of label information, and current regulations and product notes are the reference.

## Climate and soil data

| Western and southern plains | Temperate monsoon, humid summers | Alluvial, clay | Rice wine, soju |
| Eastern mountains | Temperate, cold winters | Granite, mountain soils | Traditional drinks, beer |
| Jeju Island | Oceanic, mild and windy | Volcanic soils | Soju, fruit wine |
| Urban areas (Seoul, Busan and others) | Urban climate, concentrated consumption | Not applicable | Beer, wine, soju |

This table is a macro comparison; raw materials and process for a specific product still need to be checked with the producer and label. The core variables in Korean drinks are raw materials and production systems, not vineyard-style geographic differences.

## Market and drinking

The domestic market is led by soju and beer, rice wine is common in restaurants and festive settings, and wine has steady demand in restaurants and the gift market. Soju suits barbecue, seafood and group dining; rice wine suits lighter dishes; beer suits everyday and restaurant occasions; wine suits formal dining and gifting.

Drinking should be moderate. Rice wine and some traditional drinks need chilling and should be finished soon after opening; soju and beer are more stable but should still be kept away from heat and direct light.

## Common misconceptions

First, that Korea only has soju. Rice wine, beer, fruit wine and wine are also produced or consumed.

Second, that all soju is traditional distilled spirit. Modern diluted soju and traditional distilled soju use different processes.

Third, that rice wine is the same as sake. The raw materials, fermentation and flavours differ from Japanese sake.

Fourth, that Korea has a large wine industry. Korean wine is mainly imported, with limited domestic production.

Fifth, that stronger soju is better. Alcohol content relates to style, occasion and personal preference.

**Short definition:** A country in the southern Korean Peninsula best known for soju; soju is a distilled spirit, rice wine is fermented, and beer and wine have steady markets, with alcohol labelling administered by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety.
