# Armagnac trail: single estates of Bas-Armagnac

The vineyards of Bas-Armagnac have no walls; they run block after block over the gentle slopes where the Gers meets the Landes. This route takes two single estates: [Château de Laubade](ref:laubade) at Sorbets and [Domaine d'Ognoas](ref:ognoas) at Arthez-d'Armagnac. The first is a private property run by the Lesgourgues family for three generations; the second is a public estate owned by the Landes department. Both keep grapes, distillation, ageing and bottling on the same land, which makes them a good pair for asking what "single estate" actually means over a day and a half.

## Bas-Armagnac: a road laid on sand

Armagnac has three subregions: Bas-Armagnac in the west, Ténarèze in the centre and Haut-Armagnac in the south and east. Bas-Armagnac is dominated by tawny sands and sandy clay, with iron deposits that colour the soil reddish brown; it drains quickly and warms early in spring, so the grapes ripen evenly, the spirit is finer and more floral, and it ages well over decades. Ténarèze is a transition zone of heavier clay and limestone, giving fuller spirits; Haut-Armagnac has sparse vineyards and a plainer style.

The appellation crosses the Gers, the Landes and Lot-et-Garonne, with an Atlantic influence in the west and a Mediterranean one in the east; the vineyard as a whole is shaped like a vine leaf. "Single estate" here does not mean a single plot but a chain: grapes, still, cellars and bottling all on one property, with vintage and batch traceable. Put the [Armagnac](ref:armagnac) map in the car, then drive west along the country roads, and the reason two sides of one road taste different becomes obvious.

## Stop one: Laubade at Sorbets

[Laubade](ref:laubade) stands at Sorbets in the Gers. The château was built in 1870 by Joseph Noulens, a statesman, and his wife Jeanne Paquin, a Paris fashion designer. Maurice Lesgourgues bought the estate in 1974, restarted distillation and revived vintage bottling; today Denis runs it with his sister Jeanne and his brother Arnaud. The 80 hectares form a single block around the château, on tawny sand and sandy clay; in the field the estate uses green manure, mechanical weeding and cereal rotations, keeps 600 sheep for organic manure, and maintains 15 hectares of Gascon oak and 10 hectares of poplars.

A single Armagnac-style continuous still, custom-built in 1974, distils the varieties separately: Baco, Ugni Blanc, Folle Blanche, Colombard and Plant de Graisse among them. The white wines are unsulphited and distilled as soon as fermentation ends. Nine cellars hold about three thousand 420-litre casks, with spirit aged separately by variety and vintage; staves are selected and seasoned on the estate from Gascon oak and made into barrels by local coopers. The estate received the Entreprise du Patrimoine Vivant label in 2015, has held ISO 9001 since 2008, claims around 250 awards, and was named Spirit of the Year 2020 by La Revue des Vins de France.

Two visits are offered: the introductory Premiers pas tastes three classic Armagnacs; the deeper L'essence de Laubade walks the cellars and vineyards, shows the monumental sculptures in the grounds and ends with five Armagnacs. Book through the Rue des Vignerons platform or on +33 5 62 09 06 02; the office is open Monday to Friday, 8:30–12:00 and 13:30–16:00, and closed at weekends and on public holidays.

## Stop two: Ognoas at Arthez-d'Armagnac

[Ognoas](ref:ognoas) lies at Arthez-d'Armagnac in the Landes, at 1043 Route d'Ognoas. Its seigneurie goes back to the late 11th or early 12th century and belonged to the viscounty of Marsan; in 1240 the marriage of Matha de Mastas to Gaston VII Moncade brought it into the Béarn inheritance until 1607. In 1274 Arnaud Bosquet was the only man entitled to call himself seigneur d'Ognoas. Etienne Lormand bought the seigneurie in 1770, his son added Tampouy in 1775, and in 1847 the last Lormand, without an heir, left the estate to the Church, which was run by the bishopric of Aire-sur-l'Adour.

The separation law of 1905 transferred diocesan property to the department, and the estate passed to the Landes. Today it is a departmental property of about 650 hectares, run by the Landes council as a showcase of the Landes terroir; its main products are Bas-Armagnac and Floc de Gascogne. The main buildings have been restored and are open to the public, and the estate and shop are open all year. Compared with Laubade's family story, Ognoas reads as land preserved by public institutions: seigneurie, Church and department stacked on the same cellars.

## The Armagnac still: the case for one pass

About 95% of Armagnac is made on the Armagnac-style continuous still. It is an all-copper apparatus standardised in 1818 by Sieur Tuillière, a stove maker in Auch under Louis XVIII, and then adapted by local distillers. Wine feeds continuously from the bottom of the cooler; alcohol vapour rises through the plates, meets the wine, and is condensed in the serpentine. The spirit leaves at 52%–72%, traditionally 52%–60%.

This is a different road from Cognac's double pot distillation. Continuous distillation is done in one pass, at a lower strength, keeping more flavour compounds and a heavier body that needs time to settle. Distillation must finish by 31 March of the year after harvest; many growers call in a travelling distiller who brings the still to the cellar door and fills the barrels straight away. Ageing follows in 400-litre pièces, mostly Gascon or Limousin oak; strength falls by about 0.5 degrees a year, the angel's share; new wood gives way to old once extraction is right, and the spirit finally moves into glass bonbonnes to stop changing. Read [distillation](ref:distillation) and [oak ageing](ref:oak-ageing) side by side, and the weight of Armagnac looks like a choice rather than a flaw.

## Vintage, grade and how to read the label

Armagnac grades also refer to the youngest component: VS (three stars) at least 1 year, VSOP at least 4 years, XO at least 10 years, and Hors d'Age with its youngest spirit over 10 years. From 2010 the trade pushed to simplify the labels, though the old names remain common. More distinctive than the grade is the vintage: it names only the harvest year, and bottles must spend at least 10 years in wood; because cellars are often humid, vintages are frequently sold at natural strength between 40%–48%.

A vintage is not a guarantee of quality but a traceable year: ripeness, acidity, the distillation run and cellar management all change the result. Laubade's Celebration Vintages range covers bottlings aged 15 to 50 years; Ognoas sells its vintages directly from the cellar and shop. First check whether the label says vintage or blend, then look at strength and bottling date; neither is better, it is a question of whether you want that year's personality. Set [brandy](ref:brandy) beside it and Armagnac's place in the wider category becomes clear.

## Putting the two stops into a Gascon itinerary

Both estates are rural and public transport is effectively unavailable, so a car is required. Coming from Bordeaux or Toulouse, take Sorbets first, then head northwest into the Landes for Arthez-d'Armagnac; the drive passes Eauze, Labastide-d'Armagnac, Nogaro and Condom. Eauze is the market town of Armagnac, Labastide-d'Armagnac is a 13th-century bastide, and Condom has the Armagnac museum; use them for supplies and overnight stays rather than forcing them into one day.

Spring and autumn are the most comfortable seasons; harvest falls in October, and winter is distillation, when the cellars are busiest but also coldest. Both stops need booking: Laubade through Rue des Vignerons or by phone, Ognoas within cellar hours, with Saturday afternoon by reservation and weekends and public holidays open 14:00–17:30 from 2 May to 30 September, weekdays 9:00–12:00 and 14:00–17:30. Tasting is free at Ognoas and charged by tour at Laubade; prices, sessions and hours change, so check official channels. Rural roads are unlit at night, so do not drive after tasting.
