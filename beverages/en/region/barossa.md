# Barossa

Barossa is South Australia's best-known wine region, north-east of Adelaide, famous for Shiraz, old vines and full-bodied reds. It is not a single GI but a zone made up of the Barossa Valley and Eden Valley regions.![related photograph of Barossa](/media/photos/region-barossa-cae6ecd8.jpg)

## Definition and scope

Barossa lies in South Australia, about an hour north-east of Adelaide. In the Australian GI hierarchy, Barossa is a zone containing two regions, Barossa Valley and Eden Valley. Each region has its own GI boundary and labelling rules.

The Barossa Valley GI covers 578 square kilometres with 11,609 hectares of vineyard; the Eden Valley GI covers 591 square kilometres with 2,169 hectares. Together the two regions hold about 14,000 hectares of vineyard and form one of Australia's most important red-wine areas.

## History

European settlers established vineyards in Barossa in the 1840s. Wine Australia records that Shiraz planted in 1843 still survives locally, regarded as among the oldest Shiraz in the world. Through the late nineteenth century the region's vineyards and wineries took shape.

The early twentieth century brought disease, economic swings and market change, but Barossa's old vines survived. From the late twentieth century Shiraz and GSM blends gained international recognition, and Barossa became a flagship red-wine region for Australia.

## Geography and climate

Barossa has a Mediterranean climate: hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. The Barossa Valley floor is warm, with a January mean of about 21.9 degrees Celsius and only about 220 mm of growing-season rain; the surrounding hills rise into cooler mesoclimates, and Eden Valley receives about 229 mm of growing-season rain with clearly cooler ripening and harvest conditions.

Altitude is central to reading Barossa. Barossa Valley vineyards sit at 112 to 596 metres and Eden Valley at 219 to 632 metres. The valley floor has enough heat to ripen Shiraz and Grenache fully; higher sites have a larger day-night range, retain acidity better and suit Riesling and tighter-structured reds.

Soils are low-fertility clay loams through to sandy soils, ranging from grey and brown to red. Barossa Valley soils differ sharply in drainage and water holding, and subsoil acidity restricts root growth; Eden Valley has ironstone and quartz gravels, with weathered mica-schist on some slopes and low water-holding capacity. These differences form the basis of [terroir](ref:terroir).

## Sub-regions

The two regions differ clearly in climate and varieties:

- Barossa Valley: warm valley floor and low slopes, with Shiraz about half the plantings and Cabernet Sauvignon and Grenache also important; Grenache is often blended with Shiraz and Mataro as GSM.
- Eden Valley: higher and cooler at ripening, with Riesling the leading white and Shiraz the leading red; Riesling shows clear lime notes and can age for more than ten years.

Neither region has registered sub-regions, and place names on labels are mostly site names in common use. Winery names, brand names and GI names should not be confused.

## Varieties and raw material

Shiraz is Barossa's signature variety, occupying about half the Barossa Valley vineyard. Ripe blackberry, plum, liquorice and spice are common descriptors, with full body and ample tannin. Cabernet Sauvignon does better on cooler sites and is riper with softer tannin than in Coonawarra or Margaret River. Grenache shows ripe raspberry and pepper and is mostly blended.

Eden Valley centres on Riesling and Shiraz, with Chardonnay and Cabernet Sauvignon as well. The raw material is [grape](ref:grape). Old vines are an important Barossa resource, but age is not quality: vintage, viticulture and winemaking on the same site matter just as much.

## Winemaking

Reds are destemmed and [fermented](ref:fermentation) with temperature control, and maceration follows variety and style. Shiraz often sees longer maceration and pumpovers to extract colour and tannin, then ages in French or American [oak](ref:oak-ageing) with a winery-specific share of new barrels. Grenache has lighter tannin and is often co-fermented or separately aged with Shiraz and Mataro before blending.

Whites ferment cool. Riesling is mostly kept in stainless steel to preserve acidity and aroma, and some wines keep a little residual sugar; Chardonnay may see oak and lees stirring. Sparkling wine is also made in Barossa, though in limited volume.

## GI and labelling

Australian wine regions are identified by GIs administered by Wine Australia. For a single region, variety or vintage claim, at least 85% of the wine must come from the named region, variety or vintage; blends must be declared on the label, with the dominant source listed first. Barossa, Barossa Valley and Eden Valley are GIs at different levels and cover different areas.

The Barossa Old Vine Charter was introduced in 2009 by the Barossa Grape & Wine Association and sorts old vines into four tiers: Old Vine (35 years or more), Survivor Vine (70 years or more), Centenarian Vine (100 years or more) and Ancestor Vine (125 years or more). It is a voluntary industry label, not a statutory quality grade, and it does not change GI origin rules.

Australia has no national winery classification. Market standing comes from winery history, wine performance and media scores rather than an official grade.

## Labels and serving

The label should be checked for the GI name, variety, [vintage](ref:vintage), [alcohol](ref:abv), volume and bottler. A wine labelled as a [single variety](ref:single-varietal) must meet the 85% variety share; blends must list their varieties under the rules. Shiraz and GSM are served at about 16–18 degrees Celsius, Riesling at about 8–10 and Chardonnay at about 10–12.

Shiraz suits grilled meat, red meat and hard cheese; Grenache and GSM suit poultry, stews and spiced dishes; Riesling suits seafood, Asian dishes and spicy food. Refrigerate after opening and finish soon. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating Barossa as one single region. Barossa is a zone, and Barossa Valley and Eden Valley are two different regions.
Second, thinking Barossa grows only Shiraz. Shiraz leads, but Grenache, Cabernet Sauvignon, Riesling and Chardonnay all matter.
Third, treating old vines as a quality grade. The Old Vine Charter is voluntary and age does not mean a better wine.
Fourth, assuming all of Barossa is hot. The valley floor is warm, but Eden Valley and high sites are clearly cooler.
Fifth, taking a GI name for a brand. A GI is a geographic boundary; winery names and brands are separate.
