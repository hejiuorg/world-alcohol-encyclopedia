# Michel Rolland

Michel Rolland (1947–2026) was one of Bordeaux's best-known wine consultants and a central figure in the 1990s argument about the “flying winemaker”. His method links grape ripeness, fermentation and maceration, and oak ageing into a repeatable process, giving estates advice from harvest to final blend. Supporters say he raised stability; critics say he made red wines from different places taste increasingly alike.

## Life and education

Rolland was born in Libourne on 24 December 1947 and grew up at his family's Château Le Bon Pasteur in Pomerol. He studied at the Institut d'Œnologie de Bordeaux and graduated in 1972; he married his classmate Dany in 1970. In 1973 the couple founded Laboratoire Rolland in Libourne, first for analysis, later for consulting. In 1978 he and his brother Jean-Daniel took over the family estate; in 1986 he and Dany bought Château Fontenil in Fronsac.

In 2013 he sold a majority stake in the family's Bordeaux properties to Hong Kong's Goldin Group. In 2020 he sold a majority share in Rolland & Associés to his long-term partners Jean-Philippe Fort, Mikaël Laizet and Julien Viaud. He died of a heart attack on 20 March 2026, aged 78.

## The laboratory and the consulting business

Laboratoire Rolland sits in Pomerol and began as an analytical service: acidity, sugar, alcohol and fermentation status were the numbers estates needed every day. Rolland combined analysis with tasting on site and gradually turned the laboratory into a consulting house. By the 2020s his network covered more than 150 estates in 14 countries; at his peak he was arguably the most in-demand consultant in the world.

A consultant does not make the wine in place of the estate. He takes part in key decisions: when to pick, how to sort, how warm to ferment, how long to macerate, how much new oak to use, how to blend. The estate team does the daily work. Keeping this distinction clear is the only way to separate a “consulting style” from contract winemaking.

## Technical ideas: ripeness, maceration, oak and micro-oxygenation

Rolland starts from ripeness. He argues for picking when the grapes reach phenolic maturity, judging by analysis and by tasting in the vineyard; unripe tannin from early picking, he says, made many old Bordeaux taste dry. After harvest he stresses sorting and crushing, controls temperature and extraction during fermentation, and extends maceration when needed to gain fuller fruit and softer tannin.

For ageing he uses [oak](ref:oak-ageing), with attention to barrel origin, toast level and the proportion of new wood. Micro-oxygenation is the technique most often linked to him: after fermentation, oxygen is introduced slowly and in measured amounts to mimic gradual oxidation in barrel, affecting tannin polymerisation and colour stability. The technique is usually credited to Patrick Ducournau, who developed it in Madiran in 1991; Rolland promoted and scaled it in the 1990s rather than inventing it.

His real strength was often described as blending. Wine Spectator's obituary quotes Paul Hobbs saying that Rolland could work from dawn through lunch without getting up, tasting and re-tasting a blend; that skill is harder to copy than any single technique. Judgements about [fermentation](ref:fermentation) and the structure of [red wine](ref:red-wine) ultimately meet on the blending table.

## Clients and the “flying winemaker”

In 1987 Rolland's first client outside Bordeaux was Simi in California, where Zelma Long was winemaker and Paul Hobbs was her assistant. In 1988 he travelled to Argentina with Hobbs, met Nicolás Catena and others, and began working in South America. His client list later included Château Pontet-Canet, Château Figeac and Château La Conseillante in Bordeaux; Harlan, Staglin, Screaming Eagle and St. Supéry in the United States; Ornellaia and Monteverro in Italy; Casa Lapostolle in Chile; Marqués de Cáceres in Spain; and estates in Argentina, South Africa, India, Croatia and Armenia.

The term “flying winemaker” is often said to have become current because of him: one man carrying a technical language between hemispheres, taking Bordeaux method to Napa, Mendoza and Tuscany. Supporters see technical diffusion; critics see local difference being flattened.

## Estates and products

Rolland's own properties included Château Le Bon Pasteur in Pomerol, Château Fontenil in Fronsac, Château Rolland-Maillet in Saint-Émilion and Château Bertineau Saint-Vincent in Lalande-de-Pomerol. In Argentina he was involved with Clos de los Siete and owned Val de Flores and Bodega Rolland in the Uco Valley. These estates were both his testing ground and a showcase for his consulting method.

He is often linked in the press with the “garagiste” style of Saint-Émilion and Pomerol: small-lot, low-yield, highly extracted wines with a high proportion of new oak and rapidly rising prices. But garagiste was not a movement he founded. Its more representative initiator was Jean-Luc Thunevin of Château Valandraud. Rolland was one consulting representative of the style, not its sole author.

## Reputation and dispute

The case for him is concrete: he improved hygiene and stability, helped estates limit losses in bad vintages, and helped Bordeaux regain international attention after the low point of the 1970s. Wine Spectator's obituary says that at his height he was arguably the world's most in-demand consultant, especially for blending.

The criticism is equally concrete. Wine Spectator writes that detractors felt his style was “heavy-handed and homogenizing, minimizing terroir”; after 2010, estates including Château La Gaffelière and Château Pavie-Macquin ended their work with him and moved toward a more restrained, fresher style. In 2004 Jonathan Nossiter's documentary Mondovino made him a symbol of globalised style. Blaming him for all “Parkerisation” or “international style” is inaccurate, but he is the name most easily attached to it.

## Common misreadings

First, crediting him with inventing micro-oxygenation. He promoted and scaled it. Second, equating a consultant with a contract winemaker. The estate decides, and his clients were not uniform. Third, attributing every rich, concentrated wine to him. A consultant offers advice, the estate decides. Fourth, taking his portrayal in a documentary as the whole. Fifth, treating garagiste as his movement. It was a shared phenomenon among small estates in Saint-Émilion and Pomerol. Sixth, assuming his method was only “more”; he also stressed ripeness and balance.
