# New Year Drinking: Pepper-and-Cypress Wine and Tusu Wine

Drinking on the first day of the first lunar month has a long documentary history in China. The Jingchu Suishi Ji, written by Zong Lin of the Liang, describes the day: “old and young all set their caps and robes in order and pay their respects in turn. They offer pepper-and-cypress wine and drink peach broth. They offer tusu wine.” It also notes specifically that “the order of drinking begins with the youngest”. The passage brings three things together: the etiquette of greeting, the medicine of plague protection, and an order of serving that runs against age. Understanding New Year drinking means starting from all three.

## Where New Year drinking comes from

The old “beginning of the year” is not identical with the modern word “Spring Festival”. In classical texts, “yuandan”, “zhengdan” and “yuanti” usually mean the first day of the first lunar month; today's Spring Festival holiday and customs took shape gradually after the modern calendar reform. The change of name does not alter the core structure: the start of the year is the point where time begins again, and people mark it with ritual, food and gathering.

Wine has several roles at this point. It is an offering in ancestral rites and New Year greetings, a “medicine” against seasonal illness and a prayer for health, and a medium through which family members bless one another in order of seniority. Treat New Year wine only as “a good drink at the festival” and its oldest layer disappears.

[Time in a Cup](ref:time-in-a-cup) has already discussed the relation between wine and season. New Year drinking is the densest case in that line: one day, one family, one sequence, with wine tying time, body and relationship together.

## Pepper-and-cypress wine and tusu wine

Pepper-and-cypress wine usually refers to wine infused or prepared with pepper and cypress. Pepper is aromatic and pungent; cypress was often associated with evergreen life. The old explanations quoted in the Jingchu Suishi Ji link pepper and cypress with wishes such as “warding off a hundred illnesses” and “a light body and long life”. This language belongs to ancient health culture and belief, and should not be read directly as a modern medical claim.

Tusu wine is another seasonal medicinal wine. Its recipe was not uniform over the centuries; common accounts combine various herbs with wine and drink it at the New Year to ward off epidemics. Because ingredients and preparation changed with period and region, no single recipe should be treated as “the correct tusu wine”.

Pepper-and-cypress wine and tusu wine are not necessarily the same thing, nor did both always appear in every region. The texts place them side by side, showing that the New Year drinks list was composite: different wines carried different meanings, and a household could choose what was available.

## Why the youngest drink first

The Jingchu Suishi Ji says that “the order of drinking begins with the youngest”, and quotes Dong Xun's explanation: at the New Year children drink first because they “gain a year”, and wine is offered to congratulate them; the old “lose a year”, so they drink later. “Gaining” and “losing” a year here do not judge length of life but the direction of age: a child has added a year, while an elder has moved closer to the end of life.

This order is the reverse of the usual banquet rule that elders drink first, which makes it especially noteworthy. New Year drinking is not an ordinary rite of seniority but a symbolic order centred on welcoming the new: blessing comes first to those who have gained time, and only then to those from whom time is taken.

Readers today need not treat this as a rule to follow. It is better seen as a window: the ancients used the order of drinking to express an attitude toward time, and that attitude changes with family structure, ideas of longevity and medical conditions.

## Wine within the New Year ritual order

New Year drinking is embedded in a sequence of actions: arranging clothes and caps, greeting in turn, offering wine, eating, decorating doors, setting off firecrackers. Wine is not an isolated step but a shared action after the greetings. Who raises the cup first and who blesses whom correspond to the order of greeting.

Wine also served a function of “opening” the body. Ancient New Year foods often included pungent items such as the five-spice plate; pepper, cypress and tusu are also aromatic and warming. These choices belonged to seasonal ideas about “opening the five viscera” and “raising yang qi”. There is no need to copy them today, but one can understand that New Year food and drink were meant to adjust the body's state, not only to be abundant.

Wine also expressed the public nature of the family. New Year drinking was usually not solitary but involved old and young at home and visits between neighbours; wine connected the private household with outside relationships. This still resonates with today's Spring Festival gatherings.

## The New Year drinks list in the sources

The Jingchu Suishi Ji lists the order: pepper-and-cypress wine, peach broth, tusu wine, malt sugar sweets and the five-spice plate. Peach broth, sweets and the spice plate stand beside wine, showing that New Year food was a set, not a single drink.

The same passage quotes the Simin Yueling: “the day after the la sacrifice is called the small year... they offer pepper wine, beginning with the youngest.” This shows that the “youngest first” rule was not an isolated remark but had an earlier documentary source. It also reminds us that seasonal texts often quote one another; when reading a passage, distinguish the original text, the quotation and the commentary.

“Spring wine” in the Shijing belongs to another line: it links harvest, brewing and a birthday wish, as discussed in [Classic of Poetry](ref:shijing). The pepper, cypress and tusu of the New Year lean more toward plague protection and welcoming the new. Both show that wine in seasonal life was part of ritual language, not merely a consumer product.

## Later changes and regional differences

After the Tang and Song, tusu wine remained common in New Year poetry and local records, but its recipe, method and range changed continually. Some accounts emphasise the whole family drinking together, others a particular order; some places stress pepper and cypress, others tusu. Piecing records from different periods into one uniform “ancient system” creates a neatness that never existed.

Regional difference matters too. Jingchu was the scope of the Jingchu Suishi Ji and cannot automatically stand for all of China. The north, Jiangnan and Lingnan each had their own New Year foods, and the drinks and medicinal ingredients changed with them. Today's local customs are often the result of long movement and mixing.

Modern Spring Festival dinners and visits still keep the structure of “drinking together” and “blessing”, but the plague-protection colour has faded greatly. Wine now serves reunion, celebration and hospitality; this is a shift in the centre of gravity, not a simple disappearance of tradition.

## Common misreadings

First, treating tusu wine as a fixed recipe. Recipes differed across periods, and taking one book's ingredient list as a national standard ignores historical change.

Second, reading “beginning with the youngest” as an overthrow of seniority. It is a New Year symbol, not a rule for ordinary banquets.

Third, taking ancient claims about medicinal wine as modern medical conclusions. Talk of plague protection and health belonged to the knowledge of the time and cannot replace today's clinical evidence.

Fourth, treating the Jingchu Suishi Ji as a national survey of customs. It mainly records the Jingchu region; New Year drinking elsewhere needs other sources.

## How to understand this custom today

New Year drinking can be separated into three layers: marking time, ideas about the body, and family relationships. The first explains why people drank at the start of the year; the second why pungent ingredients such as pepper, cypress and tusu were used; the third why order and shared drinking mattered.

Anyone continuing the custom today can choose a low-alcohol drink suited to a meal and put the emphasis on reunion and blessing rather than reproducing a prescription. The ancient “plague protection” function should not be romanticised; the safety limits of drinking still apply.

For writers, the most valuable part of such a custom is the explanation, not the recipe. Saying which book a record comes from, which region it describes and how it changed is more useful than offering a seemingly authoritative New Year drinks list.
