# Gekkeikan Okura Sake Museum

The Gekkeikan Okura Sake Museum stands on Minamihama-cho in Fushimi, Kyoto, among the white-walled storehouses along the moat known as the Hori River. Gekkeikan converted a brewery built in 1909 into a museum, opening it in 1982 and admitting the general public regularly from 1987, the company's 350th anniversary. "Okura" in the name is the founding family's surname, not a modern sponsor. The museum tells more than one company's story: it explains how Fushimi became a sake town through water, river transport and urban demand.

## Place and history

Gekkeikan begins in 1637, when the first Okura Jiemon came from Kasagi, south of Kyoto, and opened a sake shop near the Fushimi horse market. The shop was called Kasagiya and its sake Tama no Izumi. Over generations the Okura family turned a household brewery into a modern sake company. The brand name Gekkeikan means laurel wreath, a sign of honour; what fixed the brand was not a legend but investment in bottling, machinery and advertising from the Meiji period onwards.

The building is itself an exhibit. It was a brewery erected by Okura Shuzo in 1909, with a timber frame, white plaster walls and a thick earthen storehouse structure typical of Fushimi's brewery quarter. It became a museum in 1982 and opened regularly to the public in 1987. On 29 February 2024 the entrance, tasting area and brand shop were renewed: tasting moved to a quieter spot and expanded to about 1.5 times its former area, while the entrance gained a reception desk and waiting space. The museum is not a frozen old house; its visitor route keeps being adjusted.

Water explains Fushimi. The district sits in the southern part of the Kyoto basin and draws soft groundwater with few minerals. Fermentation proceeds more gently, which favours supple, aromatic sake. The hard water of the Nada district, by contrast, tends to produce firmer, drier styles. The displays separate the chain of water, rice, koji, fermentation, pressing and bottling, but they do not claim that soft water alone makes good sake; rice variety, polishing ratio, yeast and the skill of the toji matter as much.

## Space and displays

The route is not long; most visitors finish in 40 to 60 minutes. Galleries hold the tools of Fushimi brewing: washing and soaking equipment, steamers, koji trays, wooden vats, presses and bottling gear, set alongside old photographs, signboards and film. The museum's collection of brewing tools is designated a tangible folk cultural property by the City of Kyoto, a status that says more about its weight than any social-media label. Labels are mainly in Japanese, but an in-museum guide can be read on a visitor's own phone using the museum's free Wi-Fi.

The museum adjoins the Uchikura brewery across a courtyard. That space is still in use, with a Tajima-style toji brewing in winter; at the height of the cold-season brewing, the smell of steamed rice and fermentation reaches the street. Visitors cannot enter the Uchikura brewery, but displays and film explain its process. It is more accurate to think of the site as a museum in a converted brewery plus a working brewery next door than as a theme park covering the whole plant.

The tasting area closes the route. The museum prepares about ten sake by season, and each visitor chooses three. Staff do not guide the tasting; the pace is your own. Next to it, the brand shop sells standard Gekkeikan products, museum-only sake and collaborations with Kyoto brands. Both the shop and the tasting area are cashless, and from July 2026 the museum stopped accepting cash altogether.

## How to visit

General admission is self-guided. The museum recommends booking online: reservations open at 09:30 two weeks before the visit date and close at 16:30 the day before, first come, first served. Without a booking you may still queue, but waits are likely when busy and entry can be refused once capacity is reached. A booked group is limited to 12 people; group visits of 13 or more are currently suspended.

Tickets are priced by age: 600 yen for visitors aged 20 and over, 100 yen for 13 to 19, and free for 12 and under. Visitors aged 13 and over receive a souvenir, and those aged 20 and over receive three tastings; the booking page and on-site notice are authoritative. The tasting is not unlimited: three pours from the seasonal list, enough to compare a ginjo, a junmai and an ordinary sake.

Separately, the museum runs a guided brewery tour with staff explanation, with its own times, capacity and booking. For displays and tasting alone, general admission is enough. Those who want the toji's and brewery's stories should book the guided option. Both use the same entrance but different assembly times, so read the ticket type carefully.

## Practical information

The address is 247 Minamihama-cho, Fushimi-ku, Kyoto 612-8660, Japan; telephone 075-623-2056. Hours are 09:30 to 16:30, with the last admission at 16:00. Closures are 13 to 16 August for Obon, 28 December to 4 January, and occasional temporary days, so check the homepage before travelling.

Tickets: 600 yen for visitors aged 20 and over (three tastings and a souvenir), 100 yen for 13 to 19 (souvenir), free for 12 and under. The museum has been entirely cashless since 1 July 2026 and accepts cards, transit cards and electronic payment only, so cash will not buy a ticket. A booked group is limited to 12 people and group visits of 13 or more are suspended; book online from 09:30 two weeks before the visit date until 16:30 the day before, with same-day places subject to availability.

Keihan railway is the easiest approach: about five minutes on foot from Chushojima, ten from Fushimi-Momoyama, ten from Kintetsu Momoyama-Goryo-mae and eighteen from JR Momoyama. There are about 22 ordinary parking spaces; coaches must book online and then confirm the bay by telephone. Food and drink are not allowed inside, and pets are excluded except assistance dogs. There is no live guide in the general route; explanatory panels and free Wi-Fi are provided, and drivers, visitors under 20, and pregnant or breastfeeding visitors may not taste.

## Etiquette and misconceptions

The first misconception is that this is a Gekkeikan factory tour. It is a museum in an old brewery, and the adjoining Uchikura brewery is not open; to see a full production line, look for brewery open days or book another brewery's tour. The second is that admission always includes sake: the 20-plus general ticket includes three tastings, but guided tours, shop purchases and limited bottles are charged separately.

Do not down a tasting in one go. Temperature, aroma and glass shape all affect judgement: smell first, take a small sip, and compare two before deciding what you like. Do not reach your cup under another person's bottle, and do not pour for anyone who is driving. The museum expressly excludes drivers of cars, motorcycles and bicycles, anyone under 20, and pregnant or breastfeeding visitors from tasting. This is a legal and safety boundary, not unfriendliness.

A third misconception concerns payment. From July 2026 the museum is entirely cashless; visitors carrying only cash will be stuck at the door. A fourth is to treat every Fushimi brewery as one attraction: Gekkeikan, Kizakura, Takara and others have separate facilities and rules, and the walks between them are not short.

## Neighbourhood and routes

The museum sits at the heart of the Fushimi brewery district, within walking distance of the canal boat pier, Teradaya (where Sakamoto Ryoma once stayed), Gokonomiya Shrine and the approach to Fushimi Inari. A sensible half day is the museum and tasting in the morning, a walk along the canal to Teradaya, then Keihan to Fushimi Inari; alternatively, visit Inari at dawn and the museum in the afternoon.

Kyoto Station is about seven kilometres away, and neither the train nor the drive is long. Fushimi and Uji can be combined, but both involve walking and standing, which is tiring in summer; a half day in each, on separate days, is more comfortable. Anyone travelling specifically for sake should keep Fushimi and Nada in Kobe on separate days: the water and the styles differ enough that doing both at once blurs the comparison.
