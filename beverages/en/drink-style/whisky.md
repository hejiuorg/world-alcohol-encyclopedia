# Whisky

Whisky (spelled whiskey in Ireland and the United States) is a spirit made from grain by mashing, fermentation and distillation, then aged in oak. Grain, still, cask and local climate shape the flavour; regional rules determine which names such as "Scotch" and "bourbon" may legally be used.

## Definition and boundaries

The process chain is grain mashing, fermentation, distillation and cask ageing. The sugary liquid after mashing is close in principle to brewers' wort, so whisky can be understood as fermented grain beer that is then distilled and aged; but [beer](ref:beer) itself is a fermented drink and is not distilled. Compared with brandy, whisky's raw material is grain rather than grapes or fruit.

"Whisky" has different legal definitions in different countries, usually sharing grain raw material, distillation, oak ageing and a minimum [alcohol by volume](ref:abv). A grain distillate that has not been aged in oak cannot be called whisky; some markets permit very short ageing, but the label must state it truthfully.

## History

Whisky appears in written records in 15th-century Scotland, building on monastic and local distillation traditions. In the 17th and 18th centuries, taxation and licensing split legal from illicit distillation and drove improvements in still design. In the 19th century, the continuous still and the railways brought blended whisky to a mass market.

Ireland, the United States and Japan developed their own traditions: Ireland emphasised triple distillation and pot stills, America built bourbon and rye around corn and rye, and Japan imported Scottish methods in the 20th century and developed precise blending. In the 21st century, single malt and cask experiments returned to the centre of the market.

## Main traditions

- **Scotch whisky**: must be distilled in Scotland and aged in oak for at least 3 years, with a bottling strength of at least 40% ABV. Single malt comes from one distillery; blended whisky combines malt and grain whiskies from several distilleries.
- **Irish whiskey**: protected by an Irish geographical indication, often triple-distilled or pot-still, usually softer in texture.
- **American whiskey**: bourbon uses corn as its main grain, at least 51%, and new charred oak; rye whiskey uses rye as its main grain. Neither has to be made in a particular state, but both have strict labelling rules.
- **Japanese whisky**: influenced by Scottish methods and known for precise blending; recent industry rules set clearer requirements for ingredients, distillation and ageing location.

## Raw materials and mashing

Whisky grains include barley, corn, rye and wheat. Barley is often malted first to produce enzymes that break down starch; peat-dried malt brings phenolic aroma, measured in ppm. Unmalted grain relies on the enzymes supplied by malt to complete saccharification.

Mashing mixes crushed grain with hot water so that starch converts to sugar. After lautering, the clear wort goes to fermentation. Yeast turns sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide over tens of hours and produces esters and higher alcohols, the by-products that form the base of later flavour. Fermentation time and temperature affect the fruitiness and grainy character of the body.

## Distillation and ageing

[Distillation](ref:distillation) happens in copper pot stills or continuous stills. Pot stills usually distil twice, and Scottish and Irish differences lie in the number of distillations and the equipment; continuous stills are more efficient and are common for grain whisky. The distiller separates heads, heart and tails by the cut, and the heart decides the final style.

The new spirit must go into [oak casks](ref:oak-ageing). Cask types include ex-bourbon barrels, sherry casks and new oak; cask size, toast level and warehouse temperature and humidity all change the rate of maturation. Oak brings colour, vanilla and spice and allows slow oxidation. A coastal or inland warehouse brings different saline and woody notes.

## Flavour and tasting

Whisky aromas fall into grain, peat, floral-fruit, spice and wood groups. Scottish single malt commonly shows peat smoke, sea salt and fruit; bourbon emphasises vanilla, caramel and corn sweetness; rye brings pepper and spice; Irish whiskey is usually softer. The palate runs from light to heavy, with strength and cask ageing deciding the structure.

In tasting, look at colour, then smell for grain and cask first, then peat or floral-fruit notes. On the palate, check how well alcohol and body are integrated and whether the finish is clean. A little room-temperature water softens the alcohol and opens the nose, but add it gradually. In comparative tasting, fix the region and style first, then judge completeness.

## Drinking and pairing

Neat reveals the full aroma; a little room-temperature water softens the alcohol and opens the nose, but add it gradually. Ice cools and dilutes, which suits stronger or heavier whiskies. A highball is another legitimate serve; neat is not the only "correct" method.

At the table, light single malt suits seafood and white meat, peated whiskies suit grilled and smoked food, and bourbon suits barbecue and dessert. High strength amplifies heat, so very spicy dishes are best avoided. A tulip or stemmed glass is preferable, gathering aroma without pushing the alcohol forward.

## Common misconceptions

First, that single malt is always better than blended whisky. They are different structures, and blending aims at consistency and balance.
Second, that older is always better. Over-long cask ageing brings too much wood and astringency, and base-spirit structure matters more.
Third, that darker means older. Colour is affected by cask type, char and caramel colour and cannot be used alone to judge age.
Fourth, taking "single malt" to mean "single cask". Single malt means malt whisky from one distillery and may still be blended from several casks.
Fifth, that whisky must come from Scotland. Ireland, the United States, Japan and other places all have mature legal traditions.
