# Peat

Peat is partially decomposed plant matter that accumulates slowly in cold, wet, oxygen-poor bogs. When burned as fuel it releases heavy smoke and phenolic compounds; the whisky industry uses peat smoke to kiln malt so that phenols attach to the grain and then pass through mashing, fermentation and distillation. Peatiness is an optional whisky style, not a legally required feature of Scotch whisky.![Peat image](/media/photos/term-peat-36a70265.jpg)

Figure: Peat. Image: David Stanley from Nanaimo, Canada, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons ([source page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Peat_(49302157252).jpg)).

## Definition and boundaries

Peat is neither coal nor mud. It consists mainly of mosses such as Sphagnum, sedges and reeds; it has less carbon than coal, burns cooler and produces more smoke. The degree of decomposition, water content and plant mix affect how it burns and the phenol mix in the smoke, so peat from different bogs and depths is not the same fuel. In whisky, "peatiness" refers to the smoke, herb, tar, ash and medicinal notes carried by phenols; it is not the same as all smokiness, and not the same as "Islay style". Peat is a fuel and a treatment; smoke is the sensory result.

## Etymology and origin

Peat comes from the Old English pēat, meaning plant material piled up in a bog; the peat bogs of Scotland and Ireland have long been a fuel source. During malting, wet malt is dried in a kiln, and if a peat fire is used the phenols in the smoke are adsorbed by the malt. Islay, with its peat resources and maritime conditions, became the emblem of heavily peated whisky, but peat is not unique to Islay; Speyside, Highland and island distilleries also make peated products.

## Mechanism and parameters

The key parameter is the phenol level of the malt, usually expressed in ppm (mg/kg of phenols, as gallic acid equivalents). Industry bands are roughly 1–10 ppm for light, 10–25 ppm for medium and above 30 ppm for heavy; this is a malt figure, not the smoke intensity in the bottle. During distillation, phenols differ in boiling point and solubility, and the cut points decide which reach the heart; cask type and time further change the texture of the smoke. Common phenols include guaiacol, 4-methylguaiacol and cresol. Malt phenol levels are often measured colorimetrically and expressed as gallic acid equivalents; the value reflects total adsorbed phenols rather than the concentration of any single phenol. New-make phenol levels are usually lower than the malt figure because mashing, fermentation and distillation remove some, and later cuts bring more smoke and bitterness.

## Practical use in the industry

Distilleries design style by controlling peat dose, kilning time and malt source. Heavily peated whisky is common on Islay, with names such as Ardbeg, Lagavulin and Laphroaig; but Bunnahabhain is mainly low-peat or unpeated, and Bruichladdich also makes the unpeated Classic Laddie. Speyside and Highland distilleries also produce peated spirit. Ardbeg 10 and Lagavulin 16 are commonly treated as references for heavily peated Islay style, but their recipes and cut points differ, so the region alone cannot tell you the strength. [The Macallan](ref:macallan) is known for its sherry-cask style and is not usually heavily peated; [Glenfiddich 18](ref:glenfiddich) also emphasises fruit and cask expression, making it a useful non-heavily-peated contrast.

## Confusable terms

First, peat versus smoke. Peat is the fuel and process source; smoke is the sensory result, and smoke can also come from cask charring or other fuels. Second, ppm versus in-bottle intensity. Ppm describes the malt's phenol level, while in-bottle smoke also depends on distillation cuts, maturation and dilution. Third, Islay versus heavy peat. Islay is a region, not a guarantee that every bottle is heavily peated; heavy peat also exists elsewhere. Fourth, peat versus Sphagnum moss. Sphagnum is one of the plants that forms peat, while peat is the whole accumulated layer; treating the two as synonyms ignores the mixed origin of peat.

## Common misuses and disputes

First, assuming every [whisky](ref:whisky) is peated; in fact most Scotch whisky is not heavily peated. Second, treating ppm as a direct palate intensity. Third, equating peat with "disinfectant"; different phenols give herbal, tar, ash and maritime notes. Fourth, assuming heavier peat means higher quality. Fifth, ignoring how peat balances against sweetness, acidity, fruit and wood. There is also dispute over whether ppm labelling should use a single measurement basis, since data from different laboratories and batches are not always comparable. Another misuse is to reduce peat to "iodine" or "disinfectant"; these are only descriptions of some phenols and maritime associations and do not cover tar, ash, herb and sweet smoke.

## Typical figures and examples

Malt phenol levels are commonly given in ppm; light is about 1–10 ppm, medium about 10–25 ppm, and heavy above 30 ppm. Bruichladdich's Octomore 13.4 is officially listed at 137.3 ppm, an ultra-heavy level; such numbers describe malt treatment intensity, not in-bottle smoke concentration. Islay is not uniformly heavily peated: most Bunnahabhain products are low-peat or unpeated, and Bruichladdich also has an unpeated range. Legally, the Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 set the definition and production requirements for Scotch whisky, but peat is not a mandatory condition.

## Recording and describing

When recording a peated whisky, note at least the malt phenol level if stated, the peat source, the distillery and the cask type; in tasting, separate smoke, herb, tar, ash and maritime notes rather than writing only "peat". When buying, ppm is a strength clue but cannot replace tasting; the same ppm can give completely different styles at different distilleries. A "peated" label usually means peat-kilned malt, without stating a specific ppm. If a distillery gives a phenol value, record whether it refers to malt or new-make; the two are not directly interchangeable.
