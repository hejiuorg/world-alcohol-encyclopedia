# The World Atlas of Wine

The World Atlas of Wine is a reference organised around geography: it uses terrain, climate, soil and administrative boundaries to explain why wine in a place tastes the way it does. “Atlas” in the title is not decoration but method: look at the place first, then discuss style. For readers in translation, the book is also a regional encyclopaedia, and the edition and the rendering of place names matter as much as the text.![The World Atlas of Wine thematic illustration](/media/photos/theme-wine-spirits.jpg)

## Authors and background

Hugh Johnson (born 1939) was born in London and long served as the wine correspondent of The Sunday Times. From 1977 he edited the annually updated Pocket Wine Book, and in 1989 he published The Story of Wine.

Jancis Robinson (born 1950) passed the Master of Wine examination in 1984 and has long written a wine column for the Financial Times. She edited the first edition of The Oxford Companion to Wine in 1994, and in 2012 co-authored Wine Grapes with Julia Harding and Jose Vouillamoz. Her involvement gives the atlas greater rigour on varieties, regulation and technical detail.

The collaboration rests on a division of labour: Johnson supplies historical narrative and overall description, Robinson handles varieties, rules and data updates, and map editors and regional authors contribute as well. The first edition appeared in 1971 from Mitchell Beazley in London; later editions followed roughly every six years, with a sixth edition in 2007, a seventh in 2013 and an eighth in 2019.

## Editions and structure

The book has three layers. The first is a general section on vinification, tasting, climate and soil. The second treats countries one by one, summarising wine history, the regulatory system and the main regions. The third consists of regional entries, each giving maps, grape varieties, legal rules, style descriptions and representative estates.

The maps are not a single uniform base but are scaled by importance: large regions get overview maps, while core villages get enlarged parcel maps. As a result, the classic European regions are mapped in far more detail than newer ones, whose entries rely more on prose. A reader who only skims the maps may overestimate the weight of the areas shown at large scale.

Edition differences matter. New editions redraw boundaries, add regions and update data; old editions preserve the understanding of a region at the time, including places later merged or renamed. Any figure for area, output or classification should carry the edition and year, or old information will be mistaken for current conditions.

## Core content and arguments

The book's implicit argument is that wine style results from three layers of conditions. Natural conditions include latitude, altitude, aspect, soil drainage and the moderating effect of rivers. Regulation sets permitted varieties, minimum alcohol, maximum yields and ageing requirements. Human choices appear in variety selection, planting density, pruning and winemaking.

Bordeaux is the model case: gravel soils on the left bank drain well and suit the late-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon, while clay and limestone on the right bank favour Merlot and Cabernet Franc. Burgundy pushes the idea of the site to its limit, so that neighbouring plots on one slope can fall into different grades because of gradient, soil depth and orientation. Champagne depends on chalk and a cool climate, while the steep Mosel slopes rely on heat reflected from the river and on slate that stores warmth.

The book also reminds readers that appellation is not quality. The legal system answers the question “may this wine carry this name?”, not “is this bottle good?”. Within one appellation, differences between estates and vintages can be larger than the average difference between two neighbouring appellations.

## Wine-cultural value: regions on the map

Culturally, the atlas's greatest contribution is to turn terroir from a slogan into identifiable terrain. Readers can see how a river bends, which way a slope faces and what separates one village from the next, so that “here is not the same as there” stops being an empty phrase.

In terms of chapters, the French section covers Bordeaux, Burgundy, Champagne, the Loire, the Rhone and Alsace; Italy covers Piedmont and Tuscany; Spain covers Rioja and Sherry; Germany covers the Mosel and the Rhine; and country sections follow for the United States, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and South America. Institutions such as the 1855 Bordeaux classification, the Burgundy grand cru system and the Champagne village hierarchy are explained in the relevant entries.

## Limits and controversies

First, coverage is broad but shallow. A single region usually receives a few pages, and detail still requires monographs, regional association material and the regulations themselves. Second, maps are simplified models with limited precision and cannot be used as legal documents or as evidence of parcel ownership. Third, estate recommendations carry author judgement and are shaped by samples and markets; they should not be read as a ranking.

Fourth, the geographical-determinist narrative underplays other variables. Climate change is shifting ripeness and suitable altitude, while winemaking technique, clonal selection and market preference also shape style; a map alone cannot explain why one bottle succeeds. Fifth, editions lag behind regulatory change, so developments after publication need separate verification. Sixth, Chinese editions vary in the rendering of regions and estates, which makes searching in the original language error-prone.

## How to read it today

Beginners can read the general chapters and country overviews first, building a framework of climate, variety and regulation, then study one or two familiar regions in detail. When reading maps, keep the original place names in view and note village and appellation names rather than relying only on translated forms.

More advanced readers can compare editions to watch boundaries, classifications and recommendation lists change; this reveals how industry understanding shifts better than any single edition. For current facts, use regional association websites, the regulations themselves and OIV statistics, treating the atlas as an explanatory framework rather than a data source.
