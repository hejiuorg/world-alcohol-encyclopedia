# Climbing High: Du Fu and a Cup of Rough Wine at Kuizhou

Climbing High has only eight lines, yet it is one of Du Fu's heaviest autumn poems. There is no banquet, no urging of guests, and the cup is never raised; wine appears only in the last line, and it appears as something stopped. For that reason the wine here is not a social medium but a daily object taken away, and what remains is its absence.

## The poem and its setting

The poem was written in the autumn of 767 at Kuizhou, in what is now the Chongqing area around Fengjie. Du Fu had already left his Thatched Cottage in Chengdu and was drifting along the Yangtze, often ill and separated from family and friends. He climbed to a high place and saw the sharp wind, the high sky, the cries of apes, clear islets, white sand and endless falling leaves.

Climbing high was an old custom of the Double Ninth festival, but the title does not name the festival. Older commentaries often connect this climb with the ninth day of the ninth month, while others read it simply as an autumn ascent. What is certain is the season and the place: autumn, the river, a height. The exact day is not documented well enough to fix.

## Text

> The wind is sharp, the sky is high, the apes wail in sorrow;
> the islet is clear, the sand white, birds wheel and return.
> Endless falling leaves rustle down;
> the inexhaustible long river rolls in.
> Ten thousand miles of sad autumn, always a guest;
> a hundred years of much illness, alone I climb the terrace.
> Hardship and bitter regret thicken the frost on my temples;
> worn out, I have just stopped the cup of rough wine.

This is the received text. Just stopped is sometimes written with a different character meaning new pavilion, and clear islet, white sand also appears as blue islet, white sand; these variants do not change the overall sense, but they affect how the last line is read, as discussed below.

## The first four lines: scenery opens outward

The first couplet begins with the wind and the sky close at hand. The sharp wind is touch, the high sky is sight, and then the wailing of apes adds sound. The Three Gorges had many apes, and their cries are often linked in classical poetry to travel and homesickness. The next line brings the camera back to the river: the islet is cold and clear, the sandbank white, and birds circle low. In fourteen characters, wind, sky, apes, islet, sand and birds appear in turn, with hardly an explicit subject, and yet the reader is placed inside a complete autumn scene.

The second couplet pushes the view to its limit. “Endless falling leaves rustle down” is a look upward and far away, leaves without a boundary; “the inexhaustible long river rolls in” is a look down and into movement, water without an end. The first couplet dealt with particular things; this one becomes scale: leaves without limit, a river without end. The infinity of space and the continuity of time press in together.

The parallelism is exact: sharp wind against clear islet, high sky against white sand, the apes' wailing against the birds' return; endless against inexhaustible, falling leaves against the long river, rustling down against rolling in. It does not arrange scenery into two columns; it makes sound, colour and movement interlock.

## The last four lines: from scenery to the self

The third couplet turns to the poet. “Ten thousand miles of sad autumn, always a guest” places distance and seasonal sorrow together: far from home, in autumn, and always a guest, not merely once on a journey. “A hundred years of much illness, alone I climb the terrace” places the length of a life beside the state of a body: in old age, often ill, climbing alone. The endless and inexhaustible of the previous couplet now find their point: the larger the scenery, the more solitary the person.

The final couplet closes tighter. Hardship and bitter regret thicken the frost on my temples speaks of a lifetime of difficulty and regret turning both temples white as frost; worn out, I have just stopped the cup of rough wine speaks of stopping the cup in a state of poverty and disappointment. The previous line is still about hair; the next suddenly falls to a concrete action: not drinking.

## Why wine appears only as a stopping

The poem contains the word wine once, in the last line, as rough wine. Rough wine is wine not yet clarified, as opposed to clear wine; in the Tang it was an everyday low-strength grain drink. It was neither rare nor expensive, and precisely because it was everyday, stopping it feels heavy.

The two words just stopped matter. Just means that he had not always abstained, but had stopped recently; stopped means an interruption that is passive and forced. Why did he stop? Older commentaries mostly explain it as giving up wine because of illness, while others read it as having no wine because of poverty, or both. The poem gives no medical explanation, and the reader should not diagnose the poet. What is certain is that wine here is not a pleasure but a habit taken away.

This is the exact opposite of Du Fu's poem about receiving a guest. In [A Guest Arrives](ref:ke-zhi), the household is poor and can offer only old unfiltered wine, but the wine is still on the table and the host still urges the guest to drink; in Climbing High even the cup is set down. The same poet: wine is sometimes the sincerity of hospitality, sometimes a former life that body and circumstance no longer allow. For the craft background of Tang grain wine, see [Yellow Wine](ref:yellow-wine); but rough wine should not be equated directly with any finished drink of today.

## Form and reputation

Climbing High is a regulated verse poem in seven-character lines, eight lines in four couplets, with the two middle couplets parallel. It is often held up as a model of the form, and the Ming critic Hu Yinglin gave it very high praise in his Shisou, from which the later phrase the finest seven-character regulated verse in history derives. Such judgements belong to a tradition of literary criticism rather than a measurable title, but they show how persistently later readers have studied the poem's metre, syntax and arrangement of scene and self.

There is also a formal peculiarity: all eight lines tend toward parallelism, including the opening and closing couplets. The strict form does not deaden the poem; it makes every line carry two tasks at once, describing scenery and a person, the present moment and a whole life.

## Common misreadings

First, taking endless falling leaves as pure landscape. It is also a measure of time, and together with the inexhaustible river it forms a background that cannot be exhausted.

Second, taking a hundred years as the exact number one hundred. Here it means a lifetime, one's later years, a common expression in classical poetry.

Third, taking I have just stopped the cup as a declaration of abstinence. It records a situation, not health advice, and it does not state the reason for stopping.

Fourth, equating climbing high directly with the Double Ninth festival. Climbing was a Double Ninth custom, but the title does not name the festival, and the dating and the exact day remain open to discussion.

Fifth, reading the poem as pure pessimism. The scenery of the first four lines has power, and the sorrow of the last four has power too; the poet offers no solution, but he states his situation clearly, and that clarity is itself a way of bearing it.
