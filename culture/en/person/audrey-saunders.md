# Audrey Saunders

Audrey Saunders is an American bartender and bar owner who opened Pegu Club in SoHo, New York, in August 2005. Her influence rests not on a single fashionable drink but on a set of practices that can be trained and checked: fresh juice, house-made syrups, measured pours and timed shaking, and repeated calibration of temperature and dilution. She treated the bar as a classroom and turned the cocktail from a matter of feel into a sequence that could be written down and repeated.

## Life

No reliable public source exists for her birth year, so the field is left empty here rather than filled from rumour. She worked for years in New York restaurants and bars, including a post at Bemelmans Bar in the Carlyle Hotel, and later moved into restaurant beverage programmes; around 2000 she was head bartender at Beacon in Manhattan. It was at Beacon that she created the Gin-Gin Mule, the drink that became her signature, and helped put gin back at the centre of the American bar at a time when vodka dominated almost every list.

In August 2005 she opened Pegu Club, named after the British colonial-era club that once existed in Rangoon; the recipe for its namesake cocktail appears in the Savoy Cocktail Book of the 1930s. According to trade reporting, the bar announced its permanent closure in April 2020 and never held the fifteenth-anniversary party planned for 29 August that year.

## Timeline

- Around 2000: head bartender at Beacon; creates the Gin-Gin Mule.

## Working Relationship with the Drinks Trade

Saunders's role was head bartender and bar partner. The actual work was designing lists, setting service standards, training staff and controlling costs. The first thing she did at Pegu Club was not to invent drinks but to tighten the boundaries of production: which gin, how much juice, what size of ice, how long to shake, at what temperature the drink goes into which glass, all written as steps that could be carried out.

The list also made a structural choice: more than twenty gins and only four vodkas. In a period when vodka ruled American bars, that was a public position, since the bar took on the inventory and the teaching cost of a spirit widely treated as old-fashioned. The gin revival became a major strand of the 2000s cocktail movement, and Pegu Club is one of the names most often cited in that story.

She also took part in industry discussion and teaching, explaining her method to other bartenders. Her influence therefore spread through people: many who later ran well-known bars trained inside her system, or learned from her list how to treat a classic.

## Signature Drinks and the List

The Gin-Gin Mule, created around 2000 at Beacon, is her best-known drink. It joins the mint, lime and sugar of the mojito to the ginger of the Moscow Mule, with gin as the base. The structure is simple, but the handling of ginger, the proportion of lime and the dilution from ice decide whether it works. The double "gin" in the name refers to gin and to ginger.

The Old Cuban is another frequently cited creation: aged rum, lime, mint and bitters, topped with champagne. It lifts a tropical sour frame into a celebratory setting, and shows that she was not opposed to building a drink with sparkling wine at the top.

Her Pegu Club Cocktail is a restatement of a classic: gin, orange liqueur, lime and two kinds of bitters, dry and full of citrus oil, suited to being the first drink of an evening. She also promoted the Fitty-Fitty Martini, roughly equal parts gin and dry vermouth, which uses more vermouth to pull the [dry martini](ref:dry-martini) back from "chilled gin" into a drink whose proportions can be argued about.

## Method and Training

Her method can be reduced to four points. First, measure: use a jigger rather than an eye, so the same drink comes out the same way across shifts and across hands. Second, freshness: squeeze lime to order, make ginger juice to order, cook the syrup in house, and do not substitute bottled products. Third, temperature and dilution: use large, dry ice, and shake or stir for the time the recipe specifies, so that dilution is part of the design rather than an accident. Fourth, repetition: an apprentice makes the same drink until the movements are stable, then learns the next one.

None of that is exotic; what is hard is doing it at full service. Her bar therefore worked more like a kitchen, with recipe cards, station assignments and checks on the finished drink. The later "procedural" turn in craft cocktail bars grew largely out of practice of this kind.

## Reputation and Disputes

The usual assessment places her at the centre of the New York cocktail revival and calls Pegu Club a school for bartenders. That has a factual basis: the list, the training and the movement of people did shape a generation behind the bar.

There are clear limits as well. The first is economic. A bar with high labour, expensive ingredients and low turnover depends on high prices and steady traffic; in 2020 the lease and the collapse of revenue during the pandemic forced Pegu Club to close permanently, and the case is now cited as an example of how fragile the craft-bar model is (The Spirits Business, 2020). The second is narrative. Crediting a movement that involved bars, media, distributors and customers to a few names hides a great deal of anonymous work; Saunders herself has repeatedly stressed the role of partners and teams. The third is recipe drift. The Gin-Gin Mule has travelled very widely, and many bars use ginger beer instead of fresh ginger juice, so the taste changes; the wider it spreads, the harder the original becomes to check.

A further point is discussed less often. The "professionalism" of such bars can itself create a threshold. Customers need to know how to order and how to ask in order not to look ignorant, and for people outside that language the bar may be no friendlier than an ordinary one. This is a long-standing criticism of the craft cocktail movement, and it is also something she had to weigh when designing service.

## Common Misreadings

First, treating Pegu Club as her work alone. The bar had partners, a team and investors, and the list was collective work.

Second, taking the Gin-Gin Mule for a simple mix of ginger ale and gin. Its key points are the handling of fresh ginger, the proportion of lime and the dilution from ice.

Third, filling in her birth year with an arbitrary number. Where no reliable source exists, the project leaves the field empty.

Fourth, crediting the cocktail revival of the 2000s to one person. It was pushed by bars, media, supply chains and customers together.

Fifth, reading "reviving the classics" as copying old recipes. Her Pegu Club Cocktail and her martini both adjust proportions; what is revived is the structure, not the numbers.
