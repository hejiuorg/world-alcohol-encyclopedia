# Hongqu

Hongqu is a purple-red qu made by steaming rice, inoculating it with Monascus and culturing it. It is both a traditional saccharifying starter and a food colouring material. Within [huangjiu](ref:yellow-wine) it defines the red-qu huangjiu category, and it has separate uses in red-qu paste, red fermented bean curd and pigment production.

## Definition and boundaries

The basic method is to steam rice, inoculate it with Monascus, culture it under controlled temperature and humidity so that mycelium grows and pigments form, and then dry it for storage. The finished product ranges from purple-red to dark red, and the grains may keep their rice shape inside. Hongqu is a qu, not a pigment: the colour comes from microbial culture, not from an added dye.

It differs from wheat qu and jiuyao. Wheat qu cultures moulds on wheat, jiuyao contains Rhizopus and yeasts, and hongqu is dominated by Monascus. Their saccharifying power, fermenting power and aroma contribution differ, so they cannot simply be swapped. Temperature, humidity and turning timing in [qu making](ref:qu-making) also determine hongqu quality. Hongqu is also not only one physical form: powder, grains and red-qu paste are different products with different uses and specifications.

## Names and origin

The Chinese name points to colour and starter type: qu is the saccharifying carrier, and hong means red. English often renders it as red yeast rice or red koji. Red yeast rice stresses rice cultured with Monascus; red koji stresses the qu itself. Other European languages use calques such as red yeast rice, but the scientific name remains Monascus.

The naming difference creates a difference in understanding. In Europe and North America, red yeast rice often means a food supplement and suggests a cholesterol-lowering function; in China, hongqu more often means a brewing and food material. Mixing the two contexts turns a raw material into a health product. This page deals with hongqu in drinks and food processing and offers no health advice.

## Production and the organism

Monascus is a filamentous fungus, with Monascus purpureus among the common species. Steamed rice serves as the substrate; mycelium grows on and inside the grains, secretes enzymes that break starch into sugar, and produces pigments. Temperature, humidity, aeration and turning decide mycelial growth and pigment accumulation; poor control brings contamination, uneven colour or off-flavours.

Monascus produces more than pigments. Research and application also examine monacolins and other secondary metabolites, which is why hongqu production needs strain management and process control. Colour depth cannot be equated directly with quality or efficacy; strain, substrate and culture conditions all change the pigment profile.

## Pigments, flavour and metabolites

Monascus pigments include red, orange and yellow fractions, often described as red, orange and yellow Monascus pigments. Pigment content depends on strain, substrate and culture conditions, so colour depth varies. The reddish-brown colour of red-qu huangjiu comes mainly from hongqu itself, not from caramel colour or an added dye; this is a key visible difference from some other huangjiu.

In flavour, hongqu fermentation brings a sweet-leaning, full palate and a light qu aroma, while also affecting acidity and bitterness. Sugar type and alcohol content vary widely between products and should follow the label. Monascus pigments are used as colorants in the food industry, and red-qu paste is used for curing, braising and colouring in Fujian cuisine; these uses share the raw material with brewing but have different process goals.

## Use in the drinks industry

Red-qu huangjiu uses glutinous rice as its main material, with hongqu handling saccharification and part of fermentation. The basic flow matches huangjiu generally: steeping, steaming, cooling by spreading or rinsing, qu mixing, jar fermentation, pressing, clarification, heating and ageing. The amount of hongqu, fermentation temperature and time affect colour depth and palate. Red-qu wine can be understood as a huangjiu type in which hongqu is the main saccharifying starter; the exact category still depends on the product standard and label.

GB/T 13662-2018, Huangjiu, was published on 2018-09-17 and implemented on 2019-04-01, and it sets terminology, classification, requirements and test methods. Red-qu huangjiu is one category distinguished by starter type and process; sugar type is determined by the total sugar index, not by region or colour. Treating hongqu as a synonym for sweet would misread the label.

## Huangjiu classification and the Fujian region

Fujian is the core region for red-qu huangjiu. Fujian Laojiu from the Fuzhou area and Chen'gangjiu from Longyan belong to this type, and Gutian in Fujian is known for hongqu production. Sugar type and strength vary widely between products and should follow the label. Compared with the rice and wheat-qu huangjiu of [Shaoxing](ref:shaoxing) and the millet jiaomi huangjiu of [Jimo](ref:jimo), the most visible difference in the red-qu route is colour and starter type.

Fujian red-qu huangjiu is often described as sweet and full, but not every product is sweet. Under GB/T 13662-2018, sugar type is set by the total sugar index; the same region can produce dry, semi-dry, medium-sweet and sweet products. Region is a stylistic clue, not a legal classification.

## Terms easily confused

The first group is hongqu, wheat qu and jiuyao. Wheat qu uses wheat as its carrier, jiuyao contains Rhizopus and yeasts, and hongqu is dominated by Monascus; their microbial makeup and enzymes differ, so they cannot be swapped simply because all are qu. The second pair is hongqu and red yeast rice. Hongqu is the process product and starter type; red yeast rice is a specific grain form, and hongqu can also be ground or made into red-qu paste.

The third pair is red-qu huangjiu and Shaoxing huangjiu. The first is defined by hongqu, the second by wheat qu and glutinous rice; their colour, aroma and palate routes differ, but both are huangjiu. The fourth pair is hongqu and red-qu paste. Red-qu paste is a seasoning made from hongqu and distillers' grains, not the brewing starter; treating the paste as hongqu confuses raw material with finished product. [GB/T 13662-2018 Huangjiu](ref:gbt-13662-2018) is the standard basis for huangjiu classification, but it does not classify non-beverage products such as red-qu paste.

## The safety dispute: citrinin

Monascus fermentation can produce citrinin, a fungal metabolite that needs to be controlled and is associated with kidney toxicity. Production controls the risk through strain selection, culture conditions and testing; regulators set limits and labelling requirements for related foods. EU Regulation 2023/915 sets a citrinin limit of 100 micrograms per kilogram (100 µg/kg) for food supplements based on rice fermented with Monascus purpureus; that limit applies to supplements, not to every hongqu food.

Hongqu also contains monacolins, and monacolin K is structurally identical to lovastatin. EFSA published an opinion in 2018 on the safety of monacolins in red yeast rice, and the conclusions fed European regulatory discussion of red yeast rice supplements. For consumers, the most important practice is to read the label and producer information and not to treat hongqu as a health promise; functional claims for hongqu products should follow their approved category and instructions, and this beverage page offers no health advice.

## Common misuses and disputes

First, treating hongqu as an artificial colorant. Hongqu is a cultured qu, and the pigments are produced by Monascus. Second, assuming red-qu wine is wine with added colour; the colour of red-qu huangjiu comes from hongqu itself. Third, assuming hongqu is always healthier; hongqu is a material and process concept, not a guarantee of benefit. Fourth, assuming all red-qu huangjiu is sweet; sugar type depends on the total sugar index and the label. Fifth, assuming redder is better; colour depends on strain, dosage and fermentation conditions and cannot stand alone as a quality conclusion.

Disputes centre on citrinin and functional claims. Supporters stress traditional craft and flavour value; critics worry about toxin risk in fermentation and health claims for supplements. The regulatory route is to separate brewing material, food colourant and dietary supplement. Another dispute is the name: red yeast rice in English leans toward supplements, while hongqu in Chinese leans toward a starter type, so translation without context easily misleads.

## Typical figures and examples

Verifiable facts and figures: GB/T 13662-2018 was published on 2018-09-17 and implemented on 2019-04-01; EU Regulation 2023/915 sets a citrinin limit of 100 micrograms per kilogram (100 µg/kg) for food supplements based on rice fermented with Monascus purpureus; Monascus purpureus is a common species; Monascus pigments include red, orange and yellow fractions; Fujian Laojiu, Chen'gangjiu and Gutian hongqu are common regional examples.

Concrete examples: a Fujian red-qu huangjiu may be reddish-brown and sweet-leaning, but its label sugar type is still set by total sugar; a red yeast rice supplement may declare monacolin K content, which does not equal the specification of a brewing material; red-qu paste used to colour Fujian dishes is a seasoning, not a brewing qu. These examples show that hongqu has different identities in different products.
