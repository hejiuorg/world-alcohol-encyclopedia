# Full-moon feast

The full-moon feast is a banquet held when a baby reaches one month old. It is at once an announcement, a thank-you, a naming and a social occasion: relatives and friends learn that a new member has arrived, helpers are thanked, the child receives a public name, and gift exchange finds an occasion. Wine is not the centre of the meal, but it is often the gesture that turns private joy into a public event.

Three dates need separating: the full moon, the hundredth day and the first birthday. The full-moon feast belongs to the first month, the hundred-day feast to the hundredth day, and the “grabbing” ceremony to the first birthday. Merging them into one script is a common modern error.

## Time and place

“Full moon” means one month after birth, but counting differs: some families follow the lunar month, some count thirty days, and some move the date forward or back for the baby's health. Scale varies from a few tables at home to dozens in a hotel.

Customs are widespread among Han communities but differ in detail. In parts of the north, head-shaving is important, and the first hair may be kept or made into a brush. In Shanxi and Shaanxi, the mother's family may bring dough figures, clothes and a longevity lock. In Jiangnan, red eggs are dyed and given away, signalling good news and completeness. In parts of Guangdong, the feast is tied to ginger vinegar and pig's trotters, with the focus on the mother's recovery. In southern Fujian and Taiwan, savoury rice and sesame-oil chicken are common. Urban and rural, coastal and inland practices differ, and a single national script would misrepresent them.

## Sequence and vessels

A Song dynasty note gives a relatively full urban record. Dongjing Meng Hua Lu, in its section on raising children, describes the “baby-washing gathering” at one month in Bianjing: relatives gather, warm scented water is poured into a basin with fruit, coloured coins, spring onions and garlic, coloured silk is wrapped around the basin, a hairpin stirs the water, and guests throw coins in. The sequence combines washing, blessing and gift-giving, showing that the full-moon event was already a public social occasion in the Song.

In later practice, common steps include choosing a date, sending invitations, arranging the venue, bringing the baby out, shaving or cutting a lock of hair, choosing or announcing a name, receiving gifts, returning gifts and toasting at the table. Some places add godparent ceremonies, longevity locks and studio photographs. Red eggs, locks, hair brushes and gift boxes join wine as material proof of the happy event.

Wine usually appears in two places: toasts at the banquet and gift bottles brought by guests. Parents or grandparents lead the toasts toward elders and visitors; gift wine is placed on the table as a symbol of added joy. The choice follows family and local habit: [yellow wine](ref:yellow-wine) and rice wine are mild and suit tables with a new mother and elderly guests; [baijiu](ref:baijiu) is more common at northern banquets; beer, juice and tea are increasingly present.

## Symbolic meaning

First, announcement. The table is a public setting, and raising a cup passes the news to everyone present. Second, thanks. Parents toast elders, relatives and helpers. Third, naming. The name is spoken publicly, giving the child a social identity. Fourth, protection. Locks, red eggs and head-shaving all carry the idea of keeping the child safe, and wine gathers these wishes around one table.

Fifth, sociability. The feast is part of gift exchange, and cash gifts, return gifts and table arrangement all maintain relationships. Sixth, gender and family. In some places the scale of the feast traditionally differed for boys and girls; this is often avoided in conversation today, but it is a fact that cannot be skipped when understanding older customs.

## Historical change

Ancient ritual books contain rules for cutting a baby's hair and naming, for example the Liji's Nei Ze, which says that at the end of the third month a day is chosen to cut the hair. This is not the same as the later full-moon feast: the timing, participants and social setting differ. The ancient three-month rite belonged to elite families, while the later banquet is tied to kin and neighbourhood networks.

As a folk banquet, the full-moon feast is better documented in late imperial gazetteers than in pre-Qin ritual. The Song “baby-washing gathering” is the earliest relatively full urban record, while Ming and Qing gazetteers fix head-shaving, red eggs and banquets as standard steps. Calling it “ancient” is therefore imprecise. A safer statement is that celebration of a birth is old, while the specific full-moon banquet took shape in late imperial local society.

Contemporary change comes from three directions: hotels and wedding companies package the banquet, photography and gifts; social media extend the announcement beyond the table; and younger families deliberately reduce the scale or declare “no cash gifts”. The life-rite wine discussed in [wine and festivals](ref:festivals) is being redesigned at exactly this point.

## Contemporary disputes

First, the mother and breastfeeding. Alcohol passes into breast milk and may inhibit the milk-ejection reflex; the WHO position is that the safest choice during breastfeeding is not to drink. A new mother should not be pressed to drink, and “a little rice wine to boost milk” should not be treated as sound advice. Second, infant safety. Crowded, noisy rooms and smoking guests can overstimulate or infect a baby; a quiet room, limited visits and no kissing or close breathing are sensible.

Third, pressure to drink. A happy occasion should not turn “no” into rudeness. Fourth, competition over cash gifts. Turning a blessing into a price contest weakens the rite and burdens young parents. Fifth, commercialisation. Photography packages, venue styling and gift boxes form a consumption chain that pushes families to spend on appearances.

A sensible approach is to announce “tea instead of wine”, provide alcohol-free drinks for the mother, keep the banquet short, avoid smoking near the baby, and replace cash gifts with practical presents or a clear no-gift statement.

## Common misreadings

First, confusing the full-moon feast with the first-birthday feast. The grabbing ceremony belongs to the first birthday, not the first month.

Second, assuming the baby must be shaved completely. Tradition often keeps a lock of first hair; a baby's scalp is thin, and close shaving increases the risk of injury and infection.

Third, believing rice wine boosts milk. Alcohol inhibits the milk-ejection reflex and enters breast milk; the “boost” claim has no reliable basis.

Fourth, assuming the feast is ancient in its present form. Pre-Qin ritual describes a three-month hair-cutting rite; the Song provides a fuller “baby-washing” record; the hotel banquet is modern.

Fifth, thinking more wine means more face. The point is announcement and thanks, not a drinking contest.

Sixth, assuming the feast must be large. Scale should follow the family's situation; a small family meal is equally valid.
