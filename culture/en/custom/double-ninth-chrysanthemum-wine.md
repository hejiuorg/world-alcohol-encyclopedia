# Double Ninth chrysanthemum wine

Double Ninth falls on the ninth day of the ninth lunar month, a day whose two nines give it the name Chongyang, “double yang”. People climb heights, wear zhuyu, view chrysanthemums, drink chrysanthemum wine and eat Double Ninth cake. Chrysanthemum wine is not one fixed recipe but a family of seasonal drinks in which chrysanthemums take part, made for the weeks around the festival. Its meaning is not only flavour: “nine” sounds like “long”, chrysanthemums bloom in late autumn, and wine becomes a way to express long life, health and respect for elders.

Because it is a family of drinks, one household's method cannot stand for the whole tradition. The Han method, the Song shop wine and today's home-steeped spirit are related but not identical.

## Time and place

The festival date is fixed at the ninth day of the ninth lunar month, after the autumn harvest and when chrysanthemums open. Brewing can begin a year earlier. Xijing Zaji says to gather stems and leaves “when chrysanthemums open”, mix them with millet and brew, so that the wine is ready by the ninth day of the following ninth month. The record ties the festival, the farming calendar and the fermentation cycle together.

Regionally, the custom is widespread in Han areas but methods differ greatly. In the middle and lower Yellow River regions it is linked with climbing and chrysanthemum viewing. Around Tongxiang in Zhejiang, Huangshan in Anhui and Bozhou in Anhui, medicinal chrysanthemums are famous, and local families have long steeped hangju, gongju or boju in wine; the “four famous chrysanthemums” are boju, chuju, gongju and hangju. In parts of Lingnan, Double Ninth is also a day for ancestor offerings, so wine is used for sacrifice rather than for a banquet. In the north, cake, mutton and wine often form a single autumn menu. Writing one local recipe as the “ancient standard” erases these differences.

## Sequence and vessels

Traditional methods fall into two families. The first is steeping: dried chrysanthemums go into yellow wine or spirits, sealed for seven days to a month until the aroma is drawn out. The second is fermentation: chrysanthemums are brewed with millet and qu, in a process close to rice wine, with a longer cycle and a flavour closer to the millet brew described in Xijing Zaji. A third, medicinal version adds goji or angelica, but that belongs to a different body of knowledge and should not be confused with the festival drink.

The vessels are simple: an earthenware jar, a porcelain jug, a warming bowl and small cups. The jar breathes and keeps out light, suiting storage; the jug makes pouring easy; the warming bowl heats wine in a water bath rather than over a flame. The usual sequence is to return from climbing, set out Double Ninth cake, pour the wine, serve elders first and then drink together. In some places wine is sprinkled at the door or at a grave for protection and remembrance, a different gesture from the toast at the table.

## Symbolic meaning

The first layer is long life. Nine is the extreme yang number, “double nine” is read as “long, long”, and chrysanthemums still flower around the frost, so the three support a wish for longevity. The second is protection from illness. Zhuyu and chrysanthemum were both credited in traditional pharmacy with dispelling wind and brightening the eyes, and wine served as the carrier. The third is respect for elders: at the Double Ninth banquet the oldest are served first, turning a wish into a visible act. The fourth is the autumn harvest: new millet, new flowers and new wine give thanks for the year.

A fifth layer belongs to literati culture. Tao Yuanming's image of picking chrysanthemums by the eastern fence linked the flower with retreat and integrity, and later Double Ninth poetry and wine gatherings borrowed that image. The same cup can thus be filial piety at a family table, moral character in a poem, and a protective charm at a local fair.

## Historical change

Xijing Zaji gives one of the earliest full accounts: on the ninth day, wear zhuyu, eat cakes, drink chrysanthemum wine, and brew in one year for drinking the next. In the Three Kingdoms period, Cao Pi explained in a letter to Zhong Yao that Double Ninth was a day for banquets because nine is a yang number and the sun and moon both answer to it, showing that the feast had entered elite life by the Wei and Jin. The story of Tao Yuanming having no wine one Double Ninth, until a friend sent some, tied the drink to literati anecdote.

The Tang and Song were a second peak. Double Ninth poetry flourished. Wang Wei's line about everyone wearing zhuyu except the one brother who is absent is about separation; [Li Qingzhao](ref:li-qingzhao)'s line about holding wine at the eastern fence after dusk is about autumn longing and solitude. In city life, Dongjing Meng Hua Lu records the splendour of chrysanthemum viewing in Bianjing and notes that wine shops bound chrysanthemums into doorways to attract customers, showing that the drink had moved from household brewing into the market.

Ming and Qing gazetteers continued to record Double Ninth wine, but recipes became more local. In 1989 the Chinese government designated the ninth day of the ninth lunar month as a day for the elderly, and the 2012 revision of the Law on the Protection of the Rights and Interests of the Elderly made it the official Senior Citizens' Day. In 2006 the Double Ninth festival was listed in the first national intangible cultural heritage catalogue. The emphasis on honouring elders is a modern institutional development and should not be read back into the Han period.

## Contemporary disputes

The first dispute is efficacy. Chrysanthemum contains volatile oils and flavonoids, but “prolonging life” and “curing disease” belong to traditional health discourse, not clinical conclusions. Treating the wine as medicine can delay proper care. The second is the risk of home brewing. Steeping involves pesticide residues, container hygiene, alcohol strength and storage temperature; fermentation can spoil through contamination. The third is interaction with medication: anyone taking anticoagulants, diabetes drugs or sedatives should ask a doctor before drinking.

The fourth is the shift from respect to pressure. Making an elder drink “for filial piety” can cause falls or blood-pressure swings. The fifth is commercialisation: gift-boxed chrysanthemum wine can carry a high price while the contents are ordinary rice wine with flavouring. The sixth is heritage performance: a “traditional brewing” demonstration that keeps the gestures but drops the craft becomes stage dressing.

A safer approach is to treat the wine as a seasonal flavour cue: a small amount, with food, alongside chrysanthemum tea, with the emphasis on the climb, company and a greeting.

## Common misreadings

First, that there is only one recipe. There are steeping, fermentation and medicinal paths, with different ingredients and cycles.

Second, that Han people already steeped chrysanthemums in distilled spirits. Xijing Zaji describes a millet fermentation; distilled spirits became widespread much later, so today's method cannot be projected onto the Han.

Third, that “prolonging life” is a medical finding. It is a blessing and a health saying, not a promise of efficacy.

Fourth, that Double Ninth is only a festival for the elderly. Climbing, zhuyu, the autumn harvest and protection from evil were all part of it; honouring elders is one layer.

Fifth, that the wine improves indefinitely with age. The aroma of steeped flowers fades, and low-strength wine can spoil; ageing only makes sense for certain fermented wines.

Sixth, that any chrysanthemum can go into wine. Ornamental varieties may carry pesticide residues, and their cultivation differs from that of medicinal chrysanthemums.
