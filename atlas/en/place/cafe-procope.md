# Le Procope

![Le Procope image](/media/photos/place-cafe-procope-e1f880f0.jpg)

Figure: Le Procope. Image: Jean-Marie Hullot from France, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons ([source page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Le_Procope_1,_Paris_2010.jpg)).

## How "the oldest café in Paris" works

Le Procope stands at 13 Rue de l'Ancienne Comédie in the sixth arrondissement, where the street turns between Boulevard Saint-Germain and the old theatre quarter. Its official page is direct: it has traded since 1686 and is "the oldest café in Paris". That sentence needs unpacking. 1686 is the year Francesco Procopio dei Coltelli opened a café here; the room, restaurant and salons you see today have been altered and rebuilt many times. "Oldest" refers to the beginning of a tradition of café keeping at this address, not to tables, walls or chandeliers preserved untouched from the seventeenth century.

Even with that qualification, its place in Paris café history is real. Procope was not the first place in the city to sell coffee, but it turned coffee into a public experience: sit down, take a porcelain cup, be served at a table. For late seventeenth-century Paris that was a new drink and a new way of socialising. What customers paid for was not only a dark liquid but a table from which to watch, be seen and talk. That shift matters more than the earliest possible date.

## From Sicilian coffee maker to Enlightenment salon

Francesco Procopio dei Coltelli came from Sicily and is usually shortened to Procopio. He brought Italian café practice to Paris and opened near the old Comédie, a quarter full of actors, writers and theatre audiences, which was naturally good for a business built on people coming and going. In the eighteenth century Procope became a meeting point for Enlightenment figures; the official page names Voltaire, Rousseau and Diderot as regulars. Exaggerated anecdotes about Voltaire drinking dozens of cups a day are later embroidery and should not be treated as precise records; what can be said is that his name is genuinely attached to the house.

During the French Revolution, Procope is also often described as a place where political figures met. That narrative needs the same caution: a café is an exchange of information, but later storytelling likes to attach every historical turn to a particular table. The safer view is that Procope provided a continuing public drawing room where people of different classes and professions met in one space. Set it inside the [history of taverns](ref:taverns) and selling drinks turns out to be only the surface function; what is really consumed is seating, time and conversation.

## The menu: coffee, food and wine

Today Procope is a café-restaurant-glacier, and the official site classifies it as Restaurant – Café – Glacier. Lunch and dinner are built on traditional French cooking, with coq au vin and braised beef cheek named by the house; the menu also offers onion soup, foie gras, snails, fish and vegetarian options. The set structure is clear: at lunch there are two tiers of entry-main or main-dessert, and a full three-course menu; in the evening there is another set named after the Revolutionaries. Prices change, so read the official menu rather than an old guide.

For coffee, "un café" means an espresso; "café crème" is the milky version; non-coffee drinkers have hot chocolate, tea and desserts. It is also a full restaurant, so the [wine](ref:wine) list is more complete than in an ordinary café, with glasses and bottles; aperitifs and digestifs are easy to find. The official site also runs a café-salon slot, usually on weekday afternoons and without reservations, for people who only want a coffee and a pastry; the dining rooms take bookings through the official site. Check the official channels for current times and prices.

## Getting there and when to go

The address is 13 Rue de l'Ancienne Comédie, 75006 Paris, France. The nearest Métro is Odéon on line 4, about two minutes on foot; Saint-Germain-des-Prés church and Les Deux Magots are also a few minutes away. Parking is scarce, and the official site lists École de Médecine and Marché Saint-Germain as the nearest car parks, but driving in this quarter is not relaxing; use the Métro or walk. The Seine, the Louvre and the Musée d'Orsay are all within a reasonable walk, so the café fits into a Left Bank half-day. The street is short and partly pedestrian, and the entrance is easy to miss behind a modest frontage; look for the number rather than a large sign. Inside, the historic dining rooms are on the ground floor, with the café salon and courtyard beyond.

The official page lists restaurant hours of 12:00–00:00 daily and a café-salon slot of 15:30–17:30 Monday to Friday, with no booking. Holidays, private events and summer terrace arrangements change this, so check the website before setting out. For a full meal, especially at weekends and in high season, reserving is safer than queueing; for coffee only, the weekday afternoon salon is quieter than the lunch rush. The hidden courtyard terrace on the Cour Saint-André side is pleasant in good weather, but seats are limited and cannot be specified.

## Ordering and sitting

At the door, decide which experience you want: a full meal, the café salon, or a quick drink at the bar. For a meal, wait to be seated and do not wander into the historic salons looking for an empty table; the café salon opens at set times, and you order once seated. If you only want a coffee, say so at the door so you are not seated in the dining room. Your French need not be perfect: "un café, s'il vous plaît" is an espresso, "un café crème" is coffee with milk, and "une carafe d'eau" is free tap water. Menu prices usually include service, so a tip is not compulsory; leave small change or round up the bill.

Dress is not formal; smart casual is enough. This is a busy tourist address, but it is still a working restaurant, not a museum gallery. When photographing, avoid other diners' faces, do not move neighbouring chairs to improve the frame, and do not treat the historic rooms as a free exhibition. With children, lunch is easier than dinner; with limited mobility, telephone ahead about the thresholds and narrow passages of an old building.

## The neighbourhood and common mistakes

The first mistake is to turn "oldest in Paris" into "oldest in the world"; the two claims cover different ground. The second is to believe Voltaire had a fixed seat; no table is reserved for any famous customer, and such stories are narrative tradition. The third is to treat Procope as a coffee-only café: it is first a restaurant, and the kitchen and wine list carry real weight. The fourth is to imagine the Enlightenment salon as a quiet reading room; lunch and dinner are loud, and a quieter conversation belongs outside peak hours.

Nearby you can walk through the old Cour du Commerce Saint-André, see Saint-Germain-des-Prés church, the Odéon theatre and the Musée Delacroix; a few minutes further are Les Deux Magots and Café de Flore. To put Paris's cafés and bars on one urban map, read [Pub and city](ref:pub-and-city); for French drinking culture, read [France](ref:france). Procope works best as the meal stop on a Left Bank route, not as the only place you visit in a day.
