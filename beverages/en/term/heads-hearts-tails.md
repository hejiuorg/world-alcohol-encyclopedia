# Heads, hearts and tails

Heads, hearts and tails are the three fractions of a pot-still run. They are not three different spirits but three successive parts of one distillation. The operator switches collection vessels according to aroma, taste and strength: heads come first, hearts in the middle, tails last. Hearts make up the body of the final spirit; heads and tails are usually recycled into the next batch.

## Why the run must be cut

Fermented wash contains more than ethanol and water. Yeast produces esters, higher alcohols, aldehydes, acids and sulfur compounds, and the raw material adds phenols and other volatiles. These components have different boiling points, so they do not enter the vapour at the same time or in the same proportion. The lowest-boiling substances come over first, then ethanol with a band of aroma compounds, then heavier, oily components.

If the whole run were collected together, the spirit would carry both the sharpness of the heads and the heaviness of the tails. Cutting is a trade-off between yield and cleanliness. A wider cut raises volume but admits more impurities; a narrower cut gives a cleaner spirit but can strip aroma. There is no universal formula: the decision depends on equipment, raw material and the style being made.

## What each fraction contains

Heads, also called foreshots, are dominated by low-boiling compounds such as acetaldehyde, ethyl acetate and methanol. Acetaldehyde smells of green apple or solvent, ethyl acetate can smell of nail polish at high concentration, and methanol boils at about 64.7°C, earlier than ethanol. Heads usually smell aggressive and taste sharp, so they are not used as the body of a spirit.

Hearts are the ethanol-rich middle. They also carry the esters and some phenols that define style. Their strength is high, their texture rounder, their aroma clearer and their bite lower. Hearts are not “pure ethanol”; they are the fraction where unwanted compounds have been reduced to a workable range. Too little and the spirit is thin; too much and it is coarse. That is why hearts are treated as the body.

Tails, also called feints, are dominated by higher-boiling compounds: fusel alcohols such as isoamyl and isobutyl alcohol, fatty acids, some sulfur compounds and phenols. They often smell oily, of cardboard or soap, sometimes with smoke, and their strength falls as they run. Tails are not automatically waste: some phenols and long-chain esters add body and smokiness, so a distillery may deliberately keep a small share.

## Why hearts are the body

Hearts are the body because they satisfy three conditions at once: enough ethanol, enough aroma, and unpleasant compounds below their sensory thresholds. Too many low-boiling aldehydes and esters from the heads give solvent notes; too many higher alcohols and fatty acids from the tails give oiliness and bitterness. Hearts sit between the two and balance best.

That does not mean more hearts is always better. Moving the cut towards the heads gives more impact and more risk of sharpness; moving it towards the tails gives more weight and smoke, with a risk of haze and bitterness. Distilleries choose different points according to their equipment and house style, which is a major source of variation within one region. For how equipment shapes reflux, see [pot still](ref:pot-still).

## What happens to heads and tails

The usual answer is recycling. Heads and tails are collected separately. Heads often go back into the next batch's low wines, while tails go into a feints receiver and are added to the next distillation. The logic is simple: both fractions still contain ethanol and recoverable aroma, and discarding them wastes spirit and increases the load on wastewater treatment.

Recycling is not infinite. Some heavy compounds accumulate over repeated passes, so a distillery will periodically discard part of the feints or treat the final residue separately. Some producers add a small amount of tails back to the spirit for extra body; this needs care, because too much brings haze, bitterness and stability problems. For strength measurement, see [alcohol by volume](ref:abv).

## How cut points are judged

Cut points rest on three signals: the strength of the distillate, its aroma and its taste. Strength is the most obvious number, and operators measure the run continuously, sampling more densely near the expected transition. Aroma and taste decide the final point: the head phase often smells of solvent, the heart phase turns fruity and floral, and the tail phase brings oil and cardboard.

Experienced ranges differ by spirit. Many Scotch distilleries make the heads cut at roughly 70%–75% abv and the tails cut at roughly 55%–65% abv on the second distillation; lighter styles cut earlier, heavier styles later. These are common ranges, not regulations, and they should not be transferred directly to another still or raw material. For the legal framework, see [Scotch whisky](ref:scotch-whisky).

## Differences between spirits

Whisky is usually distilled twice, with the first run producing low wines and the second producing the cut spirit. A few distilleries distil three times and cut more narrowly for a lighter body. Cognac's second pass, the bonne chauffe, also separates heads, hearts and tails, and recycles the ends into the next batch. Pot-distilled rum often keeps more tails for a heavy ester profile, while column distillation draws streams continuously and has no equivalent batch cut.

Vodka aims at a high degree of purification: the cut is narrow and the spirit is further rectified or filtered, so the aromatic signature of heads and tails is largely removed. Tequila is usually distilled twice with a wider cut that retains vegetal character. The principle that hearts are the body holds across categories, but how wide the hearts should be is entirely a matter of style. For the wider process, see [whisky](ref:whisky).

## Common misunderstandings

First, treating heads and tails as “the poisonous parts” and assuming a simple cut makes the spirit safe. Methanol and other hazardous compounds cannot be separated reliably by three intuitive buckets; commercial distilleries rely on monitoring and legal controls, and home distillation is illegal in many places and genuinely risky. Second, assuming a higher proportion of hearts is always better; a very narrow cut can be clean but empty.

Third, treating cut points as a fixed recipe. Equipment, raw material, fermentation, season and target style all move the ideal point, and the same distillery may adjust between batches. Fourth, assuming tails always taste bad. A small, well-chosen tail fraction can add weight and smoke; dose and timing are what matter. Fifth, reading “cut the heads and tails” as “throw both away”. The industry norm is recovery, not disposal.
