# Huan Xi Sha: Yan Shu Sees Time in a Cup of Wine

Almost nothing happens in this song. A person hears a new song, drinks a cup of wine, walks into a small garden, sees flowers fall and swallows return, and then paces alone. There is no farewell, no war, no violent grief or joy; it simply places a person in a garden at the end of spring, in the evening, and lets him slowly realise that time is passing. Wine appears only once, yet it is the starting point of the whole song: only after that cup do the looking and the thinking begin.

## A song that looks utterly ordinary

Huan Xi Sha is the name of the tune, in two halves of forty-two characters, three lines to each half. Yan Shu gave this song no separate title, and by convention it is known by its first line, One new song, one cup of wine. Its language is extremely plain, with almost no rare characters and no need for allusion; yet after one reading it is hard to forget the couplet No way to stop the falling flowers, the returning swallows look familiar.

Yan Shu (991–1055), courtesy name Tongshu, came from Linchuan in Fuzhou, present-day Fuzhou in Jiangxi. In the second year of the Jingde reign (1005), he was summoned as a child prodigy and was granted the status of a metropolitan graduate; he later rose to be a chancellor and was given the posthumous title Yuanxian. His collection Zhuyu Ci survives. That a man at the top of the bureaucracy should write so slight and wistful a song is not a contradiction: the more stable a life, the more easily one notices the passage of time in ordinary scenery.

## Text

> One new song, one cup of wine; last year's weather, the old pavilion. The sun sets in the west; when will it return?
> No way to stop the falling flowers; the returning swallows look familiar. Alone I pace on the fragrant path of the small garden.

This is the received text. One new song means a newly written lyric to a tune; one cup is a cup of wine; last year's weather means the weather resembles last year's; the old pavilion is the pavilion of former days; “when will it return” asks when it will come back; no way means it cannot be recovered; look familiar means as if once known; the fragrant path is a path covered with fallen blossoms; pace means walking back and forth.

## One new song, one cup of wine: wine and song in daily life

The opening couplet puts two pleasures together: a newly written song and a cup of wine. It is not the clinking of cups at a banquet, but closer to a small daily drink taken while sitting alone. The song is newly written, the wine is one cup, and both quantities are small; this smallness quiets the scene.

Last year's weather, the old pavilion immediately lays today over last year. The weather is still that weather, the pavilion is still that pavilion, and even the way of listening to a song and drinking may not have changed. But the words last year tell the reader at once: the scenery has not changed, the person has passed through a year. The song's skill lies in not saying directly that I have grown older or that time flies, but in letting the same scenery set off a different time.

“The sun sets in the west; when will it return?” is a question. The sun will of course rise again tomorrow, but today's sunset will not come back. On the surface the question asks the sun; in fact it asks time: can that day, that cup of wine, that afternoon return? The poet gives no answer, and the reader knows there is none.

## Last year's weather, the old pavilion: stillness and motion

In the three lines of the upper half, the weather, the pavilion and the setting sun are all external things. Weather and pavilion are still, the sun is moving; last year's weather and the old pavilion fix the space in place, while the setting sun pushes time away. A person standing in an unchanging place and watching something that moves is most likely to feel time.

This is also a common method in classical Chinese poetry: using unchanging things to write about a changing person. The pavilion does not speak and the weather does not keep records; they are simply there. Precisely because they are there, the person discovers that he is no longer in the same place. Wine and song work the same way: the same tune and the same cup, but last year's drink and this year's drink may be entirely different in the mind.

## No way to stop the falling flowers, the returning swallows look familiar

The opening couplet of the lower half is the heart of the song and the couplet Yan Shu prized most. “No way to stop the falling flowers” writes loss: the flowers fall and no one can stop them; the returning swallows look familiar writes return: the swallows fly back as if seen somewhere before. One going, one coming; one loss, one gain; the match is exact.

No way acknowledges human helplessness. The falling flowers are not a disaster, only the normal process of the season; but precisely because it is normal, it feels impossible to recover. Look familiar is not confirmation but a feeling: perhaps it is not the same swallow, perhaps only last year's flock has returned, but the familiarity is enough to make a person pause.

The beauty of the couplet is that it neither turns loss into despair nor turns return into consolation. The flowers really have fallen and the swallows really have come back; loss and compensation happen at the same time, and the person can only stand between them. Yan Shu valued these lines highly and is said to have used them in a poem as well; the same couplet appearing in different forms shows that his sense of time and recurrence was something he turned over repeatedly.

## Alone I pace on the fragrant path: a walk after wine

The last line brings the camera back to the body. The fragrant path is a small road covered with fallen blossoms, which shows that the flowers have been falling for some time; pacing alone means walking back and forth, which shows that the poet has not left at once and has not found a place to stop. The action is light, yet it brings all the looking and thinking down into the body.

The word alone deserves attention. No second person appears anywhere in the song; hearing the song, drinking, watching the flowers and walking are all done alone. Wine here is not a social medium but company in solitude. A cup of wine is not enough to intoxicate; its function is to slow a person down, slow enough to hear the song, slow enough to see the flowers fall, slow enough to walk a few more turns on the path.

For the craft background of grain wine in the Song, see [Yellow Wine](ref:yellow-wine); note that Song wines and today's yellow wine do not correspond exactly. The wine in this song is not named, and that makes it more general: what matters is not what was drunk but what was seen while drinking.

## Textual variants

Huan Xi Sha is short, and differences in its transmission lie mainly in punctuation and in the wording of a few anthologies rather than in whole lines. This poem entry follows the received text given by Gushiwen and checks it against Complete Song Ci. When quoting, one should remember that the tune name Huan Xi Sha is not the same as the title of this song: the first line, One new song, one cup of wine, is often used as a title, but strictly speaking it is only the first line.

## Common misreadings

First, taking One new song, one cup of wine as heavy drinking. The quantity is only one cup, and the scene is a small daily drink, not a banquet.

Second, reading no way as despair. It is an acknowledgement of the falling flowers, not a denial of life.

Third, taking look familiar as recognising the same swallow. It is a feeling of familiarity and need not be the same bird.

Fourth, taking Alone I pace on the fragrant path as a leisurely stroll. Pacing carries hesitation and reluctance, not pure recreation.

Fifth, equating Yan Shu's wealth and rank with a life without care. What he writes is precisely the sensitivity to passing time within a secure life.
