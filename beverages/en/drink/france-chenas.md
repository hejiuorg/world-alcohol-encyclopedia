# Chénas

The name Chénas comes from the oak, chêne in French. Before it became vineyard, this ground was continuous oak woodland, and many parcels still carry the names and boundaries left when the trees were cleared. That history explains Chénas better than the label "smallest cru": it keeps the fragmented feel of small clearings, together with the tight backbone typical of the granite slopes in the north. The usual bottle is 750 mL, at about 12.5%–13.5% [alcohol by volume](ref:abv).

## The village name left by the oaks

The place-name explanation is direct: Chénas shares its root with the French word for oak, chêne. Since the Middle Ages this woodland in northern Beaujolais was gradually cleared and planted, and vines replaced the oaks. The name fixes that change of land use, and gives Chénas a "forest to vineyard" layer that the other crus do not carry in the same way.

Like most Beaujolais villages, Chénas has no premier or grand cru tier. Vineyards within the village and on adjoining ground all fall under one cru, and the legal area is not confined to the village centre but also includes part of a neighbouring commune; the official delimitation governs the exact line. A bottle of Chénas may therefore draw on slopes in more than one village while carrying a single name.

## The smallest of the ten crus

Chénas is the smallest of the ten crus. A small area means limited total volume, so it appears on the market less often than the larger crus to the south; on restaurant lists it is often pushed behind Moulin-à-Vent and Fleurie.

Yet "smallest" is a scale, not a quality judgement. A small area concentrates the differences of parcel: producers have little room to choose, and their wines tend to stay close to the few slopes they hold. When buying Chénas, knowing a reliable estate is more useful than knowing a grand appellation concept.

## Granite, quartz and aspect

The soil is mostly weathered granite sand, with some parcels containing quartz grains and schist. It is thin, drains fast and holds little fertility, so roots go down for water and yields are naturally restrained. The quartz makes drainage even more complete, which explains why Chénas can keep relatively fresh fruit in wet years.

Aspect sets the pace of ripening: south-facing slopes catch plenty of sun and ripen more fully, while higher and shaded parcels stay cooler and retain more acidity. The overall style is therefore a little firmer than Fleurie, without the thickness of Moulin-à-Vent. The official delimitation governs boundaries and parcel assignments.

## Family estates and merchant bottling

Chénas is dominated by family estates, with cooperatives and merchants also involved. Because the area is small, many estates produce very little, so the share of grapes or wine bought and bottled by merchants matters proportionally more. The bottler information on the label is the most direct way to separate estate wine from merchant wine.

Grapes from the same slope can go to different bottlers, splitting the style in two: estate bottling stresses parcel character, merchant bottling stresses consistency and uniformity. Reading "Mis en bouteille au domaine" against "par un négociant" is more effective than comparing the weight of the bottle.

## Whole clusters and medium structure

Chénas often uses whole clusters, and some estates practise semi-carbonic maceration: whole grapes finish intracellular fermentation in a carbon-dioxide-filled vessel, then are pressed and pass into a conventional [fermentation](ref:fermentation). This brings direct red-fruit aroma and lighter tannin, a common approach in northern Beaujolais.

Compared with Fleurie, maceration in Chénas tends to run a little longer and extraction is slightly higher, so the wine has some grip rather than pure suppleness. Some wines go into used [oak](ref:oak-ageing); new barrels are usually a very low proportion, so that spice and toast do not cover the fruit. Fermentation temperatures are kept moderate to balance aroma and structure.

## Tighter than Fleurie, earlier than Moulin-à-Vent

Typical aromas are red cherry, raspberry, strawberry and violet, with some wines showing spice and minerality. The palate has clear acidity, fine but gripping tannin and a light-to-medium body, finishing with more backbone than Fleurie.

Set the three villages side by side and the differences are plain. On sheer floral openness Fleurie is louder and Chénas more reserved; on tannin grip Chénas is firmer than Fleurie but softer than Moulin-à-Vent; on weight and ageing potential [Moulin-à-Vent](ref:moulin-a-vent) is clearly a step above, while Chénas comes into its drinking window earlier. In one line: Chénas is a tighter Fleurie, not a smaller Moulin-à-Vent.

## How cool and warm years reshape Chénas

In warm years the fruit ripens fully, flavours are sweeter and riper, alcohol sits near the upper end of the range and tannin is more evident, pushing the style toward Moulin-à-Vent. In cool years acidity is clearer, the body lighter and spice and minerality more prominent, though under-ripe vintages can show green stalk.

Read Chénas through the base and the stated strength, then the sweetness or bitterness, and finally how it is served. The sequence matters.

## Price tier of a very small cru

Chénas sits in the middle-upper band of the Beaujolais crus, usually above regional Beaujolais and close to regular Fleurie; because the area is the smallest and volume limited, estate-bottled wines that name a specific parcel move up another step. Only relative bands are described here; no figures are given.

Two checks are most practical. First the bottler: small-volume estate bottling deserves closer attention. Second vintage and alcohol: warm years run higher and cool years lower, and that is a choice of style rather than a rank. A proper channel and a complete back label are the basis for avoiding blended wine. Unopened bottles should be kept out of light, lying flat, at an ideal 12–15 degrees Celsius.

## Misunderstandings about area and the village name

First, assuming the smallest area means the weakest quality. Scale and quality are not the same thing, and the small parcels of Chénas can be more distinctive.
Second, reading Chénas as "chestnut". The name shares its root with the oak, chêne, and refers to the old oak woodland, not to chestnuts.
Third, thinking Chénas is made only inside the village. The legal area also includes part of a neighbouring commune, subject to the official delimitation.
Fourth, treating Chénas as Beaujolais Nouveau. It is a separate cru built on balance between structure and fruit, not a wine for drinking in the year of harvest.
Fifth, judging authenticity by price or packaging. The bottler and back label are more reliable.
