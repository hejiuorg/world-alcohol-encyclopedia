# Zhongyuan Festival and wine

The Zhongyuan Festival falls on the fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month, commonly called “the half of the seventh month”. On this day Taoism speaks of Zhongyuan, Buddhism of Ullambana, and folk practice makes it a festival of ancestor remembrance, offering and charity. Wine has a clear place here: it is not for merriment but for offering. A cup poured for the dead, or tipped onto the ground, turns an abstract grief into a visible act.

## Time and place

The main date is the fifteenth of the seventh lunar month, but some regions, especially in the south, observe the fourteenth. Such differences reflect local history, migration and calendar habits; it is not simply a matter of one date being “correct”. The festival is also longer than one day in places: some families welcome the ancestors at the start of the month and send them off on the fifteenth, creating a period of half a month.

Regional variation is strong. Coastal and southern China stress ancestor offerings, paper money, river lanterns and food distribution; parts of the north stress grave visits and household offerings; Shanxi and other areas have old customs of steaming dough sheep; Guangdong, Guangxi and Fujian commonly associate the day with duck. In cities, practice is often reduced to a home altar, paper offerings or a visit to a designated burning site. No single local form represents the whole.

## Historical sources

The festival has layered origins. Taoism teaches the “three primes”: the fifteenth of the first month is Shangyuan, the fifteenth of the seventh is Zhongyuan, and the fifteenth of the tenth is Xiayuan. Zhongyuan is associated with the earth office, the remission of sins and the salvation of the dead. The Buddhist Ullambana festival is linked to the story of Mulian saving his mother in the Ullambana Sutra, which stresses offerings to the monastic community as a way to help the deceased. Folk practice then combined both with native ancestor sacrifice and autumn offerings, producing the multiple faces seen today.

Song urban note-books record the market and activities around the festival: paper clothes, paper shoes and ritual goods for sale, altars and lanterns for salvation, a bustle comparable to other major holidays. Dongjing Meng Hua Lu helps us see how Zhongyuan expanded from religious rite into urban consumption and public activity. But religious ritual, folk custom and commercial sale are three separate lines, even as they influence one another.

## Procedure and vessels

Common steps include cleaning the hall or the grave, setting an offering table, arranging dishes, fruit and cakes, burning incense, pouring wine, burning paper, bowing and sending the ancestors off; some places float river lanterns, distribute food or invite monks or Taoist priests to recite scriptures. Wine is usually poured at the first offering, into a small cup; it is raised, then poured onto the ground, and the cup is refilled to symbolise continuation. The number of pours and the words spoken vary by region and family, and there is no national standard.

For vessels, traditional offering tables use pots, small cups and wine cups, and wealthier households keep a separate set of ritual vessels not used for ordinary drinking. Modern families often use plain glass or small porcelain cups; what matters is that they are reserved and clean, not that they are expensive. The libation should be slow and should not be splashed about: it is a rhythm of speaking with the dead, not a performance.

## Wine and food

Zhongyuan offerings are usually home-style dishes: rice, chicken, fish, pork, tofu, seasonal vegetables, fruit and cakes. In parts of the south, duck is the seasonal food, playing on the sound of ya (“to press”), as if to press down evil; in parts of the north, wheat foods dominate. Wine has two functions in this meal: first, to offer, letting the ancestors drink first; second, to gather, as the family sits down after the rite and wine becomes an ordinary accompaniment.

For drink choice, yellow wine and rice wine are mild and clear in colour, suitable for a home altar; baijiu may be used for libation in some regions, but only in small amounts; beer rarely appears on a formal offering table. Whether the offered wine is afterwards drunk varies: some families share it as a blessing, others pour it away or leave it at the grave. Both are common, and neither deserves scolding.

## Change and contemporary practice

Modern Zhongyuan faces several changes. The first is space: flats are unsuitable for burning paper, so many places use designated burning points, community rites, flowers or electronic incense. The second is family structure: young people working elsewhere cannot return, and online memorials and paid grave-visiting services appear. The third is expression: wine has moved from a “must-pour” offering to an option, and some families use tea instead.

These changes are often described as decline, but they can also be read as ritual searching for a new container. As long as the wish to remember remains, forms will change. What deserves caution is the commercial packaging of the festival as horror-themed entertainment, or the turning of ancestor rites into a contest of display; both dilute the real content of the day.

## Disputes and health boundaries

First, burning and the environment. Concentrated paper burning causes air pollution and fire risk, and many cities restrict or prohibit roadside burning. Flowers, a wine offering and a moment of silence can preserve the intention.

Second, drinking and driving. Grave visits and family meals cluster around the festival, and the risk of driving after drinking rises. No “just a little” is worth it.

Third, drunken loss of control. Emotions are already heavy at an ancestor rite, and alcohol can amplify grief or start family arguments. Drinking less often keeps the rite quieter and more dignified.

Fourth, respect for belief. Zhongyuan involves Taoism, Buddhism and folk practice, and families differ. Outsiders should not dismiss it as “superstition”, nor force their own practice on others.

Fifth, food safety. The seventh month is hot, and offerings left out for long periods spoil easily; reheat thoroughly before eating, and store wine properly.

## Common misreadings

First, reducing the day to “Ghost Festival”. It is at once Taoist Zhongyuan, Buddhist Ullambana and folk ancestor remembrance; the horror label hides the side of grief and charity.

Second, assuming everyone observes the fifteenth. Some regions use the fourteenth, and the length of the festival varies.

Third, equating Ullambana with Taoist Zhongyuan. They influenced each other, but their scriptures and rites have different sources.

Fourth, treating a libation as an invitation to drink. It is an offering to the dead and the spirits, not an excuse to press the living.

Fifth, assuming everyone eats duck. It is a regional custom, not a national rule.
