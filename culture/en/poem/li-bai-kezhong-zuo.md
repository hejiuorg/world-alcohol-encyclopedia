# Written While Travelling: Li Bai's Lanling Wine and a Host Who Makes a Guest Forget Home

Li Bai's “Written While Travelling” (Kezhong Zuo, also known as Kezhong Xing) is only four lines and twenty-eight characters, yet it turns an ordinary act of hospitality on a journey into an experience of being made at home in a foreign place. There is no homesickness here and no heavy lament over a wandering life; there is Lanling wine, the light in a jade bowl, and a host who makes his guest forget where he is. Because the poem is so light, it is easy to miss its turn: the first couplet is about things, the second about people. The English rendering on this page is the project's own translation, not an authoritative version.

## The text, quoted sparingly

The poem is short enough to quote only its four lines:

> Lanling's fine wine is scented with turmeric;
> Poured into a jade bowl, it gives an amber light.
> So long as the host can make his guest drink his fill,
> He no longer knows what place could be a foreign land.

This is a project translation, not an authoritative version; it follows the common Chinese text rather than any single English edition.

The four lines fall into two halves. The first couplet is about wine and vessel: the first line names the place, the wine and its fragrance; the second line describes the bowl and the colour of the liquid. The second couplet is about people and relationship: “so long as” translates a conditional word, and the fourth line gives the result of that condition. If the host can make the guest drink his fill, the guest stops caring where he is.

The title's “while travelling” marks the speaker as a man away from home. Some editions give the title as Kezhong Xing; the difference between “zuo” (written) and “xing” (a travel song) is slight, and both point to an occasional poem made on a journey. The textual variants cluster around “jade bowl”: a Song-dynasty edition of Li Bai's collected works writes the character 椀 and notes the alternatives 埦 and 盌, all names for the same kind of bowl. For a short poem, the version question need not be exaggerated, but it is worth knowing that the text is not a single fixed string of characters.

## The author and background

Li Bai (701–762) was a Tang poet. Britannica describes him as one of the most famous poets of the Tang dynasty, known for his bold imagination, powerful language and vivid personality. He travelled widely throughout his life, left Sichuan in his youth, visited Chang'an in middle age, and also lived for a time in the region east of Lu. Lanling was one of the places he visited while in that region; it lies in what is now Lanling County, Linyi, Shandong Province.

The common dating of the poem is the end of the Kaiyuan era, around 739–740, after Li Bai first arrived in eastern Lu and made an excursion to Lanling. Some studies place it instead around the beginning of the Tianbao era, near his Chang'an journey. The exact year and occasion are unsettled. What is certain is the word “guest”: he is not a local but a traveller passing through. This matters, because the poem's force comes from the contrast between the guest's status and the host's generosity.

There is also a widely repeated background story: a tavern keeper or host in Lanling served the poet the local famous wine, and Li Bai wrote the poem after drinking it. Such stories have a legendary flavour and should not be treated as strict history. They do show, however, that later generations have consistently tied the poem to Lanling's local wine culture.

## Wine imagery

The wine in this poem is not the wine of solitude but the wine of hospitality. The first couplet makes it look beautiful: fragrant, coloured, luminous. The second couplet stops describing the wine and describes its effect instead. The beauty of the wine must finally land on a human relationship; otherwise even the finest adjectives would be advertising copy.

“Making the guest drink his fill” is worth reading closely. It does not mean forcing wine down the guest's throat; it means helping him relax, enjoy himself and forget his constraints. The host does not order the guest to drink more; he prepares the wine and lets the guest enter that relaxed state on his own. The last line is therefore not “I got drunk” but “I no longer know where the foreign land is”. The intoxication is only the surface; the real change is a sense of belonging.

This is also where the poem differs from ordinary homesickness verse. A traveller usually writes of missing home, longing for people, or the hardship of drifting. This poem reverses the direction: it writes about forgetting home. Lanling has not become his home; rather, the host's warmth makes the very category of “foreign land” temporarily lose its force. Wine is the medium, not the answer; the answer is the host. Li Bai turns a word of thanks to a host into a light-hearted resolution of his own situation.

Structurally, the first couplet is sensory preparation and the second is the completion of a relationship. If the last line is pulled out alone, it sounds like a polite cliché; put back beside the fragrance and light of the first two lines, it acquires weight. The wine imagery is therefore not an isolated “fine wine” but a complete act of hospitality: bring out good wine, pour it into a good bowl, let the guest drink until he relaxes, and finally make him forget that he is an outsider.

## Vessels and the kind of wine

The jade bowl is the most concrete object in the poem. It is not an everyday coarse pottery bowl but a precious vessel; using it for wine shows the formality of the reception. Jade is smooth and slightly translucent, and the colour of the wine seen through its walls tends to look brighter. “Poured in” is an action: the wine is ladled from a jar and put into the bowl, turning a name on a label into a bowl of wine before the eyes.

“Amber light” describes colour and lustre. Amber is fossilised resin, ranging from golden yellow to reddish brown; comparing wine to amber tells us that the liquid is not colourless and transparent but golden or reddish-brown, with a gleam under the light. Given what is known of Lanling wine's craft, this is closer to a fermented wine made from rice, through steaming, saccharification, fermentation and pressing — what we would now call a yellow-wine type — rather than a distilled spirit. Whether distillation existed in the Tang is still debated, but reading this poem as a scene of modern strong baijiu is to apply today's drinking habits to a Tang table.

“Turmeric” is another easy place to go wrong. The Chinese word here refers to a plant in the ginger family, whose rhizome was used as medicine, as a dye, and to steep wine; the ancients infused it in wine to give the liquor fragrance and a golden colour. “Lanling's fine wine is scented with turmeric” therefore means wine steeped with turmeric, not wine flavoured with tulip flowers. The modern tulip is a different thing altogether, and Dutch bulbs are no guide to a Tang bowl.

Lanling itself has a long history as a wine-producing place. The Lanling region of Shandong still produces a wine marketed under the name “Lanling meijiu”, usually classed as a yellow wine or a blended wine; sharing a name across centuries does not mean sharing a recipe. A reader can acknowledge the continuity of a local famous wine without equating the modern product with the bowl Li Bai drank from.

## Reception through the centuries

Among Li Bai's celebrated poems, this one is not the most conspicuous. It lacks the sweep of a long ballad and the household familiarity of a famous homesickness line; yet it appears quite often in Tang poetry anthologies, local gazetteers and writing about wine culture. The reason is practical: it describes Lanling wine so concretely that later accounts of the local wine almost always quote these four lines.

In the history of criticism, specialised commentary on this poem is relatively sparse compared with a poem such as “Bring in the Wine”. It is more often used as an example of Li Bai's “boldness” or “ease with circumstance”. That summary is reasonable, and it also narrows the poem. If one sees only boldness, one misses the conditional relation in “so long as the host can make his guest drink his fill”, and one misses that “he no longer knows what place could be a foreign land” is a very light way of saying thank you. The centre of gravity is not the amount drunk but the measure of hospitality.

Modern anthologies usually classify it under travel or under drinking. Travel, because of the title and the foreign land; drinking, because of the vessel and the colour in the first couplet. Each classification captures only half. A more accurate description might be: this is a poem about being a guest, and the wine is a gift handed to the guest by the host.

Its reception abroad is also entangled with translation. “Turmeric” is often translated literally as “tulip”, and the poem then becomes, for some English readers, a poem about tulip-flavoured wine — a classic translation-made misreading. The English version on this page uses “turmeric-scented” and states plainly that it is the project's own rendering, not an authoritative translation.

## Common misreadings

First, taking “turmeric” as the tulip. It refers to the rhizome of a ginger-family plant used to steep wine and dye cloth, not the Dutch flower.

Second, taking “amber light” as a piece of amber. Amber is a metaphor for the colour and lustre of the wine, not a lump of resin in the bowl.

Third, taking the “jade bowl” as an ordinary rice bowl. It is a precious wine vessel, and its use shows the host's formality and goodwill.

Fourth, reading the conditional word as “only”. Here it means “so long as” or “if only”, introducing the condition that the host's hospitality depends on.

Fifth, taking “make the guest drink his fill” as “get the guest drunk by force”. It means helping the guest enjoy himself; the emphasis is on the host's welcome, not on quantity.

Sixth, taking “he no longer knows what place could be a foreign land” as drunken disorientation or losing one's way. It describes a psychological ease: under the host's care, the guest temporarily stops feeling like an outsider.

Seventh, treating the poem as homesickness verse. It deliberately reverses homesickness and speaks of forgetting the foreign land, not of missing home.

Eighth, imagining Tang wine as today's strong liquor. The Lanling wine here is closer to a low-alcohol fermented grain wine, and modern high-proof baijiu is a poor guide to its taste.

Ninth, equating ancient and modern Lanling directly. The name and administrative area changed over the centuries, and today's Lanling County is not identical with the Tang place.

## How to read it

First read it as a travel poem: a stranger arrives in Lanling, and the host brings out the local wine. Then read it as a poem of hospitality: the host does not say “make yourself at home” but removes the awkwardness with a bowl of good wine. Finally read it as a short poem about belonging: a person stops feeling like a stranger not because the place is wonderful, but because someone treats him as one of their own.

Notice how the four lines divide their work. The first line gives the wine's origin and fragrance, the second its colour and vessel, the third the host's ability, the fourth the guest's feeling. The first couplet is sensory, the second relational; the more concrete the first couplet, the more credible the ease of the second. Remove the jade bowl and the amber light, and the last line collapses into an empty courtesy.

Notice also the repetition of the word “guest”: the title says “while travelling”, the third line says “guest”, and the last line says “foreign land”. Three words link identity, action and space. The poet does not say directly “I was moved”; he lets the change of identity happen by itself. This is an economical and highly accomplished way of writing.

A reader today need not treat the poem as drinking advice. It is closer to a principle of hospitality: what really puts people at ease is not how expensive the wine is, but whether the host has put the guest at the centre of his care.
