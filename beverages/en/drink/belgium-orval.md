# Orval

Orval is the single Trappist ale made at Orval Abbey in Belgium, commonly 5.2% ABV in 330 mL bottles. It is dry-hopped and fermented again in the bottle, and is known for floral, citrus and dry notes with a light acidity; its character changes with bottle age.

## Definition and position

Separate the levels first: beer is the broad category, abbey ale is a tradition and institutional background, Belgium is the region, and Orval is the abbey, brand and single product. Keeping the four apart prevents confusion between the certification mark, abbey history and the flavour in the bottle.

Orval makes only one beer, which is unusual among abbey breweries. Its style resists a single label: it has the floral note of dry hopping and the acidity and dryness brought by microbes in the bottle, and it is usually described as sitting between a pale ale and a sour beer. This entry does not force it into one fixed category.

## Producer and brand

Orval is brewed at Abbaye Notre-Dame d'Orval in Villers-devant-Orval, Luxembourg province, Belgium. The abbey's history goes back to the 11th century, and modern brewing began in the 1930s (the exact year should be checked against abbey material). The abbey has a direct supervision relationship with the brewery, which belongs to the Trappist beer system.

Abbey, brewery and product are three levels: the abbey answers supervision and where proceeds go, the brewery answers equipment and craft, and the product answers ABV, volume and style. A single product means brand and product largely overlap, but certification status should still be checked on each bottle.

## Region and ingredients

The product comes from Wallonia in [Belgium](ref:belgium). The two main Belgian traditions are abbey ale and lambic spontaneous fermentation; Orval belongs to the abbey system, yet it uses additional microbes in the bottle that give the finished beer a touch of spontaneous-fermentation acidity, which sets it apart from most abbey ales.

The ingredients are [barley](ref:barley), [wheat](ref:wheat) and [water](ref:water), plus hops and sugar. Barley supplies starch and enzymes for mashing, wheat adds softness, and dry hopping with aroma hops brings floral and citrus notes. The exact recipe and proportions are undisclosed.

## Production

Malted grain is mashed into wort, fermented with yeast in the primary [fermentation](ref:fermentation), dry-hopped, then bottled with sugar and yeast for a second fermentation. Dry hopping means adding hops late in fermentation or before bottling, mainly to extract aroma rather than bitterness, which is why the floral note is prominent and bitterness stays low.

Besides ordinary yeast, the bottle fermentation involves microbes that keep working in the bottle, so the beer becomes drier and more acidic over time and its foam and aroma change. The sediment at the bottom is normal deposition and can be left behind when pouring. The strains and parameters belong to abbey information and are only partly public.

## Specification and label

The common [alcohol by volume](ref:abv) is 5.2% ABV, with 330 mL bottles and market-specific versions. The label should be checked for product name, ABV, net content, ingredients, producer and batch; the presence of a Trappist certification mark should also be checked bottle by bottle.

Unlike most abbey ales, Orval's drinking window is closely tied to bottle age: a fresh bottle and one cellared for several years differ clearly in acidity and aroma. The local-language back label, importer and anti-counterfeit information support identity checks, and date information on the bottle or back label is worth noting.

## Flavour and tasting

Typical aromas are flowers, citrus peel, pepper and grain, with light acidity and herbal notes. The palate is dry with a light body, low-to-medium bitterness and a finish of acidity and flowers. With bottle age the acidity and dryness usually increase and the floral note softens.

Serve at about 8–10 degrees Celsius in a tulip-shaped glass that gathers the aroma; too cold suppresses the flowers, too warm pushes the acidity forward. Mind the sediment when pouring. After opening, refrigerate and finish soon rather than warming and cooling repeatedly.

## Drinking and pairing

Poultry, pork, salads, soft cheese and slightly acidic dishes pair well; dryness and floral notes cut fat and lift the food's own flavours. Avoid very sweet or very spicy food, which creates a clash of flavours.

The ABV is moderate but the beer should still be drunk in moderation. Carbonation fades after opening, so finish within a few days; store in a dark place, upright and refrigerated. Excessive drinking is harmful to health, and minors should not drink.

## Buying and storage

Buy through regular channels and check anti-counterfeit marks, the local back label, importer and batch number, and confirm the bottle mark and date information. Prices move with market and channel, so this entry gives no figures; because there is only one product, version differences come mainly from batch and bottle age rather than from a product range.

Unopened bottles can be kept in a dark, cool place, and some drinkers cellar them deliberately for a more acidic, drier profile; the effect depends on temperature and batch, so "older is always better" is not a general rule. After opening, refrigerate and finish soon.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating Orval as an ordinary pale ale. Dry hopping and bottle microbes give it floral notes and acidity.
Second, treating "Trappist style" as certification. Certification has its own mark and institutional requirements.
Third, assuming all beer is better fresh. Orval's flavour changes with bottle age.
Fourth, treating bottle sediment as spoilage. It is normal deposition from the second fermentation.
Fifth, judging quality by packaging or stories. Ingredients, craft and label information are more reliable.

To read Orval, start with the base and the stated strength, then the sweetness or bitterness, then how it is served; no single check settles it.
