# Oktoberfest: a folk festival that grew out of an 1810 wedding

Oktoberfest is often called the world's biggest beer party. That is not wrong, but it misses the more important half: Oktoberfest is first a Volksfest, a public folk festival, and beer is only its most famous component. It has a fairground, parades, brass bands, traditional dress, food and a history of horse racing, as well as strict city management and commercial allocation. To understand it, start with a wedding in 1810.

## Not a beer exhibition, but Munich's folk festival

The full name is Münchner Oktoberfest, and locals call it the Wiesn, meaning “the meadow”. It is held each year on the Theresienwiese, a short distance southwest of central Munich, in temporary festival structures rather than an exhibition hall. During the festival there are beer tents, food stalls, fairground rides, parades and music. Beer is central, but not the only content.

That is what separates it from a professional beer trade fair. ProWein or GABF are built around trade or competition; Oktoberfest is built around public celebration. Locals come with their families, visitors come for Bavarian culture, and breweries serve beer and food in tents. Its first identity is “a festival”; “drinking beer” is second.

## A wedding in 1810

On 12 October 1810 Crown Prince Ludwig of Bavaria, later King Ludwig I, married Princess Therese. Munich invited the public to celebrate on a meadow outside the city walls, and the event closed on 17 October with a horse race. The meadow was named Theresienwiese, Therese's meadow, and is still the festival site.

The race was successful enough to be repeated in 1811, and the Bavarian Agricultural Association fixed the festival as a showcase for farming. In 1818 the first carousel and swings appeared, and rides grew year by year. Beer tents, roast-chicken stalls, electric light and parades came later. Oktoberfest was not “about beer” from the start; it began as a public celebration of a royal wedding and grew into its present form.

## Theresienwiese and sixteen days

The 2026 edition ran from 19 September to 4 October, sixteen days. The dates usually sit around late September and early October and are announced annually by the organisers. The site is the Theresienwiese, served by U-Bahn and S-Bahn stations, with special traffic controls and security during the festival.

The meadow is divided into zones: large tents run by Munich breweries, smaller tents and stalls run by other operators, and rides and merchandise along the main paths. Tents have capacity limits, and popular sessions require reservations or early arrival. Visitors without a booking may try at quieter times but are not guaranteed entry. Large luggage and glass bottles are banned, and the rules are enforced by the organisers and security staff.

## Beer, tents and the tapping

Only breweries within the Munich city boundary may serve beer in the tents. The usual names are Augustiner, Hacker-Pschorr, Hofbräu, Löwenbräu, Paulaner and Spaten. The restriction is the product of history and local protection, and it gives Oktoberfest beer a clear Munich identity.

At noon on the opening day the mayor taps the first barrel in the Schottenhamel tent and shouts “O'zapft is!”, “it is tapped”, and the festival begins. Beer is usually sold by the litre, the Maß, and the strength and recipe vary by brewery. Beyond beer there are roast chicken, pork knuckle, sausages, sauerkraut, gingerbread and sweets. Traditional dress is not compulsory, but Dirndl and Lederhosen are common and have become part of the visual identity.

## Who organises it, who earns

Oktoberfest is organised by the City of Munich's festival department together with Bavarian breweries and tent operators. The city handles the site, transport, security, sanitation and permits; breweries and tent operators handle food, service and the tents; rides and stalls enter through tenders. The structure is neither purely commercial nor purely public; it is a hybrid municipal festival.

Revenue comes from beer and food sales, rides, stall rents and sponsorship. Costs include construction, security, cleaning, transport and staff. The festival visibly boosts Munich tourism, hotels, restaurants and transport, but it also brings noise, waste, congestion and intoxication. The city therefore balances “open celebration” against “order management” every year.

## Why it became a global symbol

Oktoberfest's influence has three layers. First, scale: it is one of the world's largest folk festivals, with millions of visitors, and its tents, fairground and parades form a complete municipal-festival template. Second, image: beer, Lederhosen, brass bands, roast chicken and “Prost” have become a global picture of Bavarian culture, copied by Oktoberfests elsewhere. Third, economy: it ties local breweries, tourism and the city brand together.

The image has a cost. Much of the “Bavarian tradition” seen by visitors is a festival version; rural customs, brewing craft and everyday local life are not the same thing. Oktoberfest is an entry point, not the whole.

## Boundaries and common misreadings

First, Oktoberfest is not “all-you-can-drink”. Tents have capacity and time management, drunken disruption is removed, and security and police are present. Second, not every beer may enter the tents: only Munich breweries qualify, and outside or foreign brands cannot simply set up. Third, it is not a nationwide German holiday. It is mainly a Munich event; other cities have similar Volksfeste, but they are not the same festival. Fourth, dates change annually; 19 September is not a permanent opening day. Fifth, commercialisation is heavy, and tent prices, sponsorship and tour packages shape the experience, so it is not a “pure folk” event.

Other common misreadings: treating Oktoberfest as a beer-quality competition; treating the Wiesn as the name of one tent; treating the tapping as a purely symbolic act with no supply behind it. A more accurate reading is that Oktoberfest is Munich's civic festival, with beer as its central symbol but not its entire content.
