# Champagne flute

A Champagne flute is a stemmed glass with a tall, narrow body and a slightly narrowed rim. The body rises from the foot in a long tube or a gentle taper, and the rim is usually a little narrower than the middle. It has long been treated as the standard Champagne glass, but that "standard" is a matter of habit and occasion rather than the only correct choice.

## What the glass is

The defining features are tall and narrow. The height is clearly greater than the rim diameter, the walls run nearly vertical or taper gently, and the base is small. The stem and foot lift the glass off the table so that the fingers can hold the stem without touching the body.

It differs from the shallow, wide coupe and from the wide-bellied, narrowed tulip. The flute gives the liquid a long, narrow space and a relatively small surface in contact with air; the slightly narrowed rim concentrates aroma to some degree, though less than a strongly closed shape.

The flute is commonly associated with Champagne, other sparkling wines and drinks where bubbles are the focus. The shape does not restrict the drink, but its structure makes it better suited to some drinks than to others.

## How shape affects aroma and temperature

The tall, narrow body first affects bubbles. A narrow body and a small liquid surface give bubbles a smaller free surface to escape from, so carbonation is relatively easier to keep. Fine scratches on the wall and the base can provide nucleation points, and bubbles rise along the wall in a long, thin line.

The narrow body also affects aroma. With a small surface and a narrow rim, the area from which aroma can volatilise is small and the nose has to come close to the rim. Aroma is held within a certain range, but there is limited room to open; for Champagne built on fruit, floral and yeast notes, some tasters find a flute too restrictive.

For temperature, the stem reduces hand heat and the tall body keeps the surface away from the rim, so the liquid is somewhat less affected by the environment. But thin walls and a small capacity still mean the drink warms over time. A flute is not usually used for drinks over ice, so temperature is controlled mainly by pre-chilling and serving temperature.

The narrowed rim has another effect: the liquid flows towards the middle of the mouth, making bubbles more obvious, while aroma does not spread as widely as in an open glass. This is why the flute is often described as putting bubbles before aroma.

## Capacity and suitable drinks

There is no single capacity standard, and body width and height vary widely. This page gives no fixed capacity figure; capacity depends on the specific product. Adjust the pour and the pouring height to your own glass so that the drink is not too full for nosing and bubbles.

It suits Champagne and [sparkling wine](ref:sparkling) best, especially when bubble persistence and a crisp texture are the focus. Most non-vintage and vintage Champagnes from the [Champagne](ref:champagne) region can be served in a flute, though a wider, narrowed-rim shape may be better when detailed aroma is the goal.

It also suits other drinks built around bubbles, such as refreshing drinks made with sparkling water or sparkling wine. Drinks with a lot of pulp, drinks over ice or drinks that need to stay cold for a long time are poor matches: the narrow body holds little ice and the rim limits garnish and stirring.

The same sparkling wine behaves differently in different flutes. The narrower the body and rim, the slower bubbles escape; a slightly wider body and rim open the aroma more but let bubbles fade faster. Choose according to whether bubbles or aroma matter more to you.

## Material and cleaning

Flutes are commonly made of glass or crystal glass. Ordinary glass is durable and costs less; crystal is thinner and more brilliant but breaks more easily. Thin walls are sensitive to temperature change and impact, so avoid extreme hot-to-cold washing.

Hand washing is recommended, with warm water and a little neutral detergent, thorough rinsing and upside-down drying. In a tall narrow body, detergent easily collects where the base meets the wall, so rinse an extra time. Do not wipe the inside repeatedly with a cloth, which leaves fibres and odour and may knock the rim.

Store without stacking or squeezing. The rim is thin and the body tall, so lying them down concentrates stress; a rack or enough shelf space is better. The joint between stem and foot is a weak point, so hold the stem or foot rather than the body.

## How to choose

First, look at body width. A very narrow flute leaves almost no room for aroma and the experience tends towards sparkling water; a slightly wider body balances bubbles and aroma.

Second, look at how much the rim narrows. A slight narrowing helps concentrate aroma, but too much makes nosing difficult and cleaning awkward.

Third, look at the stem and foot. The stem should be comfortable, and the foot should be steady; a small foot tips easily.

Fourth, look at wall thickness and evenness. Thin walls feel lighter but are less durable, and uneven walls affect appearance.

Fifth, check the capacity against your use. Capacity depends on the specific product and should match the pour, garnish and room for nosing.

Sixth, consider storage and washing. A flute takes space, and cramped storage raises the risk of breakage.

Do not ignore the actual experience just because "Champagne should be served in a flute". If aroma matters more, a tulip or a wider glass may be better.

## Alternative glasses

The [tulip glass](ref:tulip) is a common alternative: a wider bowl with a slightly narrowed rim gives aroma more room and still leaves space for bubbles, a compromise between the two.

The [coupe](ref:coupe) suits occasions that emphasise aroma and a retro presentation, but bubbles fade faster; if bubbles are the point, it is not the first choice.

Wider wine glasses can also hold Champagne, especially for aroma-focused tasting, but the larger the glass, the faster bubbles fade and the drink warms. For general principles of glass selection, see [choosing a glass](ref:glass).

## Common misconceptions

First, "Champagne can only be served in a flute". A flute is common but not the only correct shape; aroma-first occasions can use a tulip or a wider glass.

Second, "the narrower the body the better the bubbles". Extreme narrowness sacrifices aroma, and bubble retention is not a case where more is always better; balance the two.

Third, "a flute keeps bubbles forever". Bubbles keep escaping with time and temperature; the shape can slow this, not stop it.

Fourth, "all flutes hold the same volume". Capacities differ widely, and capacity depends on the specific product, so adjust the pour to your glass.

Fifth, "a flute is only for Champagne". Other sparkling wines and bubble-focused drinks can use it too; the key is whether the structure matches.

Sixth, "thinner walls are always more refined". Thin walls break more easily and demand more care in washing and storage; durability is also a selection factor.
