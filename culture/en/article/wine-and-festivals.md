# Wine and festivals: the calendar inside seasonal drinks

Traditional Chinese festivals are not abstract dates but sets of actions: changing clothes, honouring ancestors, eating together, going out, drinking a particular wine. Wine works as a time marker; what you drink, when and with whom reminds you where you are in the year.

This article reads four festivals. Each wine carries medicinal, ritual and social meanings at once, and the balance among them differs by period.![Wine and festivals: the calendar inside seasonal drinks thematic illustration](/media/photos/theme-beer.jpg)

## New Year: pepper and cypress wine, and tusu wine

New Year drinking is recorded early. In the Han period people drank pepper and cypress wine on the first day, steeping Sichuan pepper and cypress leaves in wine for protection and long life. Later the more common drink was [tusu wine](ref:new-year-wine): medicinal herbs such as rhubarb, atractylodes and cassia bark were put in a silk bag, hung in a well or steeped in wine on New Year's Eve, and drunk on the first day.

Tusu had a distinctive order: the young drank first and the old last, the reverse of normal seniority. The explanation was that the young gain a year while the old lose one, so the young should receive the New Year's blessing first. Such details show that festival wine is not only a recipe but a statement about time.

Homemade medicinal wine needs care today: the source of herbs, alcohol strength and steeping time all affect safety, and old recipes are not medical advice.

## Dragon Boat: realgar wine and calamus

The Dragon Boat Festival falls in the fifth lunar month. Traditional practices include drinking realgar wine, sprinkling realgar water, and hanging calamus and mugwort. Realgar is an arsenic-bearing mineral that ancients believed could repel snakes and insects and ward off evil. It is not suitable for drinking: arsenic compounds are toxic, modern medicine does not recommend internal use, and external use also requires caution.

When discussing Dragon Boat wine today, the emphasis should be on why the custom formed, not on encouraging replication. Calamus and mugwort wines also appear in some regional records as seasonal drinks, but here too symbolic meaning must be separated from actual safety.

The Record of the Seasons of Jing and Chu also notes that on the fifth day of the fifth month people cut mugwort into human figures and hung them over the door, and steeped calamus in wine. Early on, mugwort and calamus were the main actors; realgar gained weight only later in regional records. Calamus pungency comes from essential oils, and the ancients read it as protection and cleansing, not as modern disinfection.

## Mid-Autumn: osmanthus wine and new wine

Mid-Autumn drinking is almost bound to moon viewing. Meng Yuanlao records in Dongjing Meng Hua Lu that before Mid-Autumn in Bianjing “all the taverns sold new wine” and townspeople competed to drink it, with wine houses redecorated and open all night. Mid-Autumn drinking was therefore first a commercial seasonal peak, and only then an emotional symbol of family reunion.

Osmanthus wine is a common Mid-Autumn choice, infused or steeped with osmanthus flowers for a fresh, sweet aroma. It is more a seasonal flavour than a strict medicinal wine. In various regions people also drink grape or rice wine under the moon; there is no national rule linking the festival to one drink.

[Li Qingzhao](ref:li-qingzhao) writes of Double Ninth, “holding wine at the eastern fence after dusk”, placing flowers, wine and longing together. In poets' hands, seasonal wine stresses emotion more than recipe.

## Double Ninth: chrysanthemum wine

Double Ninth falls on the ninth day of the ninth lunar month, with customs of climbing heights, wearing zhuyu, and drinking chrysanthemum wine. Recipes appear in texts such as the Xijing Zaji and Suishi Guangji: chrysanthemum flowers and stems are mixed with millet to brew wine, drunk the following ninth month. It carries the meaning of prolonging life.

The social function is clear: this is a festival of respect for elders and reunion. Today chrysanthemum wine appears more often as a flavoured wine or gift box; the old medicinal claims have greatly weakened.

Dongjing Meng Hua Lu adds two details. Before Mid-Autumn new wine sold so well that around noon “every house had no wine” and taverns pulled down their wine flags; at Double Ninth, Bianjing taverns tied chrysanthemums into archways called “chrysanthemum doorways”, joining flower viewing to wine selling. Together they show custom and commerce.

## The common structure of seasonal wines

First, fixed time: the wine is tied to the season and not drunk afterwards. Second, seasonal ingredients: osmanthus, chrysanthemum and calamus are all in season. Third, complete actions: not only drinking but picking, steeping, hanging and giving. Fourth, layered meanings: protection, health, respect for elders and reunion are stacked on one another.

This structure makes wine a repeatable ritual. Even when a recipe is lost, the actions and names survive, as with mooncakes and wine at Mid-Autumn or family toasts at New Year.

## Festival wine today: what to keep and what to drop

What can be kept is the sense of season and the function of reunion: choose a wine that suits the food at Mid-Autumn, prepare a low-strength drink for the family at New Year, and eat with elders at Double Ninth. What should be dropped is putting toxic minerals into wine, treating old recipes as medical treatment, and using toasts to create pressure.

The value of festival wine is not “ancient method” but its reminder to live by the calendar. Understanding where it comes from allows selective inheritance.
