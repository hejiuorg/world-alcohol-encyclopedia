# Fruit Brandy / Eau-de-vie

Fruit brandy, or eau-de-vie (literally "water of life" in French), is a clear spirit made by fermenting fresh fruit or juice and then distilling it. Unlike grape-based [brandy](ref:brandy) or pomace distillates such as marc, its raw material is whole fruit or juice, and the aroma comes from the fruit itself. Common styles include kirsch (cherry), Poire Williams (pear), mirabelle (golden plum), framboise (raspberry), slivovitz (plum) and the German-speaking Obstler. Most bottlings sit at 40–50% ABV, usually not oak-aged, selling on fresh fruit aroma.

## Definition and boundaries

Under category 9 (fruit spirit) of Annex I to EU Regulation 2019/787, this class may be produced only by alcoholic fermentation and distillation of fresh fleshy fruit, berries or their must, with or without stones. Every distillation must run below 86% vol so the distillate keeps the raw material's aroma. The regulation requires at least 200 grams of volatile substances per hectolitre of 100% vol alcohol; stone-fruit spirits must keep hydrocyanic acid below 7 grams per hectolitre of 100% vol alcohol; the legal minimum strength is 37.5% vol.

Fruit spirits may not be coloured, flavoured or topped up with added alcohol. A little sweetening is allowed to round off the taste, capped at 18 grams per litre expressed as invert sugar. The legal name is "spirit" plus the fruit name. "Williams" is reserved for pear spirit made solely from Williams pears, and "Obstler" requires at least 85% of the mash from apple, pear or both.

Neighbouring categories stay distinct. Marc uses pressed pomace rather than juice; Geist macerates unfermented fruit in agricultural-origin alcohol before distillation; fruit liqueurs are blended with sugar and flavourings at lower strength and obvious sweetness. The [distillation](ref:distillation) route determines the legal category on the label.

## History and naming

In English-speaking usage, eau-de-vie refers to clear distillates made from fruit other than grapes. Continental European distilling traditions grew out of surplus berry and stone-fruit harvests: fruit did not keep well, so fermentation and distillation concentrated the value of excess crop. Village distilleries took root across German-speaking lands, the Alpine region and the Balkans, each with its own fruit repertoire.

The Black Forest in Germany is regarded as the birthplace of kirsch, built on long cultivation of dark morello cherries. Alsace in France has its cherry route, the Route du Pays du Kirsch, and Switzerland and South Tyrol also make high-quality kirsch. Protected examples include Kirsch de Fougerolles in France, Zuger Kirsch and Rigi Kirsch in Switzerland; Valais pear brandy in Switzerland is an AOP restricted to Williams pears.

Label fruit names map to fixed raw materials: kirsch cherry, slivovitz plum, mirabelle golden plum, framboise raspberry, Poire Williams pear. Through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, household distilling moved into commercial distilleries under tax and hygiene rules. What survived is the palate direction of a fresh-aroma single-fruit spirit, not one fixed recipe.

## Materials and process

The fruit must be ripe and flavour-concentrated. Stone fruit is usually fermented with crushed stones, which add a light almond note; berries give little juice and carry delicate aromas, so fruit input is high. For kirsch, roughly 10 kilograms of cherries go into one 700–750 millilitre bottle.

After fermentation the mash goes slowly into a copper pot still, where the distiller separates heads, heart and tails and keeps only the middle heart. Many traditional producers run a second distillation to narrow the flavour further. Holding distillation below 86% vol keeps aromatic compounds in the distillate instead of producing near-neutral alcohol.

Most finished spirits rest in stainless steel, glass, earthenware or wax-lined vessels without touching oak; caramel colouring is permitted only for spirits aged at least one year in wood. Stuck fermentation, contamination, poor cuts or uncontrolled storage can turn good fruit into a faulty spirit with solvent, cooked-fruit or sour notes.

## Style and palate

A typical fruit brandy is clear and bright, dry to off-dry, with fresh fruit, floral and light stone-almond aromas: cherry leans to sour cherry and almond, pear to ripe pear and flowers, raspberry to berry, plum to stone fruit and nut. Market bottlings are commonly 40–50% ABV, above the 37.5% floor; good examples bring fruit forward before the spirit, with a clean, non-cloying finish.

Faults are easy to read. Pungent solvent means poorly cut heads; dull cooked fruit comes from overheated fermentation or distillation; thin watery taste usually points to low-grade fruit. Unlike oak-aged grape brandies, most fruit brandies are meant young, and fruit aroma fades with long storage. Only a few wood- or earthenware-rested versions develop slow, integrated complexity.

Regional styles differ in fine ways: Alsace is aromatic and refined; Black Forest kirsch carries stone and almond structure; Austrian and South Tyrolean Obstler leans to apple and pear freshness; Balkan slivovitz is built on plum and nut; Swiss pear spirit is smoother. When comparing, fix fruit type and strength first, then read region and producer.

## Representative regions and brands

[France](ref:france)'s Alsace is the best-known French-speaking district, where family distilleries such as Massenez, Nusbaumer and Willm keep cherry, pear, raspberry and mirabelle lines. Germany's Black Forest is known for cherry water and apple-pear Obstler from producers such as Schladerer. Swiss Valais, Austria, South Tyrol and the Balkans produce protected pear spirit, Obstler and plum distillate respectively.

Representative products include Poire Williams, Kirsch, Mirabelle, Framboise, Slivovitz and Obstler. Price spreads are driven by fruit dosage, raw-material grade, protected origin and small-batch production. Single-fruit, high-dosage small bottlings carry premium pricing, while entry blends are easier to find; one expensive benchmark bottle does not represent the whole category.

## How to drink and pair

Fruit brandies are usually served after the meal as a digestif, in about 30–60 millilitres. Valais official guidance serves its pear brandy chilled at 8–12 °C; cherry and berry versions suit a cool serving, since warmth lets alcohol overrun the aroma. A small tulip or narrow spirit glass works best. They are mostly drunk neat, though a few lighter examples go into cocktails.

For food, they close out a meal with dessert: over sorbet, with fruit cake, mousse, chocolate or candied fruit. Kirsch is the traditional ingredient adding alcoholic depth to desserts such as Black Forest gateau. In cooking it can deglaze pans or accompany fruit dishes in place of some white wine. Pairing lets the fruit echo the dish's sweetness, rather than letting a heavy sauce overpower the spirit.

## Buying, storage and misreadings

On the label look for fruit name, origin, alcohol strength and whether sweetening was added. A specific fruit name should mean that fruit is the raw material, and an AOP or PGI place name must meet the corresponding rules. Being colourless is the normal state of the category, not a sign of low quality; age statements are rare, and new bottlings are usually ready to drink. "Handmade" and "traditional" are marketing words to read with raw material, strength and producer.

Store away from light, heat and vibration, upright and sealed. High alcohol lets an opened bottle keep longer than low-alcohol drinks, but fruit aroma fades over the years, so finish an opened bottle within about a year. Common mistakes are reading colour depth as age—most samples never touch wood—and treating sweetness as quality; the regulation allows only limited sweetening, and dry is the traditional baseline.
