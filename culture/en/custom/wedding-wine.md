# Wedding Wine Customs: From Hejin to the Cross-Cup Drink

The “cross-cup drink” at weddings today is often traced back to the pre-Qin “hejin”. The two are indeed related, but they cannot simply be equated: hejin was one specific step in the wedding rite, while the cross-cup drink developed many forms in later periods. Clarifying this line helps explain wine's place in a wedding and prevents modern wedding-industry language from being mistaken for ancient ritual.

## What hejin is

A jin is a gourd ladle. The old commentary says that a gourd was split into two halves to make two ladles, one held by each spouse for drinking: this is hejin. It is not two cups tied together with red string, nor the modern arm-linked drinking; its core is one vessel divided into two, with each person holding one half to complete a shared drink.

The old commentary explains the act as “joining bodies and levelling high and low, in order to draw close”. Two separate individuals, by sharing one vessel and completing one action, enter a marriage relationship. Wine is not the protagonist here; the vessel and the action are. Yet without wine the action cannot be completed.

## The three ceremonial drinks in the Yili

The Yili, Shi Hun Li, records in detail the wedding procedure of the pre-Qin shi class. On the wedding night, after the couple eat, there is a sequence of yin: the assistant washes a cup, pours wine, first offers it to the groom and then to the bride, repeating three times; the third time “uses a jin”. In other words, the first two drinks use ordinary vessels and only the third switches to the split gourd.

This detail matters. Hejin is not a casual cup but the third step in a whole ritual sequence, carrying a sense of completion and confirmation. The finer the procedure, the more clearly the ancients took the wedding seriously: wine here is a vehicle of ritual, not an ornament of festivity.

The Shi Hun Li also records “eating from the same lao”, meaning the couple share the meat from one sacrificial vessel. Shared food and shared drink belong together and form the symbol of “levelling high and low”. With this layer in view, hejin cannot be pulled out and treated as an isolated wedding performance.

## The explanation in the Liji

The Liji, Hun Yi, summarises the step in one sentence: “When the bride arrives, the groom bows and leads her in; they eat from the same lao and drink from the hejin, thus joining bodies and levelling high and low in order to draw close.” It places hejin within the larger frame of “joining the good of two surnames”: a wedding serves the ancestral temple above and continues the family line below, so every action must be “reverent, careful, correct and solemn”.

The phrase “levelling high and low” is easily misread. It does not say that husband and wife had identical status; it says that at the moment of hejin, the couple use one vessel and one action to complete a shared drink, symbolising their entry into one family order. The ancient wedding as a whole still contained clear roles and hierarchy; the “sameness” of hejin is sameness within that structure.

This also explains why wine in a wedding is often given the meaning of “completing the rite”: it is not simply celebration but the public, ritualised confirmation of a relationship. At the moment the wine is drunk, the social meaning of the marriage is affirmed.

## From hejin to the cross-cup drink

The later “cross-cup drink” has roots in hejin, but its form changed greatly. Common versions include drinking with linked arms, exchanging cups, or mixing the two cups before drinking; practice differed by period and region. Treating any one version as “how it has always been” ignores the complexity of change.

The word hejin also came to stand for marriage itself, as in “the joy of hejin”. This is an extension of meaning, not proof that the ancient rite of split-gourd drinking survived at every wedding. Continuity in language and survival in ritual must be kept apart.

In fiction and opera, wedding wine is often written with warmth and detail. The banquets and wine etiquette in [Dream of the Red Chamber](ref:dream-of-the-red-chamber) offer a reference point, but a novel is literary description, not an operating manual for Qing wedding customs.

## The functions of wine in a wedding

The first is completing the rite. Wine turns private feeling into public ceremony; with relatives and friends present, raising a cup declares that the relationship exists. The second is connection. The couple toast their elders, guests bless the newlyweds, and wine circulates through the room, linking the networks of two families.

The third is witnessing. Wedding wine often appears together with vows, blessings and seating order; who drinks first, who accompanies whom and how much is drunk all follow local rules. The fourth is celebration. Wine brings a banquet into a lively state, but the boundary of that liveliness is still set by ritual and by capacity.

These four functions do not always appear together. A small wedding may keep only the toasts, while a large banquet may include several customs. Understanding function is more useful than memorising “how many cups are required”.

## Regional and period differences

The pre-Qin ritual books describe the wedding of the shi class, not the practice of every social group. Later popular weddings added local beliefs, ethnic habits and economic conditions to the ritual model, and the drinks, vessels and procedure all changed.

The type of wine changed with the times too. Ancient weddings mainly used grain ferments; today a wedding may use baijiu, yellow wine, grape wine or mixed drinks. Equating hejin directly with one kind of wine confuses ritual with beverage. For the craft background of grain wine, see [yellow wine](ref:yellow-wine).

Urban and rural customs, and those of different ethnic groups, vary widely. Some places emphasise toasting, others teasing and drinking games, others substituting tea for wine. Comparative study should state place, group and period; one “traditional Chinese wedding” cannot cover them all.

## Common misreadings

First, equating the cross-cup drink with hejin. They are related, but the vessels, actions and ritual context differ.

Second, describing hejin as two cups tied with red thread. The ancient rite centres on a gourd split into two ladles, one held by each, not on modern wedding props.

Third, taking “levelling high and low” to mean that ancient husbands and wives were fully equal. It describes a shared action in the rite, not equality of social status overall.

Fourth, treating weddings in novels, film and television as historical sources. Literature can reflect lived experience, but it cannot replace ritual books, local gazetteers and fieldwork.

## How to view wedding wine customs today

A wedding today can keep the symbolism of hejin without reproducing the whole ancient rite. What matters is understanding what the action means: sharing one vessel, completing one action, and confirming the relationship before relatives and friends. Modern vessels can support an equivalent rite without pretending to be pre-Qin.

Drinking safety applies as well. The cross-cup drink need not make “drinking more” a proof of sincerity, and toasting should not become coercion. The original point of ritual is “reverent, careful, correct and solemn”, not getting people drunk. Explaining this is more valuable than arguing about “which cup counts as the cross-cup drink”.

For writers, wedding wine customs are easily reduced to a pile of auspicious phrases. A safer method is to state the source first, then the period and region, and only then discuss symbolic meaning. This keeps the warmth of the rite without sacrificing the boundary of fact. For a longer view of wine in season and ritual, read [Time in a Cup](ref:time-in-a-cup).
