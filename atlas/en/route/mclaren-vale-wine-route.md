# McLaren Vale Wine Route

A day trip from Adelaide to McLaren Vale: first d’Arenberg for century-old vines and the Cube, then Wirra Wirra for an 1894 stone cellar and Church Block, with a meal and a stretch of coast in between. The loop is about 80 kilometres; the point is not how many cellar doors you collect but how history, architecture, varieties and sea air compare within one day.

## Route overview

Leave central Adelaide at 9:00, take the Southern Expressway south and reach d’Arenberg around 9:45. Spend two hours between the Cube and the cellar, focusing on old-vine Shiraz, basket pressing and the building; have lunch at d’Arry’s Verandah at 12:00 or back in McLaren Vale town. At 13:30 drive ten minutes to Wirra Wirra for a tasting and cellar visit of about ninety minutes. After 15:30, add Coriole or Samuel’s Gorge as a third stop, or drive to Sellicks Beach for the coast; return at 17:30 and be back in Adelaide around 18:15.

This route suits a first visit to South Australia because it sets two faces of McLaren Vale side by side: d’Arenberg’s contemporary architecture plus old vines, and Wirra Wirra’s nineteenth-century stone cellar plus modern blends. On a repeat visit, swap the third stop for a smaller estate, or give the afternoon to Willunga and the beach.

## Regional background

McLaren Vale lies about 35 kilometres south of Adelaide, with the Lofty Ranges to the east and St Vincent Gulf to the west, making it one of South Australia’s oldest and most coastal wine regions. The Mediterranean climate brings hot, dry summers, while afternoon gully winds from the Sellicks Hills drop vineyard temperatures by a few degrees; soils run from sand and ironstone gravel to limestone clay, and the same variety can ripen a week or more apart depending on aspect. Many of the old Shiraz, Grenache and Mourvèdre vines date from the nineteenth century, giving the region one of Australia’s densest concentrations of old vines.

## Stop one: d’Arenberg

D’Arenberg is at 58 Osborn Road, the natural first stop from Adelaide. Joseph Osborn bought the established vineyard in 1912, the cellars went up in 1928, and fourth-generation Chester Osborn took over winemaking in 1984; today the estate has more than seventy wines and thirty-plus varieties and is one of Australia’s largest certified organic and biodynamic growers.

Confirm your bookings first: the Cube visit, the Blending Bench and the restaurant are three separate products, and walk-ins usually get only the basic tasting. The Cube has five levels, with the tasting room on top and the upper two rotating; if time is short, taste upstairs first, then go down to the cellar to see the basket presses and old-vine photographs. The Dali exhibition is inside the Cube; with children, keep hands off the works.

Focus the tasting on three wines: Dead Arm Shiraz for old-vine concentration, The Coppermine Road Cabernet for structure, and The Ironstone Pressings GSM for spice and red fruit. With one bottle to take away, Dead Arm is the safest choice; to see the estate’s range, add a Marsanne-Viognier or a sweet wine.

## Stop two: Wirra Wirra

Wirra Wirra is at 255 Strout Road, about ten minutes from d’Arenberg. In 1894 Robert Strangways Wigley built the cellar from local ironstone and made the first vintage in 1897; the winery closed in 1936, and Greg and Roger Trott restored the ruin in 1969 before releasing the first Church Block in 1972. The old cellar still hosts tastings and events and is itself a historical specimen of the region.

The appeal here is “old cellar, new blends”: Church Block mixes Cabernet, Shiraz and Merlot; RSW Shiraz is named after the founder; The Angelus is the flagship Shiraz; Chook Block comes from an old-vine block; Scrubby Rise and Catapult are the more everyday tiers. Start with Scrubby Rise as a baseline, then move to Church Block and RSW; the differences are concentration, oak and ageing potential.

The cellar visit is short but worth asking which walls are 1894 and which were rebuilt. The stone, iron doors and old pressing equipment show what happened between the 1936 closure and the 1969 restoration; those questions are more rewarding than another photograph of a stone wall.

## Lunch and a third stop

Lunch has two options: d’Arry’s Verandah at d’Arenberg, seasonal and needing a booking in peak season, or one of the restaurants in McLaren Vale town for something lighter. If you are driving afterwards, do not drink much at lunch; Australia enforces a strict limit, and spitting at tastings is normal.

For a third stop, choose by interest: Coriole for more varieties and olive groves, Samuel’s Gorge for a smaller and quieter estate, or Sellicks Beach and Aldinga Beach for the coast — park on the Esplanade and walk a stretch of sand. McLaren Vale has thirty kilometres of coastline, and afternoon light is kinder to photographs than another cellar door.

With children, swap the third stop for the coast or old Willunga rather than more wine; most children last about an hour among cellars and vines. Willunga has a Saturday farmers’ market that fits a morning or early afternoon.

## Seasons and roads

McLaren Vale is Mediterranean: hot and dry in summer, hottest in February and March; harvest usually runs from late February to April. Harvest is the busiest time, tasting slots are tight, and trucks and picking equipment appear on back roads, so drive slowly. Winters are mild and wet, estates are quieter, but the coast is windy — bring a jacket.

Allow about 45 minutes from Adelaide to d’Arenberg, 10 minutes from d’Arenberg to Wirra Wirra, and 50 minutes back to Adelaide; add 40 minutes for the coast. Friday afternoons and weekends are busy, and the Southern Expressway clogs on the way out of the city; avoid the 17:00–18:00 return peak if you can.

## Booking and etiquette

Book the Cube, Blending Bench and restaurant at d’Arenberg, and confirm tasting and cellar visits at Wirra Wirra. Neither estate handles large walk-in groups well; for more than six people, email ahead. Spit into the spit bucket and do not take the tasting glass; stairs, stone floors and vineyard tracks call for proper shoes.

This route does not draw a map or provide live prices; roads, hours and booking rules change, so check both estate websites and McLaren Vale Wine before leaving. If an estate closes unexpectedly, do not force it — go to an alternative cellar or the coast and the day still works.
