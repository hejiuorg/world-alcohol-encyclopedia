# The Nationalisation of Cuban Rum: From the Arechabala Family to Two Havana Clubs

Havana Club is the best-known Cuban rum brand and the subject of one of the longest-running trademark disputes in the spirits trade. Its story is told in two versions: the Cuban government and Pernod Ricard stress nationalisation after the revolution and global success, while the exiled Arechabala family and Bacardi stress uncompensated expropriation and a "stolen" brand. Both versions rest on the same events — the brand's creation in 1934, the 1959 revolution, the 1960 nationalisation, the 1993 joint venture and the 1997 trademark transfer — but characterise them in completely different ways.

## Date and place

The brand began in Cardenas, in Matanzas Province, Cuba. In 1862 the Spanish immigrant José Arechabala arrived in Cuba; in 1878 he founded a distillery and sugar refinery called La Vizcaya, which later became one of the town's largest employers. In 1934 the family launched Havana Club rum in Cardenas. After the 1959 revolution the business was taken over by the revolutionary government; from the 1960s production and export were concentrated in Havana under a state agency. After 1993 the Cuban Havana Club exported worldwide from Havana, while the US market was supplied by a different "Havana Club" made by Bacardi because of the embargo.

## Background: sugar, rum and Havana in the Prohibition era

Rum is a by-product of sugar cane farming and sugar refining. In the second half of the 19th century Cuba produced an important share of the world's sugar; at the same time, Cuban distillers were early users of the column still, making a lighter rum better suited to mixing than traditional pot-still spirits. In the early 20th century Havana became a destination for American tourists and bartenders; during US Prohibition (1920–1933) large numbers travelled south to Cuba, fostering the local cocktail style of the Cantineros, the Havana bartenders, and bringing rum cocktails to international attention.

In 1934, just after Prohibition was repealed, the Arechabala family launched Havana Club as a premium rum aimed at the American market, using double ageing. For the next two decades it became one of the symbols of Cuba's "golden age" tourism and nightlife. Before the revolution the family also operated sugar, rum, candy and barrel businesses, and was a pillar of Cardenas.

## Course: revolution, nationalisation and state export

On 1 January 1959 Batista fled and Castro entered Havana, and the revolutionary government began its political, economic and social transformation. As American and Cuban businesses were successively taken over, the Arechabala family's operations in Cardenas were also seized; Ramón Arechabala was forced to leave Cuba around 1963 and eventually settled in Miami. In 1960 the government continued producing Havana Club through Cubaexport, gradually removing the family crest and adopting a new brand identity.

In 1966 the Cuban government created Cubaexport to handle alcohol exports and trademark registration; Cuba subsequently registered the Havana Club trademark in many countries. In the early 1970s the brand adopted a new logo built around the sun and the Giraldilla, the statue that is the emblem of Havana. In 1973 the family's US trademark lapsed for lack of renewal, while the family lawyer in Cuba was in prison and unable to act.

In 1991 the collapse of the Soviet Union deprived Cuba of its main economic partner, and the government sought foreign investment. Cubaexport and the French company Pernod Ricard began negotiations, and in 1993 they formally created the joint venture Havana Club International S.A., owned equally by Pernod Ricard and the state-owned Corporación Cuba Ron, to sell Cuban-made Havana Club worldwide except in the United States. The partnership expanded the brand rapidly and brought the conflict between the "Cuban" and "exile" versions into the open.

## Impact and institutions

First, state monopoly. After the revolution, Cuban alcohol production and export were concentrated in state bodies, and Havana Club became a national brand whose output, recipe and export channels were managed by state agencies and the joint venture.

Second, a dual trademark structure. In 1997 Ramón Arechabala sold the family's US trademark rights and recipe to Bacardi; in the same year the Pernod Ricard-Cuban venture sued Bacardi for trademark infringement in a New York federal court. Bacardi then produced and sold "Havana Club" in the United States, while the Cuban version could not enter that market because of the US embargo.

Third, law entangled with politics. The US embargo on Cuba, the Helms-Burton Act and US trademark procedures made the case more than a commercial dispute; administrative decisions in different administrations repeatedly changed the course of renewals and litigation.

Fourth, cultural branding. The Cuban version tied Havana Club to Cuban music, bartender competitions and the rum museum, creating a national narrative of "Cuban rum"; the exile version based its legitimacy on family inheritance and the original recipe, that is, on "expropriated property".

## Sources and disputes

First, the timing and nature of nationalisation are described differently. The family side presents the takeover as expropriation between 1959 and 1960, while Cuban and joint-venture material stresses post-revolutionary nationalisation and continued production; the exact decree dates differ between accounts.

Second, compensation is a disputed point. The Arechabala family and US interests claim no compensation was paid; Cuba argues that nationalisation followed the law of the time and that the trademark and investment were subsequently taken up by the Cuban state system. Neither side has published a complete asset settlement.

Third, the joint-venture year appears as both 1991 and 1993. 1991 is the start of negotiations and cooperation, and 1993 is the formal creation of the company; mixing the two produces a false timeline.

Fourth, the "legality" of the trademark depends on the jurisdiction. Cuba registered the brand in many countries, while the United States long refused to recognise the Cuban government's renewal; the same name is used by different parties in different markets and cannot simply be judged "real" or "fake".

Fifth, the main sources are partisan. The Bacardi-affiliated site and the Cuban-Pernod Ricard site each tell the version favourable to itself; independent studies rely on court files and trademark records, but general readers cannot verify every detail.
