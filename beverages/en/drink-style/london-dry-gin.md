# London dry gin

London dry gin is the most representative dry style of [gin](ref:gin). Its core is not production in London but a process constraint: flavour must come from redistribution, juniper must dominate, and sugar and colouring are strictly limited. The name is a category concept, not proof of origin.

## Definition and regulation

EU spirit regulation sets specific requirements for London gin: it is redistilled from neutral alcohol with juniper and other botanicals, flavour comes mainly from distillation, and aroma cannot be created mainly by adding essences afterwards; it may not be coloured; sugar is used only to round the finish and may not exceed 0.1 grams per litre; the minimum alcohol content is 37.5% ABV.

The regulation also separates distilled gin from compound gin. London dry gin carries stricter constraints, so the category word on the label has real meaning. Its difference from [vodka](ref:vodka) lies in the flavour goal: vodka aims at neutrality, while London dry gin aims at juniper-centred botanical identity.

## History

Gin grew out of genever in the Netherlands and Belgium, a grain distillate flavoured with juniper. In the 17th century it reached Britain and gradually became drier and more botanical. In the 18th century London saw large-scale cheap gin consumption, which brought social problems and licensing controls, and shaped the cultural image of gin as an urban drink.

In the 19th century continuous distillation and more stable mashing improved base spirit quality, and London dry gin gradually became an export standard. In the late 20th century gin consumption fluctuated, but the Martini and gin and tonic kept it relevant; the craft gin wave of the 2000s brought more botanical combinations and regional styles.

## Botanicals

London dry gin centres on juniper, commonly with coriander seed, angelica, orris root, citrus peel, liquorice, cardamom and cassia. Juniper gives pine and resin, coriander gives citrus and spice, angelica and orris give earth and floral notes, and citrus peel gives bright fruit.

Recipes are usually not fully disclosed, and the number of botanicals on a label is marketing information rather than a substitute for judging balance. Juniper must be clearly recognisable, or the product is closer to flavoured vodka. The ratio of botanicals shapes the top, middle and base of the aroma; more is not better.

## Distillation and cuts

The public process includes preparing neutral alcohol, adding botanicals to the distillation system, heating, cutting heads and tails, keeping the middle, reducing with water and bottling. Botanicals can be steeped before distillation or placed in a basket so steam passes through them, and the two methods extract aroma differently.

Still shape, heating rate and cut points all shape style. More reflux gives a lighter, cleaner body; a lower cut keeps more oils and aroma but risks roughness. The producer's core task is to keep juniper and supporting botanicals balanced in the same glass.

## Style spectrum

- **London dry gin**: flavour from redistribution, dry and clear, suited to the Martini and gin and tonic.
- **Plymouth gin**: protected by a geographical indication and made in Plymouth, England, usually softer and earthier than London dry.
- **Old Tom gin**: popular in the 18th and 19th centuries, lightly sweetened, with modern versions mostly reviving the style.
- **Genever**: a traditional Dutch and Belgian category based on malt wine, more grain-forward and heavier in body.
- **Contemporary gin**: rising after the 2000s, stressing botanical variety and regional expression, with juniper not always dominant.

These categories are not ranked. London dry gin is a reliable base for cocktail structure, contemporary gin suits aromatic exploration, and genever is closer to grain distillation tradition. Choose by use, not by grade.

## Tasting and mixing

London dry gin typically shows juniper, pine, citrus peel, coriander and floral notes, enters dry with clear alcohol and finishes with bitterness and spice. Taste a small amount neat, add a few drops of water to see whether aroma opens, then compare in a gin and tonic for how bubbles, sweetness and bitterness affect botanical detail.

The Martini emphasises the gin itself with only a small amount of dry vermouth; the gin and tonic is more everyday, usually mixed 1:2 to 1:3 with plenty of ice and citrus peel. Alcohol content strongly affects the final balance, so sugar and acid in a recipe need adjusting.

## Common misconceptions

First, assuming London dry gin must be made in London. It is a process category, not a geographical indication.
Second, reading dry as alcohol-free. Dry describes sweetness and style.
Third, assuming more botanicals is better. Balance and ratio matter more than number.
Fourth, treating every gin as a Martini base. Styles differ widely.
Fifth, ignoring alcohol content and release. Different markets and versions may differ, and the label is the authority.

London Dry Gin is a style, not a protected place name. The gin must be redistilled with botanicals, juniper must dominate, and added sugar and colour are tightly restricted. It can be made outside London. The style rules describe a production method and a flavour profile; they do not guarantee that every bottling comes from one city, one distillery or one recipe.

To read London dry gin, start with the production rule, then the strength, then the usual serving; no single check settles it.
