# Viognier

Viognier is a white grape of the Northern Rhône, best known through Condrieu. Its aroma is distinctive: apricot, peach, honeysuckle and citrus peel, often described as almost perfumed. But it is low in acidity and irregular in yield, so both growing and winemaking demand careful judgement. It is also a grape that almost vanished and was brought back, a history that still shapes prices and planting today.

## Definition and synonyms

The name is Viognier. It belongs to the European grape Vitis vinifera and sits in the Northern Rhône white group with Marsanne and Roussanne. Spellings differ slightly between languages but refer to the same variety. It is often treated as a byword for "aromatic white wine", yet aroma is only one side of it; balance between body, acidity and alcohol decides quality.

## Parentage and origin

The parent pair of Viognier remains unresolved. Genetic work shows a relationship with Alpine-Rhône varieties such as Savagnin and Marsanne, but no direct parents can be confirmed. Two origin stories exist: one places it in the Condrieu area of the Northern Rhône, the other has it brought to the Rhône from the Dalmatian coast by the Romans. What is certain is a planting history in the Northern Rhône going back centuries, followed by a sharp collapse in the mid-20th century.

## Botanical character

Bunches are fairly tight, berries medium with thicker skins tinged pink. It buds early and ripens mid to late, and often suffers poor fruit set (coulure) at flowering, so yields fluctuate; thick skins help aroma accumulation and give some disease tolerance. Uneven berry size and uneven ripening are common, and picking often needs several passes. Sugar rises quickly and acidity falls quickly, making the picking window narrower than for many white grapes.

## Viticultural behaviour

Viognier is moderately to strongly vigorous and suits poor, well-drained slopes. Condrieu vineyards are mostly steep terraces on granite-derived arzelle, hard to mechanise and expensive to farm. It drops fruit easily and yields are low and unstable, a major reason for Condrieu prices. In hotter New World regions yields can be higher but acidity is harder to keep; in cool regions ripening may not be sufficient. Growers trade off canopy management and picking date to retain acidity.

## Where it is grown

The Northern Rhône is the core: Condrieu was granted its AOC in 1940 and its vineyard area has gradually recovered; Château-Grillet is a single-estate AOC created in 1936 with only about 3.8 ha. Planting in the Southern Rhône is limited and mostly for blending. Outside France, Viognier is grown in California, Washington, Australia, Chile, Argentina and South Africa; California tends to make fuller, higher-alcohol versions, while Australia stresses citrus and florals. Cooler regions such as Virginia also try it, with ripeness the main challenge.

## Blending with Syrah

In Côte-Rôtie in the Northern Rhône, Viognier may be blended with Syrah up to a legal maximum of 20%, usually far below that. Its role is not to add fruit but to lift aromatic complexity, stabilise colour through co-pigmentation and soften tannin. The tradition is co-fermentation, though separate fermentation and blending is also used today. Viognier must stay restrained in the blend; too much masks Syrah's black pepper and smoke.

## Winemaking and style

Low acidity and high pH make oxidation and microbial risk important. Common practice is cool fermentation, partial barrel fermentation and lees stirring for texture; malolactic fermentation is not obligatory and can lower acidity further. Classic Condrieu shows apricot, peach, honeysuckle, ginger and beeswax with a full body and alcohol often between 13% and 14%; top wines develop honey and nuts in bottle. New World versions are often fruitier and sweeter in impression, and can feel loose when acidity is insufficient.

## Sensory profile and pairing

Young Viognier shows apricot, yellow peach, honeysuckle, citrus peel and a hint of ginger, with an oily entry, medium-low acidity and a finish of bitter almond and spice. It suits roast chicken, pork, cream sauces, lobster and Southeast Asian spiced dishes; high-alcohol versions do not work with very hot food. Serve it slightly warmer than a standard white, since cold suppresses aroma, but too warm exaggerates alcohol.

## Differences from Marsanne and Roussanne

All three are Northern Rhône whites, but they differ. Viognier is the most aromatic, lowest in acid and most oily; Marsanne is rounder and nuttier with medium acidity; Roussanne has higher acidity, more herbal and floral notes and better ageing potential. Condrieu uses Viognier alone, while Hermitage blanc often blends Marsanne and Roussanne. Lumping them together misses the variety of Northern Rhône whites.

## Producers and vintages

Condrieu names include Georges Vernay, Yves Gangloff, Pierre Gaillard, François Villard and Château-Grillet, with Georges Vernay often credited as central to the Viognier revival. Vintage differences come mainly from flowering coulure and summer heat: years with poor set give very low yields, concentrated flavour and high prices; hot years give more alcohol and less acidity. Read alcohol, appellation and producer style rather than the word Viognier alone.

## Common misconceptions

First, that Viognier is always sweet; most Condrieu is dry, and the impression of sweetness comes from ripe fruit and alcohol. Second, that more aroma is always better; over-ripeness loses acidity and detail. Third, that Viognier is only a single-varietal wine; the Côte-Rôtie blend is an important tradition. Fourth, confusing Condrieu with Château-Grillet; the latter is a separate AOC with a tiny area and different rules. Fifth, that Viognier cannot age; good Condrieu can keep for more than a decade. Sixth, that low acidity means it cannot pair with food; the oily texture suits many rich dishes as long as they are not too hot.

**Short definition:** A white grape native to the Northern Rhône, with thicker skins, low acidity and intense aroma. In Condrieu it gives full-bodied apricot and honeysuckle wines, and it is often blended in small amounts with Syrah for aroma and colour stability. It nearly disappeared in the mid-20th century, with only about 30 ha left in Condrieu around 1965.
