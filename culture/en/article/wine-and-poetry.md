# Wine and poetry: drinking from the Book of Songs to Song lyrics

Wine appears so often in Chinese poetry that “poetry and wine” has become a fixed phrase. Yet the drink was not the same in every period: the pre-Qin period had low-strength grain wine, the Tang and Song mostly rice and yellow wine, and distilled spirits became common only later. The occasions were also governed by different rules: sacrifice, village drinking, farewell, solitary drinking, exile. To read a drinking poem, first establish what was in the cup and on what occasion it was raised.

This article reads four periods: pre-Qin ritual and farming, Wei-Jin self-expression, Tang gatherings and individual circumstance, and the daily life and memory of Song lyrics. It ends by explaining why poems cannot be treated as drinking statistics.

## Pre-Qin: wine within ritual and farming

Most wine in the Book of Songs has an institutional background. Qi Yue, in the Odes of Bin, describes making spring wine after the harvest “to promote long life”, so wine belongs to the seasonal cycle and the care of elders. Bin Zhi Chu Yan describes a banquet losing its decorum, moving from courteous warmth to shouting and disorder; here wine tests ritual order. Early poetry cares less about flavour than about whether wine is placed correctly.

The drink of this period was fermented from grain, with qu converting starch to sugar before fermentation. Alcohol strength was low and keeping time short. Vessels such as the jue and gong were both drinking and ritual objects. Read the wine of the Book of Songs together with the ritual books, or a ritual scene will be mistaken for an everyday party.

## Wei and Jin: drinking becomes self-expression

From the late Han into the Wei and Jin, drinking poetry shifted from ritual to the individual. The legend of the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove links heavy drinking with resistance, but legend must be separated from evidence. The more reliable text is [Tao Yuanming](ref:tao-yuanming)'s twenty Drinking Poems: the preface says that after becoming drunk he wrote a few lines for his own amusement. Wine here is not a political manifesto but a companion to retirement, reading and thinking about life and death.

Tao's manner was influential: wine, fields, chrysanthemums and dusk combine into an image of life that later readers could imitate. Yet his “drinking” does not mean heavy daily consumption; several poems dwell on stopping and on the limits of the body.

## Tang: gatherings, farewells and individual circumstance

Tang cities had developed commerce, and taverns, wine banners and drinking games entered daily life. [Li Bai](ref:li-bai) turned drinking into extravagant imagination. In Qiang Jin Jiu, the line about cooking sheep and cattle and drinking three hundred cups is rhetoric, not a record of capacity; Drinking Alone by Moonlight turns solitude into a scene shared with the moon and his shadow. Li Bai's wine usually leads towards sky and myth, while realistic detail is pushed away.

[Du Fu](ref:du-fu) took almost the opposite path. He writes that when a friend visits, “for wine, being poor, we have only the old unfiltered brew”, and in late life, ill, he “newly stopped the cup of cloudy wine”. Wine marks economic condition, bodily condition and news of war rather than an untethered gesture. Read together, Li and Du show how one drink can become two entirely different experiences.

Numbers such as three hundred cups or a dou are literary language. Tang measures and alcohol strengths differed from today's, so lines cannot be converted into modern consumption. This needs saying whenever famous lines are quoted.

## Song: daily life and memory in the lyrics

In the Song, wine entered urban life and literati exchange more deeply. The ci form suits fragments of feeling, so wine is often tied to weather, flowering season, sleep and memory. [Li Qingzhao](ref:li-qingzhao) writes of “three cups and two cups of weak wine” that cannot withstand the evening wind; the wine neither warms nor replaces what has been lost. In Dongli Ba Jiu, drinking at dusk among chrysanthemums places the Double Ninth, flowers and longing in one picture.

Song drinking also involved elaborate drinking games and table entertainments; a wine banquet was a social occasion and a site of literary production. Su Shi, Xin Qiji and others tied wine to political disappointment, local government and memories of home. Compared with Tang poetry, Song lyrics are more domestic and better at writing the aftertaste of having drunk.

## Why poems are not drinking statistics

First, quantities in poems are often exaggeration or metrical necessity; three hundred cups cannot be read as consumption. Second, wine names are often generic, such as cloudy wine or new wine, and do not correspond to today's brands or aroma types. Third, the most moving poems often describe exceptional states: exile, farewell, illness, the fall of a state. These scenes cannot represent ordinary drinking frequency. Fourth, editions and variant characters affect wording, so the base text should be stated.

Poems supply scenes, feelings and language, not output, price or alcohol-content data. Once those limits are clear, poetry remains the best entrance: it remembers that wine was never only a drink, but a way of being with others.
