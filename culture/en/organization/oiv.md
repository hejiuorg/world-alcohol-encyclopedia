# The International Organisation of Vine and Wine: from the 1924 wine office to today

The International Organisation of Vine and Wine is usually shortened to “OIV”. It is not a league of wine estates, nor an association concerned only with tasting events, but an intergovernmental organisation made up of wine-producing and wine-consuming countries. Its work sounds highly technical: definitions, methods of analysis, statistics, labelling and trade harmonisation. Yet precisely that technical work forms the common language of today's global wine trade.

## What kind of organisation it is

The OIV's official presentation says it provides information to producing and consuming countries so that they can draw up regulations, reduce trade barriers, promote sustainable production and protect consumers. Its main aims include informing member states of measures affecting producers, consumers and industry participants; assisting other international organisations, especially those working on standardisation; and promoting international harmonisation of existing practices and standards.

In other words, the OIV's core product is harmonisation, not enforcement. It brings countries' differing definitions, methods of analysis and labelling rules onto the same table for comparison, then forms reference standards. It does not enforce rules on behalf of states, nor issue market-access permits; those powers remain with member states and national regulators.

## Starting from the phylloxera crisis

The OIV's official history traces its origin to 1874. That year phylloxera all but destroyed Europe's vineyards, and vine growers from France, Italy, Switzerland, Austria and Germany held a multi-day congress in Montpellier to seek a common response to the pest. It was one of the earlier instances of international cooperation in wine.

Thirty-five years later, with the phylloxera crisis past, another less visible danger appeared: disordered expansion of production and trade pushed fraud to astonishing scale, and markets were flooded with drinks labelled “wine”. In 1908 and 1909, conferences in Geneva and Paris discussed the problem; they proposed the first definition of wine and reaffirmed the principles of the 1891 Madrid Agreement against false indications of origin.

The First World War interrupted the process. In 1918 an international conference of wine-producing countries met again, with an agenda covering tariff revision, regulation of trade between states and the creation of an international body of exporting and importing countries to arbitrate disputes. In 1922 French agricultural associations again proposed an international wine organisation. These accumulated efforts led to the diplomatic conference of 1924.

## From the Office International du Vin to the OIV

On 29 November 1924 the parties signed an agreement in Paris to establish the Office International du Vin. In legal terms the OIV was born there; but the agreement required ratification by at least five countries before it could actually operate. Another three years passed, until 3 December 1927, before the required ratifications were in place.

This history of signing first and taking effect later shows that an international organisation does not run automatically once signatures are collected. It needs ratifications, budgets and staff. The OIV's first president was Edouard Barthe of France and its first director Léon Douarche, with a staff of only three at the start. In June 1928 the first OIV bulletin appeared; there were 7 member states in 1928 and 17 ten years later.

On 4 June 1935 the first international convention on uniform methods of wine analysis was signed in Rome. That step was crucial: without common methods of analysis, countries measuring alcohol content, sugar or volatile acidity differently could not settle trade disputes. The OIV's technical authority began to accumulate from there.

## War, the FAO and institutional transformation

During the Second World War the OIV faced the risk of disappearing. The official history records that president Barthe and secretary-general Basile Samarakis worked to keep the institution running, and the OIV bulletin continued to appear wherever conditions allowed. After the war, the founding of the UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) put every international organisation in the agricultural field at risk of absorption.

The OIV therefore worked to preserve its autonomy. In 1948 and 1949 the secretary-general travelled twice to Washington and eventually reached an agreement confirming that the FAO should not replace the OIV's function as the intergovernmental specialised body for wine. The episode shows that relations between international organisations are not always a division of labour; they can also involve jurisdiction and survival.

Barthe died in 1949, and the following year Baron Pierre Le Roy de Boiseaumarie was elected president, going on to lead the OIV for seventeen years. Samarakis died in 1956, and in 1957 the general assembly decided to rename the body the International Vine and Wine Office. The change reflected an expansion from wine trade to the whole field of vines and wine.

## The 2001 reorganisation and today's OIV

On 3 April 2001 the OIV completed its reorganisation and took its present name, the International Organisation of Vine and Wine. Its headquarters are at the Hôtel Bouchu dit d'Esterno, 1 rue Monge, in Dijon, France. It is no longer the small Paris office of the beginning but an intergovernmental organisation with multinational membership, an expert network and standing bodies.

The official presentation gives the current scale: 51 member states, 18 observers and more than 500 experts; member states account for about 88% of global wine production and 75% of consumption. That share shows the OIV is not an organisation of every country in the world, but it covers the main part of the wine industry.

Its technical work is carried out by four commissions and two sub-commissions: viticulture and table grapes; oenology and methods of analysis; economy and law; health and safety. That structure shows the OIV cares about more than how to make wine: it also covers vine growing, market rules, consumer health and trade law.

## What it does: standards, statistics, research and education

The OIV's work falls into four blocks. First, standards: setting internationally harmonised production standards and methods of analysis is its core task. Second, research and publication: it relies on its expert network to track frontier topics and publish studies. Third, databases and statistics: it collects member-state data and publishes statistics and outlooks for the global wine industry. Fourth, education and outreach: it offers research grants, sponsorships, a literary prize and a master's programme in wine management.

These activities sound scattered, but they point to one goal: letting producers, traders and regulators in different countries discuss the same product in the same language. Standards make tests comparable, statistics make markets legible, research keeps technique current, and education helps practitioners understand the rules.

That is also why the OIV is most often misread as a “world wine regulator”. It does set standards, but those standards do not automatically become member-state law. Whether a country adopts them, how it transposes them and when it applies them remain matters for national legislative and regulatory procedures.

So the OIV's influence is more like a frame of reference than a police force. When an importing country requires a method of analysis or a labelling detail, OIV standards are often used as technical grounds; but final compliance requirements still depend on border inspection, customs and domestic law. Grasping that layer prevents anyone from treating OIV resolutions as legal text to be cited directly.

## Common misreadings

First, taking the OIV for a wine estate or a trade association. It is an intergovernmental organisation whose members are states, not individual estates.

Second, treating 1924 as the start of actual operations. The agreement was signed in 1924, but the body only began once enough ratifications were in place in 1927.

Third, placing its headquarters in Paris. It was indeed in Paris early on; today it is in Dijon.

Fourth, treating OIV standards as law that takes effect automatically. Standards need adoption and transposition by member states.

Fifth, taking the OIV as only a “winemaking technical body”. It also deals with economy, law, health and safety, statistics and education.

## How to understand the OIV today

For general readers, the OIV's value is the backstage language it gives to the wine world. When you see origin, variety, vintage or grade on a label, or hear about “standard methods of analysis” or “protected designation of origin”, bodies like the OIV are often behind the harmonised definitions and rules. To read a label, see [How to Read a Wine Label](ref:read-a-label); for regions and site character, see [Terroir](ref:terroir); for France's place in the wine system, see [France](ref:france).

The OIV does not tell you which bottle tastes good, but it determines how the questions beyond taste are measured, compared and traded.
