# Dessert Wine

Dessert wine is not a grape, a region or a single law. It is a group of [wines](ref:wine) defined by residual sugar. It may be a white wine concentrated by botrytis, a wine made from late-picked or dried grapes, an ice wine pressed from fruit frozen on the vine, or a fortified wine in which fermentation was stopped by grape spirit. What holds the group together is a clear sweetness on the palate, and the acidity that has to exist to balance it.

## Definition: where the sugar sits

Sweetness in wine comes either from the grape's own sugar or from sugar left when fermentation stops. The first route is concentration: grapes lose water on the vine, are penetrated by noble rot, or are dried after picking, so the proportion of sugar rises. The second route is interruption: cooling, filtration, sulphur dioxide, or added alcohol stops the yeast. Both leave [residual sugar](ref:residual-sugar), but the flavours diverge. Concentration brings dried fruit, honey and botrytis notes; stopped fermentation keeps more fresh fruit.

Residual sugar is measured in grams per litre. EU sweetness categories for still wine place anything above 45 g/L in the sweet band, but that threshold is only one part of a labelling system, not a complete definition of dessert wine. Fortified sweet wines carry more alcohol, ice wines have sugar and acidity concentrated together, and botrytised wines owe their aroma to fungal metabolism. No single number covers all of that.

## Five routes to a sweet wine

The first is [noble rot](ref:noble-rot). Botrytis cinerea perforates the grape skin, water evaporates, and sugar, acidity and aroma compounds concentrate together, adding honey, ginger and marmalade notes. The second is [late harvest](ref:late-harvest): grapes stay on the vine, sugar rises and acidity falls, giving a more direct style than botrytis. The third is drying, whether on straw mats, wooden racks or in ventilated rooms, as in Italian passito and Pedro Ximénez. The fourth is freezing: grapes freeze on the vine and pressing leaves the ice behind, so only concentrated juice runs off. The fifth is fortification, where grape spirit is added before fermentation finishes, suppressing the yeast and preserving sugar.

All five routes concentrate something. They differ in where that concentration happens: on the vine, after picking, at the press, or in the tank. Once that is clear, dessert wine stops being a vague category and becomes a set of processes that can be compared.

## What the law only half says

EU law provides the framework for wine categories, geographical indications and labelling, while each appellation sets varieties, minimum sugar, yields and ageing in its own product specification. The 1855 Bordeaux classification placed Sauternes and Barsac within a sweet white hierarchy, and the 2013 reform in Tokaj ended compulsory puttonyos labelling in favour of grams per litre of residual sugar. Both examples show that the standards for sweet wine keep moving: a word on a label may be history, or it may be law.

## Acidity sets the ceiling

The usual failure of a dessert wine is not a lack of sweetness but a lack of acid. Sugar gives weight; acidity gives shape. Without it, a sweet wine feels sticky, short and cloying. Botrytised wines keep high acidity because of the grape itself and because the fungus consumes tartaric and malic acid at different rates. In [ice wine](ref:ice-wine), sugar and acidity are concentrated together. In fortified sweet wines, acidity is often reshaped by alcohol and oxidation. A simple test is to check whether the mouth feels clean after swallowing; if the sweetness lingers as syrup, acidity is probably short.

Alcohol is the other overlooked variable. It adds viscosity and heat, and combined with sugar it makes a wine feel thicker. High-alcohol fortified sweet wines therefore need higher acidity and longer ageing to stay in balance.

## Temperature, glass and life after opening

Light sweet whites, such as young Chenin Blanc or late-harvest Riesling, suit 6–8°C. Botrytised wines and ice wines suit 8–10°C, older botrytised wines can go to 12°C, and [fortified](ref:fortified-wine) sweet wines open up at 12–14°C. A tulip or a small white-wine glass gathers aroma without pushing alcohol into the nose.

Life after opening varies widely. Ice wine and botrytised wine have high sugar and oxidise slowly, so they keep for days to a couple of weeks in the fridge. Fortified sweet wines last longer still. Low-alcohol late-harvest wines are the most fragile.

## Pairing beyond dessert

One basic rule governs sweet wine and dessert: the wine should be sweeter than the food, or it will taste sharp and bitter. Blue cheese is the classic contrast, its salt and fat supporting the sugar. Foie gras with botrytised wine works through fat and acidity. Fruit tarts and almond desserts suit young botrytis or late harvest, while off-dry Riesling is often used with spicy Chinese food because sweetness softens heat. Ice wine is best on its own or with a light fruit dessert.

## Five common misreadings

First, that sweet wine is cheap wine. Botrytised and ice wines are made in tiny quantities with expensive selection, and often cost more than dry reds from the same region. Second, that all sweet wine depends on botrytis. Ice wine, dried-grape wine and fortified sweet wine do not. Third, that sweeter is better. Balance comes from the ratio of sugar to acid. Fourth, that an opened bottle keeps indefinitely. Low-alcohol sweet wines oxidise too. Fifth, that ice wine is made by freezing grapes after harvest. Compliant ice wine must freeze on the vine.

**In brief:** A broad family of wines defined by residual sugar rather than a single regulation, covering botrytised, late-harvest, dried-grape, ice and fortified sweet styles; the source of the sugar, the supporting acidity and the serving temperature decide whether the result is balanced.
