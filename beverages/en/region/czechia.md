# Czech Republic

The Czech beer story usually starts in Pilsen in 1842, but what actually made Czech beer was a set of interlocking conditions: soft water in western Bohemia, pale Moravian malt, the fine aroma of Saaz hops from Žatec, cool bottom fermentation and weeks or months of cold lagering. That combination allowed pale lager to appear as a clean, bright, sharply bitter drink, and it has been copied and reworked around the world ever since. ## Geography: Pilsen and four rivers

Pilsen lies in western Bohemia and is the capital of the Plzeň region. Four rivers meet around the city, the Mže, Radbuza, Úhlava and Úslava, and water is abundant. Local ground and river water is known for low mineral content, making it ideal soft water for pale lager: soft water keeps hop bitterness clean and prevents minerals from disturbing malt sweetness.

## History: the batch of 1842

In 1842 the Pilsen citizens' brewery hired the Bavarian brewer Josef Groll and produced a pale golden lager using bottom fermentation and local materials. Public material dates the first batch to 5 October 1842. It contrasted sharply with the dark beers then common, spread quickly, and the word pilsner gradually became shorthand for pale lager.

## Raw materials: malt, hops, water and yeast

Czech beer is based on barley malt, and Moravian barley is often used for pale malt; the Haná region is a traditional source of quality malt. Saaz hops are the signature variety, low in alpha acids with a fine aroma of herbs, spice and light citrus, suited to balanced bitterness rather than intense tropical fruit. Water sets minerality and bitterness, yeast sets the direction of fermentation, and only the four together produce the clean Czech outline.

## Process: decoction and cold lagering

Traditional Czech pale lager uses decoction mashing, boiling part of the mash and returning it to the mash tun to improve saccharification and malt flavour. Cool bottom fermentation follows, then several weeks of cold lagering that clarify the beer and stabilise its flavour. Modern breweries often use simplified mashing and precise temperature control, but cool, slow fermentation and thorough lagering remain central. Process differences directly affect cleanliness and bitterness, not raw materials alone.

## Styles: pale, amber and dark

Czech beer is not only pale lager. Světlý ležák is golden, with malt and hop bitterness in balance; polotmavý ležák has caramel and toasted bread; tmavý ležák shows cocoa, coffee and caramel. Different colours come from the malt bill and roasting level and do not rank the beers. Bitterness depends on hop quantity and timing and is not a quality score either.

## 12° is not alcohol

The figure "12°" on a Czech beer label usually means original wort strength in degrees Plato, not alcohol content. A 12° product commonly has about 4%-5% alcohol by volume, and the label is the authority. Reading 12° as 12% alcohol is the most common mistake. Beyond 12° there are 8°, 10°, 11°, 13° and 14° levels; higher numbers usually mean more malt and a fuller body, but attenuation, hops and process still change the final taste.

## Geographical indications: České pivo and Plzeňské pivo

In the European Union, "České pivo" was registered as a protected geographical indication on 16 October 2008. It requires Czech-grown barley, Czech hops and Czech water, and production by traditional methods. "Plzeňské pivo" also has geographical indication protection. Comparable products from elsewhere are usually labelled pilsner-style or pale lager rather than using the protected name. Name protection confirms origin and compliance with process, not a quality ranking.

## Breweries and cities

Plzeňský Prazdroj in Pilsen makes Pilsner Urquell and Gambrinus; Budějovický Budvar in České Budějovice is known for Budweiser Budvar; Prague has Staropramen; other names include Krušovice, Bernard in Humpolec and Únětický pivovar outside Prague. Bohemian and Moravian beer styles differ slightly: Bohemia stresses the cleanliness and hop bitterness of pale lager, while Moravia has more wheat and specialty beers.

## Market and consumption

The Czech Republic has long ranked near the top of world beer consumption per capita, and beer is part of everyday eating and drinking; Pilsen, Prague and České Budějovice all have established beer tourism routes. Pale lager remains the core of the domestic market, but craft and small breweries have grown noticeably over the past decade, and IPA, sour beer and barrel-aged beer now appear in mainstream bars. Export markets are led by Germany, Slovakia, Poland and the United States.

## Name disputes: Budweiser and Pilsner

"Budweiser" is the German name of České Budějovice, and also the brand name of the American Anheuser-Busch company; the two sides have fought a century of litigation and coexistence arrangements over the trademark. "Pilsner" is treated as a style name in some markets and is linked to a protected geographical indication in the EU. The disputes show that a beer place name is both an origin label and a commercial asset, not a generic word to be used freely.

## Drinking and pairing

Czech pale lager is served at 6-8℃ with roast pork knuckle, fried cheese, sausage, beef goulash and bread dumplings; amber and dark lagers suit 8-12℃ with grilled meat, game and smoked food. Czech pubs use pours such as hladinka, a full glass with foam, šnyt, half beer and half foam, and mlíko, almost all foam; foam thickness changes aroma and texture. Excessive drinking is harmful to health, and minors should not drink.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating 12° as alcohol content. Second, calling every pale lager Pilsner. Third, assuming the Czech Republic only makes Pilsner. Fourth, assuming stronger bitterness is better. Fifth, assuming soft water means no flavour. Sixth, treating "České pivo" as a generic term open to any Czech beer.

Czech Republic is best approached through the appellation and its permitted varieties, then the vintage and the producer, and then how the wine is labelled. One check alone will not settle it.
