# Château Cheval Blanc

Northwest of Saint-Émilion, the vineyards open on the right and the ground changes from limestone slopes to clay and gravel. Cheval Blanc’s main house hides behind a line of trees: pale stone, grey roof, a slim turret, a pond in front reflecting trees and sky. It lacks the dense “postcard” feel of Saint-Émilion village; it is a country house surrounded by vines. What puts it in architectural histories is the white concrete wave of the winery beside it.

## Location and history

The modern estate begins in 1832, when Jean-Jacques Ducasse, president of the Libourne tribunal, bought the core of the property. Over the next 20 years he acquired plots from neighbouring Château Figeac, forming a 39-hectare estate whose boundaries remain almost unchanged. In 1852 the wine was first sold as “Cheval Blanc”; in the 1860s Henriette Ducasse married Jean Laussac-Fourcaud, a Libourne merchant, and the estate carried out a bold replanting: half Merlot, half Cabernet Franc. The replanting was completed in 1871; by 1911 Cabernet Franc was already close to 50%.

In 1954, when Saint-Émilion first classified its wines, Cheval Blanc was ranked Premier Grand Cru Classé A. It retained that rank through successive reclassifications. In July 2021 the estate announced it would withdraw from the 2022 classification, arguing that the assessment criteria no longer matched its principles; Ausone also withdrew. Today it is wrong to call Cheval Blanc a “2022 Premier Grand Cru Classé A”. Its historical rank remains, but its current status is voluntary withdrawal.

In 1998 Baron Albert Frère and Bernard Arnault bought the estate; since then it has invested in vineyard, winery and brand. In 2011 Christian de Portzamparc’s winery opened; in 2021 the estate published an agroecology manifesto and moved toward agroforestry and lower chemical inputs. Cheval Blanc’s “aristocratic” feel comes not from a castle but from the continuity of its plots since 1832, its Cabernet Franc proportion and the precision of its winery.

## Architecture and site

The main house is a 19th-century country house: pale stone, a grey pitched roof, a slim turret. It is not grand, but its proportions are calm. In 2011 Portzamparc’s winery rose beside it: two white concrete waves, with 67 concrete fermentation vats underneath in 14 sizes from 12 to 113 hectolitres. Each vat is dedicated to one plot, with two plaques—one for vat number and capacity, one for plot number, variety and planting year. The design makes the “plot-vat-wine” relationship visible.

The winery roof is a grass slope with wild flowers, merging building and land; inside, natural light falls on white concrete and the vats stand like sculptures. It was the first winery to obtain HQE certification and won the Chicago Athenaeum’s International Architecture Award in 2013. Some find it too modern for medieval Saint-Émilion; others see it as liberating agricultural architecture from the decorative château. Either way, it is not a backdrop but a plot-by-plot fermentation tool.

The vineyard is 42 hectares, 56 plots and 244,989 vines; the soil is a mosaic of clay, gravel and sand on the Saint-Émilion/Pomerol gravel plateau. The planting is 55% Cabernet Franc, 40% Merlot and 5% Cabernet Sauvignon—unusual for the Right Bank. Cabernet Franc gives flowers, graphite, red fruit and fine tannin; Merlot gives plum, cocoa and roundness; Cabernet Sauvignon gives structure.

## Wines and palate

The grand vin is Château Cheval Blanc; the second wine is Le Petit Cheval; there is also Petit Cheval Blanc, a white. The grand vin usually shows violet, red cherry, blackcurrant, graphite, cedar, truffle and wet stone. When young its tannins are fine and its perfume open; it drinks earlier than many Left Bank first growths, but great vintages can last 20–30 years. It is not a “power” wine but a Right Bank style of finesse, flowers and velvet.

Cheval Blanc is often compared with Pomerol’s [Pétrus](ref:petrus): Pétrus is 100% Merlot on clay, a dense, powerful wine; Cheval Blanc is Cabernet Franc-led on gravel, with flowers and red fruit. Both are Right Bank, but they speak different languages. Compared with the Left Bank’s [Margaux](ref:margaux) or [Latour](ref:latour), Cheval Blanc has finer tannin, redder fruit and an earlier-opening perfume.

Le Petit Cheval is the earlier-drinking second wine, with roast chicken, beef and mushrooms. Petit Cheval Blanc is a rare white with citrus, white flowers and mineral. Do not assume a second wine is “a lesser grand vin”; it comes from different plots and younger vines, lighter in style but still showing the estate’s structure.

## How to drink

For the grand vin, open younger vintages 1–2 hours ahead and taste old bottles first; serve at 16–18°C in a Bordeaux or slightly wider glass. Pair with roast beef, lamb, pigeon, mushrooms, truffles and hard cheese; avoid heavy chilli, vinegar or sweet sauces. The perfume is floral and red-fruited, so over-decanting can erase the violet and cherry; old bottles need especially gentle handling.

Le Petit Cheval can be opened 30–60 minutes ahead, with roast chicken, beef stew, sausages and cheese. Petit Cheval Blanc should be 10–12°C, with seafood, white meat or goat cheese. If you order the grand vin in a restaurant, confirm the vintage and bottle condition; good and average years differ enormously, and the estate name alone is not enough.

## Etiquette and common misunderstandings

First, Cheval Blanc has withdrawn from the 2022 Saint-Émilion classification; do not introduce it as “2022 Premier Grand Cru Classé A”. Second, do not confuse it with Pomerol’s Pétrus; one is Cabernet Franc-led Saint-Émilion, the other 100% Merlot Pomerol. Third, “Cheval Blanc” is a place/plot name, not a colour or a trademark; the wine is predominantly red. Fourth, do not enter without an appointment; it does not offer regular public tours. Fifth, do not touch the concrete vats or plaques; they are working equipment, not exhibits.

There is another layer: the withdrawal was not a “demotion” but a rejection of the assessment criteria. To understand it, read the estate’s own statement rather than the headline “A rank cancelled”.

## Around and routes

Cheval Blanc is northwest of Saint-Émilion, near the Pomerol border: Saint-Émilion village and its monolithic church to the east, [Pétrus](ref:petrus) to the north, [Yquem](ref:yquem) and Sauternes to the south, and [Margaux](ref:margaux), [Latour](ref:latour), [Haut-Brion](ref:hautbrion) and [Lafite](ref:lafite) to the west. Putting Cheval Blanc into the [Bordeaux left-bank château trail](ref:route) is not logical; it belongs with Pomerol and Saint-Émilion on the Right Bank.

With one day, Cheval Blanc plus Saint-Émilion village is a sensible pairing. To add the Left Bank, allow at least two days. Right Bank vineyards are smaller and roads narrower; leave enough driving time between bookings, and do not schedule five estates in one day.
