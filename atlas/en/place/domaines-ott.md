# Domaines Ott

Domaines Ott* is one of the easiest names to recognise in Provence rosé, but it is not a single estate. It is a family brand built around three properties: Château de Selle in Taradeau, Clos Mireille in La Londe-les-Maures and Château Romassan in Le Castellet. All three make rosé, with small quantities of white and red, and each has its own cellar and tasting room. Château de Selle is the one most often visited. The first distinction to make is between the brand, the estate and the place you can actually enter; otherwise a single dot on a map becomes the whole company.

## One name, three estates

The official account dates the brand to about 130 years ago, when Marcel Ott founded it; the decisive move into Provençal vineyards came in 1912, when the purchase of Château de Selle gave the family its first estate in the region. Clos Mireille and Château Romassan followed, producing the present structure of three terroirs under one light. The three sit in different parts of the Var, and the wines are not copies of one another: each responds to its own soil, exposure and sea air.

## Limestone, schist and the Mistral

The image of [Provence](ref:provence) is often reduced to sunshine and lavender, but wind and stone do more to shape the wine. The Mistral blows from the Rhône valley towards the Mediterranean: dry, steady and sometimes violent. It reduces fungal pressure and concentrates ripening, at the cost of more careful trellising and protection in windy years. The CIVP divides the subsoil of Côtes de Provence into two broad systems: limestone in the north and west, crystalline rock and schist in the south and east. Château de Selle, at Taradeau, belongs to the first group, with gypsum, sandstone and red clay in the vineyards. Drainage is fast and roots must go deep for water.

These soils are not generous, and yields are not high, yet they give the rosé clear acidity and a mineral edge. The CIVP splits the whole appellation into eight production basins and recognises five complementary geographical denominations: Cru Sainte-Victoire, Fréjus, La Londe, Pierrefeu and Notre-Dame des Anges, added in 2019. These are not marketing stickers; they are geographical subdivisions written into the rules of the [appellation](ref:appellation).

## How rosé stays pale

The colour of [rosé](ref:rose) comes from contact with grape skins, not from blending. The dominant method in Provence is direct pressing: fruit is pressed soon after harvest so that juice and skins meet only briefly, then fermented cool. The result is a pale colour — grey-pink, onion skin or light salmon — with citrus, white flowers, redcurrant and a little stone fruit, dry and fresh on the palate. It is not at odds with short maceration: the shorter the maceration, the paler the colour and the lower the tannin.

Timing matters. Pick too early and the acidity is sharp and the aromas thin; pick too late and alcohol rises while the colour deepens. CIVP figures show that about 51% of the Côtes de Provence vineyard is certified organic or HVE, which suggests that light extraction is not only a style choice but part of a wider approach to inputs and clean fruit. To understand the glass, look first at pressing and temperature, then at the soil.

## Visiting the cellar in Taradeau

Château de Selle is at 5093 Route de Flayosc, 83460 Taradeau, France. Administratively it belongs to Taradeau, not Les Baux-de-Provence; the drive from Les Baux takes about an hour. The tasting room and cellar open Monday to Friday, 9:30–12:00 and 13:00–16:30. Clos Mireille and Château Romassan keep different hours, 9:00–12:00 and 14:00–17:00.

The guided tour lasts about 45 minutes and is reservation-only, subject to the day's availability. The cellar is a contemporary building using gravity flow and small vats matched to individual parcels; on a visit you see stainless steel, oak foudres and spherical ceramic vessels side by side. Such details explain the wine better than a history panel: different materials and volumes change temperature and oxygen contact, so rosé and red take separate paths.

## Reading the range and the vintages

Rosé is the core, but the range is wider than one bottle. Château de Selle's rosé leads with citrus, white flowers and minerality and works well as the baseline for a route; Clos Mireille, near the coast, is usually rounder with a clearer saline note; Château Romassan, near Le Castellet, is tighter in structure. The reds show another face of the same land, with more tannin and spice.

Vintage variation is strong in Provence. Hot, dry years raise sugar quickly and bring harvest forward; wet years demand more sorting. Pale does not mean tasteless: a good Provençal rosé keeps concentration and length behind the light colour. In the tasting room, compare the three estates' rosés first, then take one red as a contrast; that builds a coordinate system faster than drinking a single wine.

## Starting from Les Baux-de-Provence

From Les Baux-de-Provence to Taradeau, the simplest route runs south on the A8 or D554 before turning onto the country roads around the D54, with vineyards and pine woods alternating along the way. Enter the address in the navigation system. Latitude and longitude have not been taken from an official page here, so coordinates should be confirmed through official channels; parking and the meeting point are best checked in the booking confirmation.

Plan this stop for a weekday morning, because the cellar and tasting room are normally closed at weekends. To continue east to [Château Miraval](ref:mirav) in Correns, allow about an hour; the two stops fit into one day. Do not touch the cask racks or pipes, and do not drive after tasting. Provençal country roads are narrow and winding, so a designated driver or a hired car is safer than "just one glass".
