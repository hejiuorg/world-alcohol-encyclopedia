# Body

![Editorial illustration of wine body](/media/photos/editorial-body-q2.jpg)

Figure: Editorial illustration of wine body: different glass shapes and wine colours, with tonal bands suggesting weight; AI-assisted illustration, not a photograph. Image: Hejiu editorial team, project-owned.

## Definition and boundaries

Body is the tasting term for the “weight” of a drink: whether it feels light, medium or heavy on the palate. It is not a synonym for alcohol content, and it is not a quality score. Body results from several components acting together, and understanding them is more useful than memorising “light, medium, full”. Professional tasting commonly uses light-bodied, medium-bodied and full-bodied, but the three bands have no hard boundaries.

Several distinctions are worth keeping. Body answers “how much substance does this drink have in the mouth”, not “how good is it” and not “how intense is the aroma”. A highly aromatic wine can be light-bodied; a restrained wine can be full-bodied. Body also changes with temperature, glass shape and bubbles: the same wine usually feels tighter when cold and looser when warm.

## Etymology and origin

Body comes from Old English bodig, meaning the trunk or physical frame, and later extended to “substance” or “main part”. Wine tasting borrowed the word to compare the presence of the liquid in the mouth to the weight of a body. The Chinese term is a direct translation and keeps the same metaphor: it refers to the wine's presence in the mouth, not to its chemical composition.

The metaphor has limits. A body can be tall or short, thin or stout, while wine body is a continuum of weight and fullness. Reading body as “structure” also confuses it with the frame formed by tannin and acidity. The most accurate use treats body as a tactile, mouthfeel description rather than a substitute for structure, aroma or quality.

## Mechanism and parameters

The first factor is [alcohol by volume](ref:abv). Alcohol brings viscosity and heat, so higher alcohol usually means a heavier body; Wine Folly describes wines above 14% alcohol as tending to taste more full-bodied. The second is residual sugar and glycerol. Sugar and glycerol add roundness and weight, and a small amount of residual sugar can add body without making the wine taste sweet. The third is [tannin](ref:tannin), which brings astringency and “frame”, making a wine seem to have more volume. The fourth is acidity: low acidity makes a wine feel loose, while high acidity makes it tighter and lighter, and Wine Folly notes that people tend to perceive higher-acid wines as lighter-bodied.

The fifth is maceration, ageing, lees contact and dissolved oxygen, which change texture. The sixth is temperature, glass shape and bubbles: cold makes a wine feel tighter, a wide glass opens the aroma, and bubbles add tingling freshness. Beer body also relates to residual sugar, protein and carbonation: more sugar and less carbonation make the body feel fuller, while high carbonation makes it feel lighter.

## Practical use in the drinks industry

For [red wine](ref:red-wine), tannin and alcohol often support body together; for [white wine](ref:white-wine), body comes more from alcohol, glycerol and lees contact. For [beer](ref:beer), body is a standard style descriptor: the BJCP guidelines use “very light, sometimes watery body” for American Light Lager and “full body” for styles such as strong stouts. For [baijiu](ref:baijiu), body descriptions often involve alcohol content, the balance of acids and esters and a soft, sweet mouthfeel, though the specific language varies by aroma family.

In tasting notes, body should be recorded separately from aroma, acidity and tannin. The best way to practise is to compare wines of the same kind: the same variety at different alcohol levels; the same alcohol level with different residual sugar; the same region with different maceration times. Record the viscosity on entry, the coverage of the palate and the weight after swallowing, then record aroma and finish. Keeping body separate reduces subjective interference.

When pairing, body is an important clue. Light-bodied wines suit delicate dishes and seafood; full-bodied wines suit red meat, grills and rich sauces. A body that is too heavy overwhelms delicate food, while one that is too light is covered by rich food. This principle is more specific and more reliable than “red wine with red meat”.

## Confusable terms

Body and alcohol content are not the same thing. High alcohol can add weight, but a high-alcohol wine with low residual sugar and low tannin can still feel light; alcohol is one cause, not the thing itself. Body and flavour intensity are also separate dimensions. Aromatic concentration and mouth weight are independent: a light wine can be highly aromatic, and a full wine can be restrained.

Body and structure are easily mixed up. Structure usually means the frame and tension formed by acidity, tannin and alcohol; body is closer to tactile weight and fullness. Body and sweetness also need separating. Residual sugar adds body, but sweetness is a taste; a dry wine can feel full because of glycerol, alcohol or lees contact without tasting sweet.

## Common misuses and disputes

First, equating body with alcohol content and calling a 14% wine full-bodied automatically. Second, equating body with quality, assuming light is thin and full is superior; a light-bodied wine can be very refined, and a full-bodied wine can be clumsy. Third, judging body by colour. Colour comes mainly from maceration and pigments, and does not directly correspond to mouth weight. Fourth, ignoring temperature and glass shape. Fifth, using “thick” for every positive impression, which blurs the description.

The dispute centres on whether fuller is better. Commercial language often sells fullness and richness as virtues, yet many regions and varieties achieve quality through lightness and transparency. The safer judgement places body within style and pairing: the appropriate body matters more than the biggest body.

## Typical values and examples

- Wines above 14% alcohol tend to be perceived as more full-bodied.
- BJCP description of American Light Lager: very light, sometimes watery body.
- Beer body relates to residual sugar, protein and carbonation: more sugar and less carbonation make it fuller.
- A small amount of residual sugar can add body without sweetness; glycerol also adds fullness.
- Higher acidity usually makes a wine feel lighter; lower acidity makes it feel looser.
- Typical light-bodied reds: Pinot Noir and Gamay; typical full-bodied reds: Malbec, Syrah and Cabernet Sauvignon.
- Typical white: Chardonnay aged in oak is often described as fuller-bodied.

These examples remind the taster that body is shaped by components, process and perception together, and cannot be judged by alcohol content alone.
