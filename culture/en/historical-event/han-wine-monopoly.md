# The Han Wine Monopoly: State Trading in 98 BCE and Its Abolition in 81 BCE

Que originally meant a single-plank bridge, and came to mean a state checkpoint and monopoly. In the third year of Tianhan, Emperor Wu 'first established the que wine monopoly', bringing brewing and selling under state control; in the sixth year of Yuanyuan, Emperor Zhao consulted the worthy and learned, and in the seventh month abolished the monopoly officials, replacing direct state trading with private declaration and taxation by law. The policy lasted less than twenty years, yet became a standard starting point for later debates on wine monopoly and wine tax.![The Han Wine Monopoly: State Trading in 98 BCE and Its Abolition in 81 BCE category illustration](/media/photos/theme-asian.jpg)

## Date and place

The policy ran from 98 BCE to 81 BCE and covered the whole Han empire, not a single commandery. The fiscal pressure of Emperor Wu's reign came from long foreign wars and large-scale construction, and salt, iron and wine were brought under state control one after another. Wine differed from salt and iron: it was not a means of production but a widely consumed everyday drink, so a monopoly both raised revenue and directly changed daily life.

## Background: war finance and state control of salt and iron

The Book of Han's annals of Emperor Wu leave only the four characters 'first que wine monopoly', and the method has to be inferred from the annotations. Ying Shao says that 'the county officials themselves brewed, monopolised and sold wine, and ordinary people could no longer sell it', meaning the state brewed and sold on its own and commoners were barred from producing or selling wine. Wei Zhao explains the word que by 'a wooden bridge across water': private brewing and selling were forbidden, and only state-run outlets could operate, like crossing a river by the official bridge.

The wine monopoly belonged to the same fiscal logic as the state control of salt and iron: take goods with steady demand and good margins into state hands, and use monopoly prices in place of ordinary taxation. The article on Chinese Social Sciences Today describes the Han period as the beginning of wine monopoly and notes that monopoly fees or special taxes long remained a source of state revenue. That summary works as background, but the records do not preserve complete figures for rates or annual income.

## Course: from monopoly to abolition

| Date | Event | Record and annotation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Third year of Tianhan (98 BCE) | First que wine monopoly | Book of Han, Annals of Emperor Wu; annotations by Ying Shao and Wei Zhao |
| Second month, sixth year of Yuanyuan (81 BCE) | Consultation of the worthy and learned on popular hardships; proposal to end the salt, iron and wine monopolies | Book of Han, Annals of Emperor Zhao |
| Seventh month of the same year | Abolition of the monopoly officials; permission to declare for tax by law; wine at four cash per sheng | Book of Han, Annals of Emperor Zhao; annotations by Ru Chun and Yan Shigu |

Emperor Zhao had reigned only a few years, and the regent, the general-in-chief Huo Guang, faced the fiscal and social pressures left by the late Emperor Wu's policies. The edict of the second month in the sixth year of Yuanyuan handed 'the hardships of the people' to the worthy and learned recommended by the commanderies and kingdoms, with salt, iron and the wine monopoly on the agenda. In the seventh month the court abolished the monopoly officials, allowed private parties to 'declare for tax by law', and recorded a price of four cash per sheng of wine.

Ying Shao's explanation of the abolition is that 'Emperor Zhao attended to the root and restrained the branches, and did not contend for profit with the realm, so the monopoly was abolished'. That sentence is a later moral summary: it frames monopoly as contending with the people for profit and abolition as promoting agriculture and restraining commerce. The actual motives may have included finance, public opinion and administrative cost, and a single annotation cannot be treated as the conclusion.

## After abolition: declaration and the price of wine

The key to 'declaring for tax' lies in the word 'declare'. Ru Chun notes that the head of household liable for tax had to estimate the property and report it truthfully; a false report was punished with a fine of two jin and the confiscation of undeclared property and sale proceeds. Yan Shigu adds that 'declare means privately estimating the truth and fixing the wording', that is, assessing it oneself and stating it clearly. Abolition was therefore not full laissez-faire: direct state trading was replaced by private declaration plus taxation.

'Four cash per sheng of wine' is a record easily over-read. It can be read as a price fixed by the authorities, or as a reference price used to assess tax; both readings lack corroboration. What is certain is that the price of wine entered an imperial edict, which shows that wine had moved from a ritual item to an everyday commodity that had to be managed.

## Influence: three policies in long alternation

First, monopoly became a tool that could be switched on repeatedly. Later dynasties restarted state trading or raised wine taxes when finances were tight, and ordered prohibition in years of famine or grain shortage; monopoly, wine tax and prohibition alternated, and the Han experiment is one of the earliest complete cases.

Second, wine tax entered ordinary revenue. The article on Chinese Social Sciences Today describes wine tax after the Han as an important source of revenue 'basically throughout', showing how wine moved from sacrificial offering to a line in the fiscal ledger.

Third, private brewing was not eliminated. After abolition, selling wine with a declaration for tax meant that private breweries could operate legally as long as they declared and paid; later debates centred on how to define private brewing and assess the tax, not on whether to ban wine outright.

Fourth, wine policy remained entangled with ritual language. The classical authority for limiting drinking was still often the Book of Documents' Jiugao, discussed in [Shang and Zhou wine ritual](ref:ritual); fiscal need and moral admonition were present at the same time, a lasting feature of Chinese wine policy. For the craft and category background, see [yellow wine](ref:yellow-wine) and [the history of Chinese wine](ref:chinese-wine-history).

## Sources and disputes

First, the Book of Han is extremely brief. The annals of Emperor Wu give only 'first que wine monopoly', without saying who ran it, how prices were set or how state workshops were organised; the record alone cannot reconstruct the system.

Second, whether 'abolishing the monopoly officials' meant ending the monopoly entirely is disputed. Some stress that 'permission to declare for tax by law' shows a shift to taxation; others hold that state trading continued in places. The sources lack the detail for a verdict, and this page does not take sides.

Third, the annotations of Ying Shao and Wei Zhao are Han-Wei interpretations, not contemporaneous archives. Ying Shao's 'not contending for profit with the realm' carries a moral judgement, and Wei Zhao's 'wooden bridge across water' is a word gloss; neither is the text of the policy.

Fourth, the nature of 'four cash per sheng' is unclear. It may be a price ceiling, an official price or a tax base, and all three readings lack direct support; treating it as a general Han wine price goes beyond what the material can bear.

Fifth, salt and iron are often narrated together with the wine monopoly, but the institutions differed. The salt and iron debate left a record of argument, while the administrative detail of the wine monopoly is thinner; filling the gaps in one with material from the other requires great care.

## Timeline

- Third year of Tianhan (98 BCE): first que wine monopoly, with the state controlling brewing and selling.
- Second month, sixth year of Yuanyuan (81 BCE): consultation of the worthy and learned on ending the salt, iron and wine monopolies.
- Seventh month, sixth year of Yuanyuan (81 BCE): abolition of the monopoly officials, declaration for tax by law, wine at four cash per sheng.
- Afterwards: monopoly, wine tax and prohibition alternated across later dynasties.
