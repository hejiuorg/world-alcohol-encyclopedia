# Barolo

Barolo is a red wine from the Barolo DOCG in the Langhe hills of Piedmont, Italy, made only from Nebbiolo and known for high acidity, firm tannin and aromas of rose and tar. It is commonly 750 mL at about 14%–15% vol, and the appellation rules require at least 38 months of total ageing, with at least 18 months in wood.

## Definition and position

Start by separating the levels: [red wine](ref:red-wine) is the category, Nebbiolo is the variety, the Langhe in Piedmont is the region, Barolo is the appellation (DOCG), and individual estates and wines are the producer level. Keeping these four apart is what stops "Barolo" from being read as a brand or as a grape.

Barolo is a place name and an appellation marker, not a trademark. Only wine grown and made inside the delimited area under the production rules, from 100% Nebbiolo, may be labelled Barolo; other products described as "Barolo style" are not appellation wine.

## Producer and brand

The Barolo area has many family estates, cooperatives and merchants, from domaines of a few hectares to larger merchants. The bottler information on the label is the basis for identity: an estate with its own parcels and a merchant who buys grapes or wine play different roles.

The appellation covers eleven wine-producing communes, including Barolo, La Morra, Castiglione Falletto, Serralunga d'Alba, Monforte d'Alba, Novello, Verduno, Grinzane Cavour, Diano d'Alba, Cherasco and Roddi. The commune or specific site is often printed in small type on the label.

## Region and ingredients

The appellation lies in the Langhe hills of Piedmont in north-west [Italy](ref:italy), sheltered by the Alps to the north, with autumn fog that is popularly linked to the name Nebbiolo through the Italian word nebbia (fog). Slope aspect and altitude create day-night temperature swings that favour aroma and acidity.

Soils differ markedly: around La Morra and Barolo the limestone and marl soils tend to give more aromatic, softer-textured wines, while Serralunga d'Alba and Monforte d'Alba have sandier, firmer soils that give more structure and need longer ageing. The raw material is [grape](ref:grape), of the [Nebbiolo](ref:nebbiolo) variety, which ripens late and has thick skins with high tannin and acidity.

## Winemaking

Harvest is usually in October; the grapes are destemmed and pressed, and maceration is long, often several weeks, to extract colour, tannin and aroma from the skins. Fermentation runs at relatively warm temperatures, followed by malolactic conversion to soften acidity. Nebbiolo is pale in colour but high in tannin, so the extraction is the difficult part of the method.

After fermentation the wine goes into [oak ageing](ref:oak-ageing). Traditional practice uses large Slavonian oak casks, emphasising slow oxidation and integration; modern practice uses small French oak, adding sweet spice and cocoa notes. The production rules require at least 38 months of total ageing, with at least 18 months in wood; wines labelled Riserva require at least 62 months of total ageing.

## Specification and label

The common volume is 750 mL, with [alcohol by volume](ref:abv) at about 14%–15% vol, varying with vintage and estate. The label should be checked for the name Barolo, the DOCG mark, vintage, the word Riserva, the commune or site name, bottler, net volume and importer information.

Commune and site names are geographical designations and must comply with the boundaries and usage rules in the production code. The Chinese back label, importer and anti-counterfeit information are the basis for checking identity; label languages differ by market, so the bottle on hand governs.

## Flavour and tasting

Typical aromas are rose, violet, red cherry, plum, tar, liquorice and truffle; the palate has high acidity, firm tannin and a full body with a long finish. Young wines often seem tight and closed, opening over time into dried flowers, leather and earth.

For tasting, serve at about 16–18 degrees Celsius in a large tulip-shaped glass that gives the aromas room. Younger vintages can be decanted for one to several hours, while older bottles should be decanted for less time so the aroma does not fade. Watch the colour shift from garnet towards brick red in the glass.

## Drinking and pairing

It pairs well with braised beef, game, truffle dishes, hard cheese and fatty meats; high acidity and firm tannin cut fat and protein and echo earthy flavours. It does not suit steamed seafood, very light vegetables or heavily spicy dishes, which can make the wine taste bitter or overwhelm the food.

After opening, refrigerate and finish within a few days, as prolonged air contact speeds oxidation. Most Barolo needs bottle age before it reaches its drinking window, and that window depends on vintage, commune and estate method. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

## Buying and storage

Buy from reputable channels and check vintage, the DOCG mark, commune or site name, bottler and importer information. Prices move with vintage, site and channel, so no figure is given here; an ordinary vintage and a famous site belong to different levels, and one wine cannot stand for the whole appellation.

For Barolo, unopened bottles should be kept away from light, lying flat, in a cool place at an ideal 12–15 degrees Celsius, avoiding heat and sharp temperature swings. Cork-sealed bottles need humidity and can be stored lying down; after opening, refrigerate and drink soon rather than warming and cooling repeatedly.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating Barolo as a variety name. It is a place name; the variety is Nebbiolo.
Second, assuming Barolo must be cellared for decades before drinking. Vintages, communes and winemaking styles differ greatly.
Third, equating pale colour with a thin wine. Nebbiolo is naturally low in pigment, yet its tannin and structure can be powerful.
Fourth, treating the whole appellation as one style. La Morra and Serralunga d'Alba differ noticeably.
Fifth, judging authenticity by price or packaging. Channel, label and anti-counterfeit information are more reliable.

For Barolo, begin with the base and the stated strength. Then look at the sweetness or bitterness, and finally ask how it is served. The answers point to different choices, not to a single score.
