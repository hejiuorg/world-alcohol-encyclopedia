# Fortified wine

Fortified wine is wine to which grape spirit has been added, raising alcohol content and changing the flavour direction. The added alcohol may stop fermentation and preserve residual sugar, or stabilise a wine whose fermentation is already complete. Different timing, base-wine styles and ageing methods produce very different categories: Port, Sherry, Madeira and Vermouth.![Fortified wine image](/media/photos/drink-style-fortified-wine-45f14e62.jpg)

Figure: Fortified wine. Image: Jon Sullivan, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons ([source page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Port_wine.jpg)).

## What “fortification” actually does

Ordinary wine gets its alcohol from yeast fermentation and usually sits in the low-to-mid teens. Fortified wine adds grape spirit during or after fermentation, pushing the final strength higher. The OIV calls these “liqueur wines” and sets an acquired alcoholic strength of 15% to 22%; some countries may allow above 22% but no more than 24% for their domestic market. The wine is made from grape must, partially fermented grape must and/or wine, with added grape distillate, spirits or alcohol of vitivinicultural origin, and may include permitted additions such as concentrated grape must or caramel.

If fortification happens before fermentation ends, the high strength inhibits yeast activity, fermentation stops and some grape sugar remains, making the wine sweeter. If fortification happens after fermentation, the wine is usually drier and alcohol mainly raises strength and stability. This timing difference is one key to the divergence between Port and Sherry.

## Main categories

Port comes from the Douro Valley in Portugal and is usually fortified mid-fermentation, retaining sugar for a rich, sweet style. It includes ruby, tawny and vintage styles, with different ageing and blending approaches. Sherry comes from Jerez in Spain and is usually fortified after fermentation; styles range from very dry Fino to sweet Cream, and the solera system is its distinctive ageing and blending method. Madeira comes from the Portuguese island of Madeira and undergoes heat and oxidative ageing, giving caramel, nut and acidity. Vermouth is an aromatised fortified wine, based on wine with herbs and spices plus alcohol or sugar.

Most of these names are protected designations. Only wine made in the defined region by the defined process may use the name. Treating “Port” as a generic term for all sweet fortified wine, or “Sherry” as a generic term for all dry fortified wine, ignores their rules and styles.

## Regulation and labels

The EU common market organisation for wine, Regulation (EU) 1308/2013, provides the framework for fortified wine, geographical indications and label terms. PDO/PGI labels tie region, variety, process and name together; specific product specifications also set minimum strength, permitted fortification timing, ageing requirements and sweetness levels.

The strength, residual sugar, vintage and regional information on a label should explain one another. A wine labelled “vintage Port”, for example, usually ages in bottle for a long time and carries a declared year; a “tawny Port” emphasises oxidative ageing and blending. When a label says “fortified”, check the category, sweetness and ageing method, not just the colour.

## Style and drinking

Fortified wines are higher in alcohol, so serving sizes are usually smaller than for ordinary wine. Dry Sherry is often chilled and served as an aperitif; sweet Port and Cream Sherry suit chocolate, nuts and blue cheese; Madeira's high acidity and caramel notes suit desserts or after-dinner drinking. Vermouth is often a cocktail ingredient and can also be served over ice.

Once opened, fortified wines usually keep longer than ordinary wine because of their higher strength and deeper oxidative character, but how long still depends on category, sugar, heat treatment and cork condition. “Fortified” does not mean “never spoils”.

## Sweetness, oxidation and ageing style

Two main axes define fortified wine: sweetness and degree of oxidation. Sweetness depends on fortification timing: fortifying mid-fermentation retains sugar, while fortifying after fermentation gives a drier wine. Oxidation depends on how the wine is aged: ageing in inert vessels keeps it closer to a fresh state, while long ageing in oak with oxygen develops nut, caramel and spice notes and a deeper colour.

The stylistic difference between Port and Sherry comes largely from how these two axes combine. Ruby Port emphasises fruit and colour, tawny Port emphasises oxidation and nuttiness; Fino Sherry stays light under a layer of flor, while Oloroso develops deeper colour and nutty aroma through oxidation. Madeira's heated ageing is another separate path.

## Common misconceptions

First, fortified wine does not mean "sweet wine". Fino Sherry can be very dry; fortification only means that grape spirit was added.

Second, fortified wine is not the same as spirits. Its base is still wine; it is stronger than ordinary wine but usually lower in alcohol than spirits such as brandy.

Third, not all fortified wines keep indefinitely. How long an opened bottle lasts depends on category, sugar, degree of oxidation and cork condition, and cannot be generalised.

## Boundary

This style describes the category, process and regulatory framework, not specific estates, vintages or prices. Check the label, producer information and professional reviews for strength, residual sugar, ageing time, drinking window and food pairing. The grape spirit used in fortification is related to [brandy](ref:brandy), but the two are different product categories.
