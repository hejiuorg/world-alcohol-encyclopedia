# Lager

Lager is an umbrella term for beers made with cool bottom-fermenting yeast and then stored for a long period near freezing point. Together with top-fermented ale it forms one of the two basic technical routes in brewing. Within the style system of [beer](ref:beer), lager is a process family rather than a single style: pale lager, dark lager and [Pilsner](ref:pilsner) all belong to it, and Pilsner is merely its most representative branch. Keeping this clear is what puts the words “lager” and “Pilsner” back in their proper places.![Lager image](/media/photos/drink-style-lager-7d0e4091.jpg)

## Definition and standards

The technical core of lager is yeast and temperature. The bottom-fermenting yeast Saccharomyces pastorianus is a hybrid of brewer's yeast and a wild yeast; it works at 7–12°C, tolerates cold, settles well and produces relatively few esters and higher alcohols. After primary fermentation the beer is stored for several weeks near 0°C, a step called lagering, from the German lagern, to store.

Style guidelines usually divide lager into pale lager, dark lager, Pilsner and bock. Pale lager commonly has 4.0–5.5% alcohol and about 20–40 IBU; dark lager commonly 4.5–6.0%. These figures describe typical ranges, not a scorecard. In everyday usage lager is also used as a synonym for “industrial mild beer”, but that is a market usage, not a technical definition.

## Origin and history

Since the Middle Ages, brewers in central Europe had stored beer in winter cellars or ice caves; the cold stabilised the beer and reduced off-flavours, but output was limited by the season. In the 19th century Bavarian brewers improved yeast handling and fermentation control, and bottom fermentation gradually became a reproducible process. In 1842 Plzeň produced a pale bottom-fermented beer, the later [Pilsner](ref:pilsner).

In the 1870s Carl von Linde's mechanical refrigeration reached breweries, making stable cold fermentation possible all year and allowing lager to move into large-scale production. In the 20th century lager industrialised further in the Americas and Asia and became the world's largest beer category by volume; family breweries meanwhile kept small, unfiltered versions alive.

## Ingredients and process

The mineral composition of [water](ref:water) directly shapes how hop bitterness is perceived and how malt aroma comes through. [Barley](ref:barley) malt supplies enzymes and fermentable sugar; some markets add rice or maize as adjuncts to cut cost and lighten the flavour. Hops provide bitterness and aroma, and their quantity and variety set the direction of the style.

Mashing may use decoction or a single-infusion rest. The wort is boiled, hopped, clarified, cooled and pitched with bottom-fermenting yeast. [Fermentation](ref:fermentation) usually runs at 7–12°C for one to two weeks; the beer then moves to storage tanks and rests near 0°C for weeks or months. The cold lets yeast settle, reduces diacetyl and naturally saturates the beer with carbon dioxide, leaving it clean and stable.

Before packaging, most commercial lager is filtered or pasteurised to extend shelf life; lager in [craft beer](ref:craft-beer) often keeps its yeast and stays unfiltered, giving fuller flavour but a shorter shelf life.

## Flavour profile

The common thread in lager is cleanliness, low esters and few fermentation off-flavours. Pale lager rests on bready, biscuit-like malt, with hops adding herbal, floral or lightly citrus notes; dark lager brings caramel, nuts, cocoa and roasted bread; Pilsner is known for the spicy, fine bitterness of Saaz hops. Cool fermentation does not mean an absence of flavour, since attenuation, carbonation and body still shape the palate.

Common defects include buttery diacetyl, cooked-sweetcorn DMS, cardboard from oxidation, and the “light struck” note that clear bottles develop in daylight. These are process or storage problems, unrelated to the style itself.

## Tasting and serving

Pale lager and Pilsner are best at around 4–8°C, and dark lager can be served a little warmer at 8–10°C. Too cold suppresses aroma; too warm makes the beer feel loose. A tall glass or stemmed glass helps gather foam and aroma. Lager is sensitive to freshness: hop aroma and delicate malt notes fade with oxidation and light, so the production date matters more than the packaging design.

With food, pale lager suits fried food, salads and seafood, while dark lager suits grilled meat, stews and hard cheese. Store away from light in a cool place and avoid repeated warming.

## Common misconceptions

First, equating lager with bland industrial beer. A fermentation method does not determine flavour intensity, and craft lager can be full of character.
Second, treating Pilsner as all lager. Pilsner is one branch, dependent on a specific water, hop and brewing tradition.
Third, treating dark lager as stout. The two differ in fermentation method and recipe structure.
Fourth, assuming lager cannot be craft beer. Contemporary craft breweries make lager widely.
Fifth, assuming a paler colour means less flavour. Flavour depends on ingredients, fermentation and freshness, not on colour.

Start with the production rule. From there, consider the strength, then the usual serving. That is how a practical judgment about Lager is built.
