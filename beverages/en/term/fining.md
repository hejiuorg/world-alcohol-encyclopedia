# Fining

Fining is the addition of a small amount of an agent to wine so that it binds suspended or dissolved target substances, forms larger particles and settles, improving clarity and stability. It works through chemical reaction and adsorption; [filtration](ref:filtration) works through physical retention. They are different routes, though production often uses them together.![Fining thematic illustration](/media/photos/theme-wine-spirits.jpg)

## Definition and scope

The boundary of fining is "using an agent to carry a target away". The target may be protein, excess [tannin](ref:tannin), pigment, some phenolics or a mild off-flavour; the agent may be a mineral, an animal protein, a synthetic polymer or a polysaccharide. Fining is not filtration: after fining, racking or filtration is usually still needed to separate the floc completely.

Nor is fining a cure for every stability problem. Protein stability is mainly achieved with bentonite, tartrate stability usually with cold treatment, and microbial stability with filtration or preservatives. Treating fining as a universal correction pushes raw-material and process problems to a later stage, often after bottling.

## Etymology and origin

Fining comes from the adjective fine, meaning delicate or pure, plus -ing: the action of making wine fine. The French equivalent collage comes from coller, to glue or stick, which is why older Chinese translations use a phrase that literally means "adding glue", though the image misleads: no household glue is involved. A more neutral phrase is "clarification with agents".

Historically, agents included egg white, isinglass, gelatin and ox blood; ox blood was later banned in most regions, and bentonite and synthetic polymers became common. The "glue" in the older vocabulary is a reminder that this craft once relied heavily on animal proteins, which also explains today's vegan and allergen debates.

## Mechanism and parameters

Bentonite is a negatively charged clay that mainly adsorbs positively charged proteins, reducing the risk of protein haze in white wine after bottling. Gelatin, egg white and isinglass are positively charged protein agents that mainly bind negatively charged tannins and polyphenols, softening astringency and some colour, and are often used on red wine. PVPP is a synthetic polymer that adsorbs pigments and some phenolics, used on oxidised or browning wine. Chitosan comes from crustaceans, and silica sol addresses specific clarity problems.

Results depend on pH, temperature, alcohol, tannin concentration, particle size and mixing. The dose must be set by bench trials at the lowest effective level, because over-fining strips aroma, texture and colour. Purdue University Extension material gives the order of magnitude: wine protein around 10–300 mg/L and bentonite addition around 60–1800 mg/L, showing how widely the requirement varies between wines.

## Use in the industry

White wines are often bentonite-fined before bottling for protein stability; reds use egg white or gelatin to adjust tannin; oxidised or browning wines are treated with casein or PVPP. The EU lists authorised winemaking practices in Regulation 2019/934, and agent types and conditions sit within that framework. When egg white, dairy products or other known allergens are used, the label must say so, a requirement that comes from the labelling rules in Regulation 2019/33.

The market also creates demand for vegan and allergen information. Bentonite, PVPP and silica sol are generally treated as non-animal; egg white, gelatin, isinglass, casein and chitosan involve animal sources. Producers may state on the label or in technical sheets whether animal-derived agents were used, but rules differ between markets.

## Terms easily confused

- **Fining versus filtration**: fining is chemical adsorption and flocculation, filtration is physical retention. Fining makes fine particles larger; filtration blocks the particles. They often work together but cannot replace each other.
- **Fining versus clarification**: clarification is the result, the removal of visible suspended matter; fining is only one means, alongside settling, centrifugation and racking.
- **Bentonite versus gelatin**: bentonite mainly removes protein, gelatin mainly removes tannin, so the directions are opposite. Calling both simply "clarifiers" hides the difference in target.
- **Collage versus glue**: collage is the fining process, not household glue; the older Chinese translation should not be read literally.

## Misconceptions and disputes

First, fining is equated with "adding glue", ignoring the variety of agents and mechanisms. Second, fining is thought to thin every wine; whether it does depends on dose and trials, and over-fining is the real problem. Third, fining is expected to solve every haze; protein, tartrate, microbial and metal-related problems each need their own treatment. Fourth, allergen and vegan labelling is ignored, as if fining were a backstage operation unrelated to consumers.

The dispute centres on the labels "natural" and "technical". Traditional animal proteins are seen by some as older and gentler, while modern bentonite and synthetic agents are seen as more controllable; but "natural" has no agreed definition. Another dispute is that the sensory cost of over-fining is hard to quantify, and laboratory stability figures do not convert neatly into flavour loss. Some producers present "unfined and unfiltered" as a mark of quality, though such wines need separate management of stability risk.

## Typical figures and examples

- Wine protein content is around 10–300 mg/L; bentonite addition is around 60–1800 mg/L (Purdue University Extension).
- Common bench-trial steps are 50, 75, 100 and 125 g/hL, used to find the lowest effective dose.
- EU Regulation 2019/934 lists authorised winemaking practices; Regulation 2019/33 requires allergen indication for egg and milk.
- Example: a white wine that turns hazy in a heat test is bentonite-fined at the lowest effective bench dose, passing protein stability while losing as little aroma as possible.
- Example: egg-white fining of red wine in a 225-litre barrel can weaken fruit and colour if dosed too high, so a barrel trial is safer than treating the whole batch at once.
