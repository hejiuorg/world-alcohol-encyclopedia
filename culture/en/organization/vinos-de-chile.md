# Vinos de Chile: presenting Chilean wine as a whole abroad

Chile sells most of its wine outside the country, and an overseas buyer usually meets the name Chile before meeting any individual estate. Turning a scattered set of wineries into a recognisable whole is the reason Vinos de Chile, which uses Wines of Chile abroad, exists. Its history is short, yet it covers the period in which Chilean wine moved from volume and low prices toward regions and sustainability.![related photograph of Vinos de Chile: presenting Chilean wine as a whole abroad](/media/photos/organization-vinos-de-chile-7a79b0b2.jpg)

## Two trade bodies become one

In 2007 Viñas de Chile, which spoke for wine producers, and ChileVid, which represented exporters, merged into Asociación de Vinos de Chile A.G. The guild history compiled by the association records that the new body held its first board meeting on 5 July 2007 and elected René Merino as its first president. This was not a change of letterhead. One organisation knew growing and winemaking, the other knew overseas orders and customs clearance; putting both views into a single board gave national promotion and company exports a shared table.

In legal terms it is a private non-profit trade association, governed by its own statutes rather than by public law. It is not a ministry or a statutory authority. Its office is in Vitacura, Santiago, close to most of the country's wine institutions.

## An association, not a regulator

The association's authority comes from its members, not from legislation. Wine law, labelling requirements and export inspection belong to public bodies. The Agriculture and Livestock Service, known as SAG, inspects wine and grape products and clears exports. The association may promote, publish information, convene the trade and encourage self-regulation, but it cannot withdraw an export licence or decide on its own who may use a regional name.

That boundary is easy to miss. A regional description or a sustainability mark published by the association can look like official certification, yet enforcement and industry schemes run on separate tracks. If you want keep them apart will not mistake an industry document for a legal text.

## Membership and decision-making

Members are wine companies, ranging from family estates to large groups. Growers are not the membership unit, which distinguishes this body from organisations that admit both growers and wineries; the concerns of the vineyard reach the board through winery members. A board chosen by the members sets direction, the president speaks for the trade, and a secretariat and specialist staff run the programmes. The association publishes an annual report, the Memoria Anual, setting out the year's promotional priorities, export conditions and project results.

The member list and the board change with elections, and no stable membership total appears in public material. This organization therefore avoids a figure that would soon be out of date; the current annual report is the place to look.

## Promotion, data and sustainability certification

Promotion is the most visible output. Under the single Wines of Chile identity the association takes part in international fairs, runs tastings and works with media, placing wines from different regions inside one national story. Market intelligence is the second output: export volumes and values, destination markets and consumer trends are gathered so that members can judge where to invest. The third is sustainability. The association promotes the Sustainability Code of the Chilean Wine Industry, whose published version is 4.0, and its technology consortium, I+D Vinos de Chile, awards the Certified Sustainable Wine of Chile mark to businesses that pass the audit. The fourth is coordination, carrying views between wineries, exporters and public bodies.

The four reinforce one another. Promotion needs evidence, evidence needs member data, and sustainability gives the national image something concrete to say.

## Dates and figures that can be checked

Four checkable points stand out: the merger of the two guilds in 2007; the first board meeting on 5 July 2007; version 4.0 of the sustainability code; and the 42 million cases of export recorded in the association's 2023 annual report. These are not the same kind of number, and the year, version and unit should travel with the source rather than being compressed into a single claim.

For membership totals, promotional budgets and staff numbers, the public material offers no stable figure, so none is given here. Where secondary sources quote such numbers, the annual report and the official site should be checked.

## Where its influence comes from

Chilean wine depends heavily on exports, and buyers often form a view of the country before they form a view of an estate. The association's contribution is to make the scattered trade legible: one visual identity, one narrative, one presence at fairs. For small wineries, collective promotion lowers the cost of entering an overseas market; for large companies it supplies a national endorsement rather than a corporate one. The market data it assembles also give the trade a common reference for planning and negotiation.

That influence is indirect. It does not change the quality of a bottle, but it changes whether a buyer is willing to give the country a first chance.

## Limits and controversies

Representation is the first limit. Wineries are the members, so growers' interests reach the board indirectly and may not be fully voiced. The second is allocation: deciding how a collective promotion budget is shared between large and small members is rarely comfortable. The third is the meaning of certification. The sustainability scheme is an industry programme, voluntary in nature and no substitute for regulation; treating the mark as a legal permit misreads its force. The fourth is exposure to shocks. Climate, exchange rates, logistics and foreign trade measures are outside the association's control, and promotion can soften but not remove them. The fifth is voice: positions are the result of negotiation among members and should not be presented as the consensus of every Chilean winery.

None of these has a simple right answer. They are a reminder to ask who an industry body speaks for and how its position was reached.

## Four common misreadings

Taking the association for a government body; taking Wines of Chile for a single estate's brand; taking the sustainability mark for a mandatory standard; taking Chilean wine for one style. All four come from the same habit of confusing the national level with the legal level.
