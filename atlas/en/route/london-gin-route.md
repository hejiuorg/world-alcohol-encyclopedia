# London Gin Route

This route reads London gin through two bars: the American Bar at The Savoy for the late-nineteenth-century to 1930s cocktail tradition, and Dukes Bar in St James’s for a tableside martini that has become a London ritual. The walk is about 1.5 kilometres; the point is not how many glasses but how the same London dry gin is treated in two very different rooms.

## Route overview

Start at 17:00 in the American Bar at The Savoy with a gin and tonic or a classic cocktail, listen to the piano and read the list for about an hour; at 18:15 walk along Victoria Embankment or Northumberland Avenue toward St James’s, passing Trafalgar Square and Pall Mall, about twenty-five minutes; at 18:45 reach Dukes Bar for a tableside martini and a snack, then dinner in St James’s or Covent Garden around 19:45. With one drink, choose the Dukes martini; with the full story, do both.

This route suits a first London visit and anyone asking why gin is a British drink. It is not a bar checklist but a comparison of two rooms: the American Bar is large, bright, musical and long-listed; Dukes is small, dark, quiet and almost entirely about the martini. Both require dress and bookings, and walk-ins often get nothing.

## Gin background

Gin arrived in England from the Netherlands in the late seventeenth century, and the first half of the eighteenth century saw the “gin craze”: cheap, quick and unaged, it spawned small distilleries and bad spirits across London. The 1751 Gin Act and Hogarth’s Gin Lane fixed gin in a narrative of moral panic, but also pushed parliament and distillers to separate “good gin” from “bad spirit”. Continuous stills spread in the nineteenth century, making cleaner, more consistent base alcohol; today London Dry Gin must be distilled with juniper and other botanicals and have no added sugar, colour or flavouring, with juniper dominant.

London Dry is not a synonym for “made in London” but a distillation and recipe standard, and it can be produced elsewhere. Styles range from very dry, peppery modern gins to traditional ones with more citrus, floral and coriander notes. A gin and tonic is usually one part gin to two or three parts tonic, built over ice and finished with lemon or grapefruit peel; a martini is a ratio game between gin and dry vermouth, anywhere from 5:1 to 15:1, where temperature, dilution and stirring versus shaking decide the result.

## Stop one: the American Bar at The Savoy

The Savoy is on the Strand; the American Bar opened in 1893 and took its Art Deco form in the 1930s. Harry Craddock published The Savoy Cocktail Book in 1930, fixing a large body of classic recipes and making the room a byword for the British cocktail tradition. There is live piano, a long list and formally dressed bartenders; the atmosphere is brighter and more social than Dukes.

Start with gin: a Dry Martini to see how the bar controls temperature and dilution, a Gin Fizz or White Lady for citrus and egg-white balance, a Negroni for the gin-to-bitter ratio. If you do not want strong alcohol, ask for a low-alcohol gin-based cocktail rather than “the strongest thing”. The list is long; tell the bartender whether you lean juniper or citrus and let them choose.

The American Bar has a dress code — smart casual, no shorts, flip-flops or gym clothes — and weekends and theatre times are busy, so book ahead. The piano is loud enough for conversation but not for quiet tasting; for a slow drink, go early on a weekday afternoon.

## Stop two: Dukes Bar

Dukes Hotel is at 35 St James’s Place, and the bar has operated since 1908, one of London’s smallest hotel bars. Its signature is the tableside Dukes Martini: a bartender wheels a trolley to the table, uses a frozen glass, gin and a small amount of vermouth, stirs or shakes on the spot and serves without ice, keeping the drink very cold and very strong. Ian Fleming was a regular, and the “Fleming Martini” is often linked to this room, though the bar does not build its identity on Bond.

Dukes has no long list; the focus is the martini, gin and tonic and a few classics. The room is small, dark and short on seats, and booking is usual. When ordering a martini, say whether you want it wet or dry and whether you want lemon peel or an olive; if unsure, take the classic and do not ask for juice or syrup. The martini is strong — two is plenty, and it is not a drink to gulp.

The dress code is more formal: men should wear a jacket, women should avoid sportswear; there are no large tables, and groups over four are hard to seat. Photographs are fine without flash and without pointing at other guests; the room is for low conversation, not a party.

## Walking and dinner

The walk from The Savoy to Dukes is about 1.2 miles, twenty-five minutes: west along Victoria Embankment, turn onto Northumberland Avenue to Trafalgar Square, then along Pall Mall to St James’s Palace and into St James’s Place. The route passes some of London’s most formal buildings and clubs, and evening light makes it worth walking; in rain or heels, a taxi takes about ten minutes.

Dinner can be in St James’s, Mayfair or Covent Garden. St James’s has old-school restaurants and clubs, Mayfair is pricier and more international, Covent Garden livelier with more choice; after two martinis, pick somewhere walkable rather than crossing the city. Order lighter fish, roast or pasta rather than very spicy food, which fights the alcohol and tires the palate.

## Seasons and etiquette

London is wet year-round, and from October to March it is dark by 16:30, so start at 16:30 in winter; in summer, start from The Savoy at 18:00. Friday and Saturday nights are busiest and need earlier bookings; December and the holiday season bring events, so check the hotel websites.

Both bars have dress codes and booking rules; do not bring large luggage or wet umbrellas inside — leave the umbrella at the door and the coat with the cloakroom. Do not order for other people or assume the most expensive martini is best; telling the bartender your taste and limit is more professional than ordering the top price. Service is usually included; tip extra only for good service.

This route does not draw a map or provide live prices; hours, booking rules and lists change, so check both hotel websites before going. If one bar is full, do not wait it out — go to the other or come back another night; London has plenty of gin bars and no need to spend the evening at a door.
