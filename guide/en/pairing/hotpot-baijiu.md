# Hot Pot and Baijiu: One Hot, One Strong

Hot pot is a meal of high temperature, high fat and strong spice; baijiu is a distilled spirit with concentrated aroma and relatively high alcohol. Put together, the stimulation adds up: chill heat amplifies the sense of burning, alcohol also amplifies the heat, and fat simultaneously coats the tongue and the stomach. The question is not whether they go together, but how to make the meal comfortable and the drinking clear-headed.

## Look at the pot first, then the glass

The first variable in hot pot is the base. Clear broth, mushroom broth and tomato broth have less spice and fat and feel gentler; a beef-tallow red-oil base concentrates fat, chilli and Sichuan pepper in a single mouthful. Before pairing, ask three questions: how thick is the oil, how strong is the heat, how hot is the broth.

The variables in [baijiu](ref:baijiu) are alcohol content and aroma intensity. The higher the alcohol, the more obvious the burning on entry and the stronger the amplification of heat; the more concentrated the aroma, the better it can stand up to a heavy red-oil base, but the more easily it overwhelms the delicacy of a clear broth.

Only by looking at the base and the spirit separately can we avoid the blanket statement that "hot pot should be paired with baijiu". Clear broth with a light-aroma baijiu, red oil with a strong-aroma or sauce-aroma baijiu, are common approaches, not rules.

## The flavour structure of hot pot

The taste of hot pot does not come from heat alone. Beef tallow provides a heavy fat sensation, chilli provides burning, Sichuan pepper provides numbness, and garlic, ginger, douban and spices provide fermented and sauce-like notes; the dipping sauce adds another layer of salt and fat. The protein of meat, the oil absorbed by vegetables and the pores of tofu all change the strength on entry.

The main source of heat is capsaicin. It dissolves in fat rather than water, so an oil-based dip, sesame paste and meat fat temporarily wrap it and reduce the immediate burning; but once the fat is swallowed, the heat returns. Understanding this explains why "a sip of liquor to kill the heat" is usually only a brief illusion.

Temperature matters too. Food just lifted from the pot is very hot, and a mouthful of high-alcohol spirit at that moment doubles the burning. Let the food cool slightly, or leave a gap between two chopsticks, and the sensation becomes much clearer.

## The role of baijiu at the hot pot table

Baijiu at a hot pot table usually serves three functions: accompanying the meal, socialising and pacing. As an accompaniment it provides aroma and alcohol stimulation; socially it provides the gesture of raising a glass and a topic; in pacing, small cups drunk slowly keep the meal moving steadily.

[Strong-aroma baijiu](ref:strong-aroma) is known for cellar aroma, grain aroma and composite fragrance, usually with a fuller body, and suits heavy red oil, beef tallow and sauce-based dips. Light-aroma baijiu is cleaner and more direct, and suits clear broth, mushroom broth and vegetable-heavy pots. Sauce-aroma baijiu has a long-lasting aroma and finish, and sits well beside the fermented notes of sliced meat and dipping sauces. These are stylistic judgements, not rigid matches.

Take [Wuliangye](ref:wuliangye) as an example: a multi-grain strong-aroma product of this kind has a rich aroma profile that echoes the spices, douban and garlic in a red-oil base; but its alcohol content is also high and its entry is strong, so it suits small, slow sips rather than being used to "suppress the heat".

## Temperature and vessels

Baijiu is usually drunk at room temperature and is not recommended for long chilling. Cooling suppresses aroma release and dulls the burning on entry, so drinkers easily underestimate how much they have consumed. If you want it fresher in summer, chill it briefly, but do not freeze away the aroma.

The vessel affects how much enters the mouth each time. At a hot pot table, small cups of 10 to 15 millilitres are common, one cup at a time, which makes pacing easy to control; switching to a large glass or drinking straight from a sharing bottle clearly raises the volume per sip. The small cup is not formality: it breaks "how much I drink" into perceptible small units.

## Pace and quantity

A hot pot meal is long and the pot keeps boiling, so people easily raise a glass continuously while eating. A suggested pace is: eat two mouthfuls, take one small sip, and clear the palate with water or unsweetened tea in between; do not replace water with liquor, and do not chase the heat with liquor.

The higher the alcohol, the longer the interval should be. Baijiu at 52% vol and at 38% vol differ clearly in alcohol for the same volume; calculated as pure alcohol, the former is about 1.37 times the latter. Converting "how many cups" into "how much alcohol" is more reliable than relying on feeling.

If someone at the table does not drink, or needs to drive, hot pot does not lack alternatives: sour plum drink, soy milk, unsweetened tea and sparkling water all provide palate cleansing and cooling, so baijiu need not be treated as a necessity.

## Differences between ingredients

Ingredients such as tripe and duck intestine, which need quick dipping, are crisp and absorb relatively little oil, so the overlap with liquor is lighter; fatty beef and fatty lamb are rich and oily, and feel heavier with high-alcohol spirits. Vegetables absorb oil noticeably, especially leafy greens, whose fat intake may exceed that of meat; tofu products are porous and absorb both broth and oil.

Seafood and river fish in a red-oil pot are covered by spices, and a light-aroma baijiu makes the fresh taste easier to distinguish than a strong-aroma one; but a heavy dipping sauce erases the difference. Staples such as noodles, rice noodles and rice usually appear at the end, when the better choice is to reduce drinking rather than open another bottle.

## The variables of dips and side dishes

Dipping sauces are often ignored but directly determine entry strength. The sesame-oil and garlic dip common in Sichuan and Chongqing is mainly fat and garlic aroma and forms a film on the tongue; the sesame-paste dip of the north is thicker, with prominent salt and sesame notes; a dry dip of chilli powder, ground Sichuan pepper and salt is concentrated and short-lived. The saltier and oilier the dip, the more it calls for small sips, so that the palate is not continuously covered.

Side dishes also change the pace. Sweet garlic, pickles and cold dishes provide acidity and crunch and can clear the palate between two pieces of meat; sour plum drink, soy milk and iced jelly provide sweetness and cooling, but they cannot replace control of alcohol intake. Keeping a palate-cleansing drink within reach is more effective than reaching for liquor to kill the heat.

## Common misconceptions

First, the hotter the dish, the stronger the spirit should be. Alcohol amplifies the heat, and a high-alcohol spirit cannot "suppress" it; it only concentrates the burning.

Second, iced baijiu kills heat better. Low temperature temporarily numbs the mouth but hides the amount consumed and costs the aroma.

Third, use liquor instead of water. Alcohol is diuretic, and hot pot already makes you sweat; dehydration makes the next day harder.

Fourth, bring only one spirit for every kind of base. Clear broth and red oil do not ask the same things of aroma and alcohol content.

Fifth, treat "being able to drink" as the pairing standard. The goal is the comfort of the whole meal, not a drinking contest.
