# Rosso di Montalcino

Rosso di Montalcino is a red wine from the Montalcino area of Tuscany, Italy, classed as DOC and made from Sangiovese. It shares the area and the variety with Brunello but has shorter ageing rules and a lighter style, usually released the year after harvest. It is commonly 750 mL at about 13%–14% vol.

## Definition and position

Start by separating the levels: [red wine](ref:red-wine) is the category, Sangiovese is the variety, Tuscany is the region, Montalcino is the producing commune, Rosso is the DOC-level wine, and [Brunello](ref:brunello) is the DOCG-level wine of the same area. Keeping these apart stops "Rosso" from being read as another name for Brunello.

Rosso and Brunello are more like two tiers from the same ground: the same variety and overlapping area, but different yield ceilings, minimum alcohol and ageing requirements. Rosso is not a reject batch; it is deliberately made to be earlier to drink and lighter.

## Producer and brand

The Montalcino area has many family estates, cooperatives and merchants, and many estates make both Rosso and Brunello from the same parcels as two product lines. Rosso is often used as the entry point to Brunello, but estates do not all follow the same strategy.

The bottler information, estate name and vintage on the label are the basis for identity. Rosso has no Riserva tier, so a "Riserva" label should be checked against another appellation or wine rather than assumed to belong to Rosso.

## Region and ingredients

The appellation lies in the Montalcino hills of central [Italy](ref:italy), south of Siena, at relatively high altitude with ample sun and day-night temperature swings that favour acidity and aroma. The Mediterranean climate has hot dry summers, and autumn rain shapes the harvest.

Soils are mainly galestro schist, limestone and marine clay, free-draining and low in nutrients. The raw material is [grape](ref:grape), of the local type of [Sangiovese](ref:sangiovese), with thick skins and high acidity, its colour and tannin varying markedly with ripeness.

## Winemaking

Harvest usually runs from late September to October; after destemming, maceration is shorter than for Brunello so that fruit and freshness are preserved. Malolactic conversion follows to soften acidity. Some estates use stainless steel to keep the wine fresh, others use old [oak](ref:oak-ageing) for texture.

Rosso has much shorter ageing requirements than Brunello and can usually be released the year after harvest; the exact month and minimum ageing period are set by the production rules. It does not go through Brunello's long combination of cask and bottle ageing, so the fruit is more direct and the tannin more approachable.

## Specification and label

The common volume is 750 mL, with [alcohol by volume](ref:abv) at about 13%–14% vol, varying with vintage and estate. The label should be checked for the name Rosso di Montalcino, the DOC mark, vintage, estate name, bottler, net volume and importer information.

The Chinese back label and importer information are the entry point for checking identity. Label languages differ by market, so the bottle on hand governs; products described as "Montalcino style" outside the area are not appellation wine.

## Flavour and tasting

Typical aromas are sour cherry, red plum, violet, tomato leaf and dried hay, with some wines showing spice and earth. The palate has medium-high acidity, medium tannin and a light to medium body with a fresh finish; compared with Brunello it lacks the dried fruit and leather layers that long ageing brings.

Serve at about 16–18 degrees Celsius in a medium-sized glass; it usually needs no long decant and can be served after a short rest. High acidity is a Sangiovese trait and feels more balanced with food.

## Drinking and pairing

It pairs well with tomato-based pasta, roasted poultry, sausages, mushrooms and medium-strength cheese; high acidity works especially well with fat and tomato. It does not suit very sweet desserts, steamed seafood or heavily spicy dishes.

After opening, refrigerate and finish within a few days. Rosso is designed for early drinking and most wines are not for long cellaring, though the window still depends on vintage and estate method. Excessive drinking harms health and minors should not drink.

## Buying and storage

Buy from reputable channels and check vintage, the DOC mark, estate name, bottler and importer information. Prices move with vintage, estate reputation and channel, so no figure is given here; Rosso and Brunello are different tiers and a price difference does not translate directly into a quality difference.

For Rosso di Montalcino, unopened bottles should be kept away from light, lying flat, in a cool place at 12–15 degrees Celsius, avoiding heat and sharp temperature swings. Cork-sealed bottles need humidity; after opening, refrigerate and drink soon rather than warming and cooling repeatedly.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating Rosso as a reject or failed batch of Brunello. They are different tiers with different aims.
Second, assuming Rosso and Brunello use different varieties. Both are made from Sangiovese.
Third, assuming Rosso must be cellared. It is positioned for earlier drinking.
Fourth, assuming a lower price always means lower quality. Tier, vintage and estate all affect price.
Fifth, confusing Montalcino with neighbouring appellations. The delimited area and rules differ.
