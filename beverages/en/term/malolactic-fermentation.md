# Malolactic fermentation

Malolactic fermentation, usually shortened to MLF, is a secondary fermentation in winemaking, but the agent is not yeast. Lactic acid bacteria convert malic acid in the wine into lactic acid and a small amount of carbon dioxide. The result is not alcohol but a change in the structure of acidity.

## Definition and boundaries

MLF can be defined by three elements: the substrate is malic acid, the products are lactic acid and carbon dioxide, and the agent is lactic acid bacteria. It usually follows alcoholic fermentation, but it can also be started during or after that fermentation by inoculation. It does not turn sugar into alcohol, so it is not a "second alcoholic fermentation", and it is not the same as every deacidification technique. Adding tartaric acid, calcium carbonate or using ion exchange belongs to a different class of operations.

The boundary also includes completion. MLF may convert only part of the malic acid, or stall midway. An unfinished MLF can keep changing in bottle, bringing cloudiness or spritz. Producers therefore use paper chromatography, enzymatic tests or chromatography to measure residual malic acid and judge whether the process has ended.

## Origin of the name

Malolactic joins malic and lactic, literally "malic to lactic". The English term secondary fermentation is also used, but that phrase is applied to the second fermentation of sparkling wine as well. The two are not the same: sparkling wine's second fermentation is still yeast turning sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, while MLF is bacteria reworking acids.

The confusion comes from the word secondary. Anything after alcoholic fermentation may be called secondary, yet yeast determines alcohol and bacteria determine acid structure. When a technical text says secondary fermentation, first ask who is fermenting and what the substrate is.

## The biochemical mechanism

Malic acid is a dicarboxylic acid with two dissociable protons; lactic acid is a monocarboxylic acid with one. MLF decarboxylates malic acid into lactic acid and carbon dioxide, so total acidity falls and pH rises slightly. The reaction is catalysed by the malolactic enzyme. It does not yield large amounts of energy in the ATP sense, but it helps lactic acid bacteria survive in wine.

Malic acid in grape juice varies with variety, ripeness and region, commonly around 1-4 g/L. The fall in total acidity depends on the starting amount, and the pH rise is commonly 0.1-0.3. For a high-acid red wine this is softening; for a white wine prized for freshness it can be a loss. Understanding the mechanism is how one decides whether deacidification is the goal or a side effect.

## Process parameters and control

Bacterial activity depends on temperature, pH, alcohol and sulfur dioxide. Inoculation is commonly around 18-22 °C; below 15 °C the process slows markedly, while above 25 °C the risk of volatile acidity and off-flavours rises. pH 3.2-3.6 is a favourable window, and below pH 3.0 the fermentation is often sluggish or fails to start. Once alcohol reaches about 10% v/v, bacterial tolerance falls; free sulfur dioxide directly inhibits bacteria, so sulfur use around inoculation must be controlled.

There are two main routes: sequential inoculation after alcoholic fermentation, or co-inoculation during alcoholic fermentation. Sequential inoculation makes it easier to observe each fermentation separately, but the wine already contains alcohol and starts more slowly. Co-inoculation shortens the total fermentation time but demands tighter temperature and nutrient management. In either route, nutrient deficiency, temperature swings and excess SO2 are common causes of a stall.

## How it is used in the industry

Red wines generally undergo or permit MLF because their acidity is higher and their structure firmer. Deacidification makes them easier to drink and more stable for later ageing. Some reds deliberately retain part of the malic acid to keep freshness, depending on variety, region and vintage.

[White wine](ref:white-wine) varies by style. Whites seeking roundness and creamy texture may undergo MLF, as in some Burgundian Chardonnay; whites seeking freshness and high acidity usually avoid it, as in most Riesling and Sauvignon Blanc. Sparkling wine base wines are often blocked from MLF to preserve acidity and a crisp bubble character. The different strategies for [red wine](ref:red-wine) and white wine show that MLF is a stylistic tool, not a fixed step.

## Sensory change: more than butter

The most direct change is lower acidity and a softer mouthfeel. MLF also changes aroma and texture: bacterial metabolism can bring creamy, buttery or yoghurt-like notes, add roundness, and shift the expression of some fruit. These effects depend on strain, temperature, time and wine nutrition, so they cannot be generalised across all wines.

Buttery notes are often attributed to MLF, but the more precise source is diacetyl. Diacetyl comes from bacterial metabolism of citrate; in moderation it reads as butter or cream, in excess as butterscotch or margarine. Not every MLF produces obvious diacetyl, and strain and fermentation conditions decide the level. Equating butter flavour with MLF mistakes one by-product for the whole process.

## Terms easily confused

The first pair is MLF and alcoholic fermentation. The first is carried out by lactic acid bacteria with malic acid as substrate and lactic acid as product; the second by yeast with sugar as substrate and ethanol and carbon dioxide as products. The second pair is MLF and "secondary fermentation". In sparkling wine, secondary fermentation means yeast fermentation in bottle; in still-wine technology, MLF is sometimes called secondary fermentation, but the reactions differ.

The third pair is MLF and manual deacidification. Adding tartaric acid is acidification; adding calcium carbonate or using ion exchange is deacidification. Neither depends on bacteria or changes the acid species. The fourth pair is MLF and lactic acid bacteria spoilage. MLF is controlled bacterial activity; spoilage is uncontrolled bacterial activity. The same organism can bring style or fault depending on conditions.

## Common misuses and disputes

First, calling MLF a "second alcoholic fermentation". Second, assuming every wine needs MLF. Third, treating lactic acid bacteria as probiotics; in wine they are process organisms, and poor control can still produce biogenic amines and off-flavours. Fourth, assuming lower acidity is always better; for high-acid styles, keeping malic acid is a deliberate choice.

Disputes centre on inoculation strategy and biogenic amines. Supporters of commercial inoculation stress control and reliability; supporters of native fermentation stress complexity and local microbiology, and both approaches have failures. Biogenic amines such as histamine receive attention in some wines, and their formation depends on strain, hygiene and fermentation management; for sensitive people this is a risk that labels and producers may need to address. Another dispute is the claim that MLF always softens a wine: in some whites it strips freshness and can make fruit seem dull.

## Typical figures and examples

Common parameters: inoculation around 18-22 °C; pH 3.2-3.6 favourable, below 3.0 prone to failure; alcohol above about 10% v/v inhibits bacteria; malic acid commonly 1-4 g/L; a completed conversion raises pH by about 0.1-0.3. Analytically, residual malic acid below about 0.1 g/L is often treated as completion, though methods and thresholds vary.

Concrete examples: a Cabernet Sauvignon at pH 3.2 and 7 g/L total acidity may fall to 5.5-6.5 g/L after MLF, with a clearly softer palate; a Riesling at pH 3.0 that undergoes MLF may lose its signature briskness. Understanding these figures alongside the stylistic goal matters more than remembering the abbreviation.
