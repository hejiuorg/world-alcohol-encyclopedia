# Tian Gong Kai Wu (chapter on qu and malt)

Tian Gong Kai Wu is a technological compendium compiled by Song Yingxing in the Ming dynasty and first printed in 1637. It is divided by branches of production, with chapters on grain, textiles, dyeing, salt, sugar, ceramics, metallurgy, ships, paper and weapons. The chapter directly relevant to wine is the seventeenth, on qu and malt, which records the making of wine qu and red qu and is a key text for understanding late-imperial qu-making.

## Author and background

Song Yingxing (1587–1666), courtesy name Changgeng, came from Fengxin in Jiangxi. He passed the provincial examination in 1615 but repeatedly failed the metropolitan examination, and turned instead to practical studies. He served as an instructor at Fenyi, as a prefectural judge in Tingzhou and as a magistrate in Bozhou, and these posts brought him into contact with artisans and local production.

The title means something like “the works of heaven are opened by human craft”: nature supplies the material, and human skill turns it into an object. Written in the Chongzhen period, at a time of fiscal strain and rapid change in handicraft production, the book's attention to cost, output and procedure has an obviously practical purpose.

The work was printed in Nanchang in 1637 with the financial support of his friend Tu Shaokui.

## Editions and structure

The book has three volumes and eighteen chapters. Their order runs from grain, clothing, dyeing and milling through salt, sugar, ceramics, casting, ships, forging, lime and oil, paper, metals, weapons, pigments, qu and malt, to pearls and jade. The qu chapter stands seventeenth, between pigments and gems, near the end of the work.

Each chapter follows a similar method: first the raw material and its uses, then the process in stages, with tools, operations and criteria for success. The woodblock illustrations are an integral part of the book, recording mills, looms, kilns and boats, as well as the spaces and movements of qu-making.

The textual history is complicated. Beyond the first printing there were Qing reprints and collectanea, and in modern times collated, annotated, translated and facsimile editions. Illustrations were often redrawn in copying and reprinting, so lines and details may be distorted and cannot all be treated as original Ming images. Pan Jixing's annotated translation, published by Shanghai Classics Publishing House in 2008, is a standard modern edition.

## Core content: the qu chapter

The chapter opens with a statement that sets qu at the centre: “Every brewing of wine must rely on qu as its guarantee; without qu, even fine rice and choice millet will not ferment.” Qu is not an additive but the precondition for fermentation. The chapter treats wine qu first and red qu second.

The wine-qu section records proportions of raw material, the addition of water, treading the mixture into blocks, culturing them in a room, turning them, drying and storing. Success is judged by colour, smell and the growth of mould inside the block; the timing of turning depends on temperature and humidity, and doing it too early or too late spoils the qu. The description has no microbiology, yet the operational logic of controlling the environment and watching for change is complete.

The red-qu section records soaking and steaming rice, inoculation, keeping it warm, reddening and drying. Red qu requires particular conditions of temperature and moisture and must be protected from contamination. The text notes that “red qu is a method of recent times”, showing that the author knew this was a relatively late technique rather than an ancient inheritance.

## Wine-cultural value: qu, red qu and colour

Culturally, the chapter's first value is that it explains qu. Yellow-wine brewing depends on wheat qu, rice qu and wine starters, and the type of qu shapes saccharification speed, fermentation temperature and final flavour. The chapter preserves names, raw materials and procedures, allowing later readers to put the names back into a working process.

The second value is red qu. Red qu serves as a brewing agent, a food colourant and a medicine. Red-qu yellow wines in Fujian and Zhejiang, and dishes cooked with red ferment, trace back to this kind of technique. By placing the making and the uses of red qu together, the chapter shows that it had already moved beyond brewing into food and pharmacy.

The third value is comparison. Read against the qu chapters in volume seven of Qimin Yaoshu, the text shows how qu types multiplied over nearly a thousand years: northern Wei records centre on wheat and millet, while the Ming account has finer categories and clearer judgements about temperature and humidity. Read against volume 25 of Bencao Gangmu, it shows how brewing qu and medicinal qu borrowed from each other in the same period.

## Limits and controversies

First, the geographical and sample limits. Most of what is recorded comes from the regions where the author served and travelled and cannot represent the whole country; the same name may mean different things in different places. Second, there is no microbiology and no quantitative control. Temperature, humidity and time are described as “feeling the warmth” or “waiting for the colour”, and copying such steps in modern production cannot guarantee safety or stability.

Third, editions and illustrations are problematic, so any citation of a procedure should name the edition. Fourth, the book's centre of gravity is qu-making and fermentation; it says little about stills, strength control or ageing, and cannot be used to reconstruct distillation in full. Fifth, it has no modern food-safety framework and does not discuss the health effects of alcohol.

## How to read it today

Begin with the table of contents to see where the qu chapter sits in the material-and-process system, then read the procedures one by one. For technical terms, use the notes in an annotated edition and compare the relevant chapters of Qimin Yaoshu rather than forcing modern ideas of “qu” onto the text.

For brewers, the value is historical reference, not a specification. Modern qu-making requires strain management, temperature and humidity records and hygiene control, none of which the old book covers.
