# Whole bunch

Whole-bunch fermentation (also whole cluster) means putting intact grape clusters, stems included, into the fermenter. It can be the entire crop, or only a share, with the rest destemmed and crushed. What changes is not whether fermentation happens but the environment of fermentation, the direction of extraction and the rhythm of oxidation.

## Definition and boundaries

In the strict sense the berries stay intact or nearly so and the stems remain on the cluster; the juice still ferments mainly through yeast, rather than mainly intracellularly as in carbonic maceration. It is not the same as whole-bunch pressing, where white grapes are left whole at the press to extract juice gently. The former puts stems into fermentation so they take part in maceration; the latter is a pressing method. The two can occur together, but they are different concepts. Calling whole-bunch fermentation a "drink category" or a "grade" is also inaccurate; it is a process choice. In practice the proportion runs along a 0%–100% scale; some producers take a middle route at 20%–50% whole bunch to combine structure with control, rather than treating it as all or nothing.

## Etymology and origin

Whole bunch and whole cluster are English descriptive terms, not legal French terminology. French uses vendange entière or fermentation en grappes entières. Modern use in Burgundy and the Rhône is linked to a later twentieth-century rethinking of stems, oxidation and extraction; before that, destemming and crushing had become the norm. Today it is both a continuation of traditional technique and a style statement, often used by producers to signal a lower-intervention approach. In the 1980s and 1990s some producers in Burgundy and the Rhône re-emphasised whole clusters, linking the choice to stem ripeness, oxidation management and less mechanical crushing; the practice later spread to the New World.

## Mechanism and parameters

Stems are usually about 3%–5% of cluster weight but occupy 20%–30% of fermenter volume. Intact stems slowly release lignin, phenolics and potassium: phenolics give a firmer frame and spice, while potassium raises pH, often estimated at 0.1–0.2 units, and reduces the effectiveness of sulfur dioxide. Stems also alter heat distribution and can slow fermentation. Proportion, stem ripeness, variety, vessel and temperature all shape the result; the same 50% whole bunch can make two very different wines in a ripe and an unripe year. Potassium comes from the stem cell fluid, and the resulting pH rise shifts anthocyanins toward blue and lowers the molecular share of sulfur dioxide, so whole-bunch wines often need more careful oxidation and sulfur management during fermentation and ageing. Stem tannin also differs in structure from skin tannin: it is often described as more linear and drier, while skin tannin feels rounder.

## Practical use in the industry

In Burgundy, Pinot Noir is often partly whole-bunched to add structure, spice and freshness, and Domaine de la Romanée-Conti is known for close to 100% whole bunch; Syrah in the Rhône, especially Côte-Rôtie and Cornas in the north, also commonly uses whole clusters. Proportions range from 10% to 100%: in ripe years the stems are more lignified and can take a higher share, while in unripe years the share is reduced to avoid greenness and bitterness. White grapes can be pressed whole, but that is a pressing technique and should not be confused with stem-included fermentation. Whole clusters also have an operational effect: intact bunches create voids in the tank, reduce pumping and skin damage, and slow early oxidation; the voids can also make temperature distribution uneven, requiring more frequent punch-downs or pump-overs. For wines intended for long ageing, producers often use whole bunch to add frame rather than simply to look "natural".

## Confusable terms

First, whole bunch versus carbonic maceration. Carbonic maceration relies on intracellular fermentation in a carbon dioxide environment, whereas whole-bunch fermentation still depends mainly on yeast; the two can overlap but are not synonyms. Second, whole bunch versus whole-bunch pressing. The first ferments with stems, the second presses whole clusters for juice. Third, stem inclusion versus stem addition. The first refers to stems carried in by whole clusters, the second may mean extra stems added, with different process and regulatory meanings. Fourth, whole bunch versus destemmed. Destemmed means the stems are removed, while whole bunch keeps them; the difference is the state of the raw material, not a matter of degree.

## Common misuses and disputes

First, assuming whole bunch inevitably brings bitterness; ripe stems at a moderate share can add structure and spice only. Second, assuming a higher proportion is better; too much raises pH, dilutes fruit and increases volatile acidity risk. Third, equating whole-bunch fermentation with carbonic maceration. Fourth, describing whole-bunch pressing of white grapes as whole-bunch fermentation. There is also dispute over stem "ripeness", which is hard to quantify: the same stem may taste woody and sweet to one winemaker and green to another, and houses can reach opposite conclusions about the same fruit. Another misuse is to treat "whole bunch" as a synonym for "no destemming at all"; in fact the proportion can be set to a percentage, and partly destemmed, partly whole-bunch is common.

## Typical figures and examples

Stems are about 3%–5% of cluster weight but can occupy 20%–30% of vessel volume; their pH effect is often estimated at 0.1–0.2 units. Domaine de la Romanée-Conti in Burgundy is known for close to 100% whole-bunch fermentation, and Côte-Rôtie and Cornas in the Rhône commonly use whole clusters for Syrah. A 100% whole-bunch batch with under-ripe stems may show greenness and bitterness; the same producer more often drops to 30%–50% in ripe years. Comparing 10% with 100% whole bunch shows that the former is more of an accent while the latter changes extraction and heat distribution; proportion is the first ruler.

## Recording and describing

When describing whole bunch, record at least four things: the proportion, stem ripeness, whether destemmed fruit was blended in, and vessel plus fermentation temperature. "Whole-bunch fermented" alone is insufficient; 10% and 100% are different processes under the same label. If the label gives no proportion, check the producer's technical notes or vintage report; if it is still unavailable, record "proportion unknown" rather than assuming close to 100%. When buying, treat "stem included" as a style clue: it often points to a firmer frame, pepper and herbal notes, but it does not guarantee quality; read it with vintage, variety and body.
