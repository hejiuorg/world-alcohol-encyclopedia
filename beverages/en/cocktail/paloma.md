# Paloma

The Paloma is a long drink of tequila, grapefruit flavour and lime. It is usually built over plenty of ice in a highball glass with tequila, lime juice and either grapefruit soda or fresh grapefruit juice, optionally finished with a salt rim and a grapefruit slice. Grapefruit bittersweetness, the vegetal note of tequila, lime acidity and carbonation define the drink, which ranges from pale pink to orange-red in the glass.

## Origin and naming

There is no single archive for the Paloma's origin. The widely told story places it in Mexico in the mid-twentieth century and links it to the popularity of local grapefruit-flavoured drinks; other accounts connect it to a brand or a bar promotion. The inventor, the year and the first location differ between versions, so this page treats the story as widely told rather than documented. No single date should be presented as settled fact.

"Paloma" means "dove" in Spanish and has no direct relationship to the taste. Explanations of the name include a namer's preference or a trademark or nickname for a drink, but these accounts lack consistent public records. The charm of the name should not be written as confirmed history; it is safer to say that the name belongs to Mexican Spanish naming habits and that the specific motive is unknown.

The drink's popularity in Mexico is closely tied to the spread of grapefruit-flavoured soda. The simplified version needs only tequila, lime and grapefruit soda, which is easy to reproduce at home and in bars; a more careful version uses freshly squeezed grapefruit juice and tops with soda. Both are called Paloma, but their structure, sweetness and bitterness differ, so a record should state whether it uses soda or fresh juice.

## Recipe structure

There are two common formulas. The simplified version is 50 ml tequila, 15 ml lime juice and 100 ml grapefruit soda, built over ice. The fresh-juice version is 50 ml tequila, 100 ml fresh grapefruit juice, 15 ml lime juice, 10 ml syrup and 60 ml soda water. The first is lighter, sweeter and more carbonated; the second is closer to the real bitterness and acidity of grapefruit but takes more steps.

Tequila provides the vegetal note and the alcoholic frame. A blanco or reposado both work: blanco is fresher and more grassy, reposado is rounder with a little wood sweetness. A heavily aged tequila can overwhelm the freshness of grapefruit, so it is not the only choice. Grapefruit bitterness comes from compounds such as naringin and is different from lime acidity; the two together give the sweetness more layers.

Syrup belongs mainly in the fresh-juice version, and even there it depends on how sweet the grapefruit is. Grapefruit soda already contains sugar, and adding syrup turns the drink into a sweet soft drink. The salt rim is not part of the formula, but it changes the perception of sweet and bitter: a little salt suppresses bitterness, lifts sweetness and makes the vegetal note of tequila clearer. A rim can cover the whole glass, half the rim or only one section, depending on preference.

## Flavour and balance

The entry is grapefruit acidity and mild bitterness, followed by the grassy or ripe-fruit note of tequila. Lime pushes the acidity brighter, and carbonation makes the whole drink feel light. Unlike the Margarita, which centres on lime and orange liqueur, the Paloma centres on grapefruit bittersweetness. The balance is between grapefruit bitterness, sugar and lime acidity. When bitterness dominates, a little salt or sugar softens it; when the drink is too sweet, add lime or switch to fresh juice; when it is too sour, first check whether the grapefruit itself is unusually acidic.

Temperature and carbonation matter. Grapefruit soda that is not cold loses its bubbles quickly and the sweetness turns sticky; too much ice turns the second half of the glass into pale pink water. The ideal is chilled ingredients, enough ice and drinking soon after mixing. The salt rim dissolves gradually, so the first half of the glass tastes saltier and the second half returns to the grapefruit itself.

Alcohol content is moderate, but a long drink is large and the drinking time is long. The bitterness, sweetness and bubbles of grapefruit can mask the alcohol in tequila, making the drink disappear faster than expected. Eating alongside, sipping slowly and drinking water is safer than chasing pure refreshment. Do not drink and drive, and avoid alcohol during pregnancy or while taking medication that interacts with it.

## Method

The usual method is to build the drink directly. If a salt rim is wanted, put coarse salt on a small plate, wet the outer rim of the glass with a lime wedge and dip the rim into the salt, keeping salt out of the drink. Fill the glass with ice, add the tequila and lime juice, top with grapefruit soda, or with grapefruit juice plus soda, and stir once gently. Do not shake, because carbonation will be lost.

For the fresh-juice version, shake the tequila, grapefruit juice, lime juice and syrup first, strain into an ice-filled highball, then top with soda. This evens out the acid, sugar and bitterness and preserves the aroma of the juice. The simplified version does not need shaking and can be built in the glass, but the tequila and soda should be mixed enough that the first sip is not pure spirit.

Garnish with a grapefruit slice or wedge, and perhaps a lime wedge. Cut the grapefruit to order; a dried edge hurts both aroma and appearance. Coarse salt is better for the rim than fine salt, which dissolves too quickly and pushes saltiness into the drink. If a salt rim is not wanted, omit it or salt only a small section so that the guest can choose.

## Variations

Replacing tequila with mezcal gives a smokier, more vegetal version, often called a Mezcal Paloma. Chilli or chilli syrup makes a spicy Paloma; blending the ingredients with ice makes a frozen version. Grapefruit juice with soda, or tonic water in place of grapefruit soda, produces a more bitter drink. Low-sugar grapefruit soda can reduce sweetness, but the acidity then needs rebalancing.

A variation should state what was replaced. Calling a mezcal drink a Paloma is acceptable, but "Mezcal Paloma" is clearer; replacing grapefruit soda with tonic moves the drink closer to another bitter long drink. Only when the base spirit, the citrus source and the sweetener are clear does a recipe comparison mean anything.

## How to order and drink

Start with the base and the ratio. From there, consider the dilution and the ice, then the garnish and the glass. That is how a practical judgment about Paloma is built.

The drink suits hot weather, outdoor drinking and Mexican food, grilled meat, seafood and salty snacks. Grapefruit bitterness balances fat, bubbles refresh the palate and the salt rim reinforces the savoury note. Sip slowly and avoid drinking quickly on an empty stomach; do not drink and drive, and avoid alcohol during pregnancy or while taking medication that interacts with it. As a long drink it is large, so a second glass should be considered carefully.

In a bar, if the grapefruit slice was cut long in advance, the aroma will be much weaker; if the salt rim is too thick, the first sip will be too salty. Check whether the rim is clean, whether there is enough ice and whether the bubbles are still alive. If the second half of the glass tastes thin, a squeeze of lime can help, but more syrup rarely does.

## Common misconceptions

First, treating the Paloma as a variation of the Margarita. Both use tequila, but their citrus structure and source of sweetness are different. Second, that the salt rim is required. It is a style choice, not the definition of the drink. Third, that only fresh grapefruit juice is authentic. The grapefruit-soda version is a widespread simplification with a different flavour. Fourth, that more tequila is better. Too much alcohol overwhelms the grapefruit balance. Fifth, that grapefruit soda is sugar-free. It usually contains sugar, and that sweetness belongs in the formula.
