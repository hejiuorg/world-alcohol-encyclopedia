# Wine

Wine is an alcoholic drink made by fermenting fresh grapes or grape must. The OIV definition requires it to come entirely from fresh grapes (crushed or not) or grape must through partial or complete alcoholic fermentation, with an actual alcoholic strength of at least 8.5% vol. Under specific climatic, soil, variety or local-tradition conditions, some regional rules allow the minimum total strength to fall to 7% vol. This definition marks the boundary of “wine”, but within that boundary there is enormous stylistic variation.

## Main categories

By the presence of bubbles, wine first divides into still and sparkling. Still wine usually has a low carbon-dioxide content; sparkling wine requires carbon dioxide entirely from fermentation and an excess pressure in the bottle of at least 3.5 bar at 20 °C. By colour, still wine divides into [red wine](ref:red-wine), [white wine](ref:white-wine) and rosé. Red wine is usually fermented with skins, extracting colour and tannin; white wine is usually pressed early to limit skin contact; rosé gains its pale colour through brief maceration.

By strength and additions, there are also [sparkling wine](ref:sparkling), [fortified wine](ref:fortified) and sweet wine. Fortified wine adds grape spirit during or after fermentation; the OIV calls this liqueur wine, with an acquired strength usually between 15% and 22%. Sweetness in sweet wine may come from late harvest, noble rot, ice wine or stopped fermentation, and is not the same as added sugar.

## From grape to wine

The basic flow includes harvest, sorting, crushing or pressing, fermentation, pressing (for reds), malolactic conversion (optional), ageing, clarification and bottling. Yeast converts sugar in the juice into ethanol and carbon dioxide; fermentation temperature, yeast strain, oxygen contact and nutrition all affect aroma and mouthfeel.

Red-wine maceration can last from a few days to several weeks, with punch-downs, pump-overs and pressing pressure controlling extraction of colour, tannin and aroma. White wine is usually fermented without skins at lower temperatures to preserve delicate aromas; malolactic conversion, lees ageing and oak use all change texture and flavour. The key to rosé is brief maceration, with colour depth depending on time and variety.

## Sweetness and strength

The OIV gives verifiable sugar thresholds for still wine. Dry wine contains at most 4 g/L sugar, or 9 g/L when total acidity is no more than 2 g/L below the sugar content. Medium dry may reach 12 g/L, or 18 g/L under specified acidity conditions. Semi-sweet may reach 45 g/L. Sweet wine has a minimum of 45 g/L. For sparkling wine, Brut has at most 12 g/L residual sugar with a 3 g/L tolerance. A sweetness label describes a residual-sugar range, not subjective sweetness.

Strength is determined by fermentable sugar, yeast tolerance and whether the wine is fortified. Most still wines sit in the low-to-mid teens, while fortified wines and some sweet wines are higher. Strength is not a quality indicator; within one category, different stylistic positions produce different strengths.

## Vintage, region and labels

Vintage is the grape harvest year, not the bottling year. Weather affects ripeness, yield and disease pressure in a given year, but skilled producers can keep a relatively stable style across vintages. Treating a “great vintage” as an absolute conclusion easily overlooks the specific wine and producer.

Labels such as AOC, PDO and DOC are primarily regulatory and geographical, not quality scores. The EU PDO/PGI system, France's AOC and Italy's DOCG write permitted varieties, minimum planting density, harvest yields, winemaking methods and label terms into their rules. For specific wines, look at a Bordeaux blend such as [Château Lafite Rothschild](ref:lafite) or a vintage Champagne such as [Dom Pérignon](ref:dom-perignon). Within one region there is still much variation: village, plot, vintage and producer all matter.

## Terroir and winemaking

Terroir usually refers to the combined effect of climate, soil, topography and long-standing human practice on a product's regional character. It is a useful explanatory framework, not a universal formula. Soil affects fruit indirectly through water, nutrients and ripeness; climate determines the balance of acidity and sugar; human choices — harvest date, fermentation temperature, maceration length and ageing vessel — also shape style. Reducing terroir to “a certain soil determines a certain flavour” ignores both scientific evidence and producer choices.

## How to drink

White wine and light reds usually suit lower temperatures, while full reds and fortified wines can be served a little warmer. Too warm and the alcohol stands out; too cool and the aroma closes down. Decanting mainly helps young, tannic wines meet air or separates sediment from older wines; not every wine needs it. When tasting, look first at colour and clarity, then aroma, then acidity, tannin, body and finish.

For food, high-acid wines suit sour or fried dishes, high-tannin reds suit dishes rich in protein and fat, and when pairing sweet wine with dessert the wine should be at least as sweet as the dessert. Rather than applying “red with meat, white with fish” mechanically, look at the wine's structure and the dish's seasoning.

## Boundary

This style describes categories and basic methods, not specific estates, vintages or prices. Check the label, producer information and professional reviews for grape proportions, residual sugar, strength, ageing potential and drinking window. Definitions and categories can be checked against the OIV and GB/T 17204-2021, but a specific product's compliance also depends on the rules of its market.
