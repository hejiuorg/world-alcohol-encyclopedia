# Japan Sake and Shochu Makers Association: The Trade Body for Sake, Shochu and Mirin

Sake does not exist on its own in Japan. Honkaku shochu, awamori and hon mirin belong to the same family of traditional drinks, and their producers face the same liquor tax system, the same consumers and the same export market. The Japan Sake and Shochu Makers Association (JSS) is the national trade body that brings those producers together.

## What kind of organisation it is

The Japanese name is Nihon Shuzo Kumiai Chuokai, and the English name is Japan Sake and Shochu Makers Association, abbreviated JSS. It was founded in December 1953 under the Act on Securing of Liquor Tax and on Liquor Business Associations, and it is a non-profit trade organisation.

Its members include producers of sake, honkaku shochu, awamori and hon mirin. Public material often mentions about 1600 member companies; the figure changes with consolidation in the trade and should not be treated as permanent. Its membership is not a single drink but several categories of traditional Japanese alcohol, a point often missed by readers who think only of sake.

It is neither a government department nor an enforcement agency. Licensing, tax and some technical standards belong to government; the association's role is coordination, promotion, research and information exchange.

## From 1953

In December 1953, amid the post-war reconstruction of Japan's liquor tax system and trade, the organisation was established. The legal framework of the time joined the securing of liquor tax with liquor trade associations, so from the start the association carried several tasks at once: stabilising the trade, protecting tax revenue and coordinating business.

Since then its work has followed the Japanese drinks market: from post-war recovery and domestic consumption to sake exports and product diversification, and more recently to the protection and promotion of traditional brewing. In 2024 the traditional brewing of Japanese sake was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity; the association was one of the important forces behind the nomination and its promotion.

## What it does

First, promotion. The association promotes sake, honkaku shochu, awamori and hon mirin in Japan and abroad, organising events, publishing material and supporting communication in export markets.

Second, technical research. It supports research on brewing and distillation, covering raw materials, fermentation, quality stability and food safety. Terms such as the [polishing ratio](ref:polishing-ratio) of sake are an entry point for consumers and a basis for technical communication in the trade.

Third, statistics and information. It collects production, sales and export data and provides an overview of the trade to members and the public. Such data do not determine any one brewery's business, but they shape the industry's reading of the market.

Fourth, coordination. It acts as a channel between members and between the trade and government, and takes part in protecting and passing on traditional brewing.

## Members and standards

The association's members are producers of sake, honkaku shochu, awamori and hon mirin, and the exact composition changes with the structure of the trade, so a fixed membership figure should not be treated as permanent.

Standards here means above all liquor tax, quality labelling and trade specifications. Sake's specific designations, such as junmai or ginjo, have their own labelling rules; these are formed by government and the trade together, with the association coordinating rather than replacing regulation. What may appear on a label ultimately depends on the law and labelling rules.

## What it means for readers

For consumers, the association's material is one way into sake and shochu, but a purchase should still be checked against the legal information on the label, and readers may consult [Introduction to Sake](ref:sake-intro). Sake is the representative grain-fermented drink of [sake](ref:sake), but sake and shochu are different crafts: the first is mainly fermented, the second distilled.

For people in the trade, the association's statistics and positions are a reference for reading the market, but they are not proof of any one brewery's quality. The brewery, the rice, the polishing ratio and the brewing year are the specific variables.

## Common misreadings

First, taking the association as a government enforcement agency. Licensing and tax belong to government; the association is a trade body.

Second, taking it as a sake-only organisation. It also covers honkaku shochu, awamori and hon mirin.

Third, taking Japanese sake as a general name for all Japanese alcohol. In Japanese usage the term usually means sake specifically, while shochu is a separate distilled drink.

Fourth, taking the association's promotion as a guarantee of quality. Promotion speaks for the trade as a whole, not for every bottle.

The Japan Sake and Shochu Makers Association is a national industry organisation for sake, honkaku shochu, awamori and mirin producers. It works on promotion, technical research, statistics and industry coordination, while licensing and tax policy sit with government bodies. It is not an enforcement agency. When reading sake statistics, distinguish association surveys from national tax-agency licensing data and company-reported figures.
