# Tequila NOM-006

Tequila is one of the most heavily protected drinks in the world. The protection is not a slogan; it is a three-layer structure: a 1977 appellation declaration, the 2012 Official Standard NOM-006-SCFI-2012, and certification by the Tequila Regulatory Council. Remove any layer and the name cannot legally be used.

What makes the system unusual is that it writes a plant species into law. Only blue agave, Agave tequilana Weber var. azul, may be used to make tequila. Other agaves can be distilled, but the resulting spirit cannot carry the name.

## Appellation and standard: two different instruments

The appellation, or Denominación de Origen, is an intellectual-property right held by the Mexican state. It rests on the protection declaration published in the official gazette on 13 October 1977. It defines the territory and bars producers outside it from using the name.

NOM is a mandatory technical standard. It specifies raw materials, process, alcohol content, labelling and test methods. Stack the two and the Tequila Regulatory Council becomes the only body authorised to assess conformity. It certifies and traces: every lot must be linkable to a field and a producer.

## Raw material: one species only

Blue agave is the only permitted source. Growers remove the leaves at maturity, a step called jima, leaving a pineapple-shaped heart that is cooked. Cooking hydrolyses inulin into fermentable sugars; the juice is then pressed, fermented and distilled.

From field to bottle, the Council cites a cycle of 5 to 8 years. That timescale explains why tequila prices react so sharply to agave harvests: a distillery cannot expand output within a year. It has to wait for plants in the field.

## Two classes: tequila and 100% agave

The standard divides products into two classes. The first is simply "Tequila": before fermentation, at least 51% of the reducing sugars must come from blue agave, while the remaining 49% may come from other sugar sources. The second is "100% de agave": all sugars come from blue agave, and the product must be bottled inside the territory at an authorised plant.

That difference is printed on the label. A bottle marked 100% agave may not be bottled outside the region and may not use other sugars; a bottle marked Tequila may be a blend of sources. Both are legal, but they differ in flavour and cost, and the consumer has to know which word is which. That is also why tequila is treated as a national drink in Mexico, and why it carries weight in export earnings and tourism.

Both classes must fall between 35% and 55% alcohol by volume. Outside that band, even correct raw material and correct origin cannot be sold as tequila.

## Five ageing classes

- Blanco or Silver: unaged, or rested briefly in inert containers.
- Joven or Gold: an unaged base, possibly blended with aged spirit; colour may come from the aged component or from permitted colouring.
- Reposado: at least 2 months in oak.
- Añejo: at least 12 months in oak containers smaller than 600 litres.
- Extra Añejo: at least 3 years.

The classes describe style, not rank. Blanco keeps the vegetal edge of agave, Reposado adds vanilla and caramel, and Añejo rewards slow drinking. The market favours aged expressions, but distilleries need cash flow, so Blanco remains the largest category by volume.

## The territory: 181 municipalities in 5 states

The appellation covers 181 municipalities: all 125 in Jalisco, 8 in Nayarit, 7 in Guanajuato, 11 in Tamaulipas and 30 in Michoacán. The core sits on the volcanic plateau around Guadalajara, where altitude and diurnal temperature range shape sugar accumulation in the plants.

The boundary is administrative, not natural. A field with similar climate may fall inside or outside the line. For a distillery, crossing a municipal border can mean losing the right to the word tequila.

## Certification, labels and enforcement

Council certification runs from field to bottling. Each lot needs an analytical report, production records and a traceability number. Labels must show the class name, alcohol content, net volume, producer or bottler details, the state of production, and either the 100% agave claim or the appropriate blended designation. Exports to the EU also enjoy geographical-indication protection, and the EU Official Journal has listed all 181 municipalities.

Enforcement is shared between the Mexican economy ministry and the Council. Typical breaches include substituting another agave for the blue variety, labelling a blended product as 100% agave, and bottling outside the territory while keeping the name. Penalties range from delisting to fines and loss of certification.

## Disputes: scarcity, craft and boundaries

The first dispute is raw material. Agave takes years to mature, so demand spikes create shortages and prices can double within two years. Distillers respond with contract farming or denser planting.

The second is the tension between industry and craft. Smallholders and traditional distillers argue that the standard turned tequila into a big-company game; the large houses reply that the standard is exactly what guarantees quality and traceability.

The third is the border with mezcal. Both are agave spirits, but territory, process and ageing differ. A clear border helps consumers choose; a blurry one helps marketers.
