# Islay

Islay lies off the west coast of Scotland, one of the southernmost of the Inner Hebrides, about 600 square kilometres in area with a population of roughly 3200. It holds one of the densest clusters of distilleries in Scotland, from Laphroaig, Lagavulin and Ardbeg on the south shore to Caol Ila and Bunnahabhain in the north, and Bruichladdich, Kilchoman and Ardnahoe, which started production in 2018, on the west. It is both a protected locality under UK law and a byword for heavily peated whisky; those two meanings need separating.

## Legal status and boundary

Under the Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009, Islay is a protected locality alongside Highland, Lowland, Speyside and Campbeltown. A Scotch whisky labelled Islay must be distilled on the island and must still meet every legal requirement for Scotch: matured in Scotland, in oak casks for at least 3 years, and bottled at no less than 40% ABV. The locality states origin; it is not a quality grade and does not guarantee a flavour. The island is long and narrow, with distilleries at both ends and in the middle, and roads and ferries shape how raw materials and finished bottles move.

## Climate and peat

The island is warmed by the North Atlantic Drift, with mild winters, cool summers, frequent rain, strong wind and consistently high humidity. This climate is poor for drying grain but good for long cask maturation; warehouse airflow and temperature swings often explain changes in the spirit better than "sea air" does. The island has extensive peat bogs, formed from mosses, heather and plant remains accumulating under semi-oxygen-poor conditions, and the peat is used to dry germinating barley. Phenolic compounds from the smoke attach to the malt and become the source of peat flavour; differences in bog depth, plant make-up and burning temperature shift the aroma from medicinal and tarry to bonfire and seaweed.

## Water and malt supply

Distilleries draw mostly from surface water, springs or wells, for example the Uigeadail area for Ardbeg and the Kildalton area for Laphroaig. Mineral content affects saccharification and fermentation, but distilleries tend to stress reliable supply rather than water as a single flavour source. Traditional floor malting has greatly declined on the island, and most distilleries buy malt from the central maltings at Port Ellen, specifying phenol levels in ppm. That means "malted on Islay" is not the same as "island style"; the real differences come from the phenol specification, the distillation cut and the cask.

## Distillation and cuts

Island distilleries almost all use pot stills, most of them distilling twice. Still shape, lyne arm angle, heating method and condenser design set the reflux ratio and copper contact, which in turn shape oiliness, sulphur and smoke intensity. The cut is the other key: the proportion of heads and tails and the point of the cut can make the same peated malt taste completely different. Heavily peated styles often keep more tails for weight, while lightly peated styles focus on the middle cut for fruit and citrus.

## South shore versus north shore

Ardbeg, Lagavulin and Laphroaig on the south shore are often taken as heavy-peated representatives, with iodine, tar, seaweed, ash and citrus peel in the aroma; Ardbeg's malt is often quoted at about 55 ppm, Laphroaig and Lagavulin somewhat lower, though intensity in the glass also depends on distillation and maturation. Caol Ila in the north is moderately peated and leans towards citrus, oil and sea water; Bunnahabhain is mostly unpeated or lightly peated, with a thick, nutty, oily body. Bruichladdich in the west makes an unpeated core range, while its Octomore series pushes phenols above 100 ppm as an experiment in extreme smoke; Kilchoman is a farm distillery that malts, distils and bottles on the island.

## Representative distilleries and turning points

Bowmore was built in 1779 and is one of the island's oldest licensed distilleries; Ardbeg and Laphroaig took licences in 1815, with Lagavulin close behind; Caol Ila was built in 1846, and both Bruichladdich and Bunnahabhain date from 1881. Port Ellen closed in 1983 and long stood as a symbol of contraction, its surviving bottles becoming collectable; the distillery returned to production in 2024, and the gap between old and new spirit is a live case study in how revival changes style. Kilchoman started in 2005 and Ardnahoe in 2018, showing that island capacity is still expanding.

## Vintage, market and tourism

The number on a whisky label is usually the minimum age in the bottle, not a single harvest; bourbon casks dominate on Islay, with sherry and wine casks used for seasoning or finishing. In the market, heavy peat earned a premium in the single malt boom of the early 21st century, driving expansion and new builds; but peat itself faces environmental and supply questions, and some distilleries are trying unpeated products and local peat alternatives. The Feis Ile music and whisky festival each May links distilleries, ferries and guest houses into a tourism chain and makes the island economy more dependent on seasonal visitors.

## Common misconceptions

First, that Islay equals heavy peat. Unpeated and lightly peated bottlings are common, as the standard ranges of Bunnahabhain and Bruichladdich show. Second, that a higher phenol ppm means more Islay. PPM is a malt measure, and distillation cuts and maturation change intensity in the glass. Third, that island whisky must taste salty. Brand stories amplify sea air and warehouse position, so each bottling needs its own assessment. Fourth, that Islay is a quality grade. It is only a protected place name. Fifth, that more smoke is better. Style preference has no ranking; balance and completion are the measures. Sixth, that every Islay distillery malts its own barley. Most malt comes from central maltings off the island.

Islay is best approached through the appellation and its permitted varieties, then the vintage and the producer, and then how the wine is labelled. One check alone will not settle it.

**Short definition:** A whisky island in the Inner Hebrides, about 600 square kilometres in area with a population of roughly 3200. Phenols from peat-dried malt, sea air and long maturation shape its image, yet the island also makes unpeated and lightly peated whisky, so Islay is not simply a synonym for heavy peat.
