# Sémillon

Sémillon is the backbone white grape of Bordeaux and one of the world's most underrated whites. Thin-skinned, low in acidity and quick to accumulate sugar, it can make dry white wine as well as rich sweet wine under botrytis. Its strength is not high acid or loud aroma but texture, a waxy feel and the complexity that comes with age.

## Definition and synonyms

The French name is Sémillon, often written Semillon in English. It belongs to the European grape Vitis vinifera. It is widely planted in Bordeaux and Australia, with a presence in South Africa, Chile and Argentina. A label saying Sémillon usually means a single-varietal or dominant wine; in Sauternes and Barsac it is often blended with Sauvignon Blanc without stating proportions.

## Parentage and origin

The parents of Sémillon are not fully established. It is one of Bordeaux's old varieties, possibly with a long history of local crossing; genetic work has not produced a clear parent pair. What is certain is that it belongs to the Bordeaux white group with Sauvignon Blanc and Muscadelle, as a distinct variety. It was already grown around Sauternes in the 18th century, and the 19th-century trade in botrytised sweet wine spread it; British shipping later carried it to Australia, South Africa and Chile.

## Botanical character

Bunches are medium, berries small with thin golden skins, and ripening is early to medium. Thin skins are the key to botrytis susceptibility and also make grey rot more likely in wet years. It buds at mid-season, ripens fairly evenly, accumulates sugar quickly and loses acidity quickly, so its picking window is narrower than Sauvignon Blanc's. Against Sauvignon, Sémillon has larger berries, thinner skins, more restrained aroma and lower acidity.

## Viticultural behaviour

Sémillon is moderately to strongly vigorous with fairly high yields, and suits gravel, limestone and clay. It ripens well on the gravel plateau of Sauternes, where fast drainage and warming help botrytis develop in autumn; gravel in Graves suits dry white. It is prone to grey rot, so growers watch autumn weather closely to choose between "noble rot" and "grey rot". The hot, humid Hunter Valley in Australia picks extremely early to retain acidity.

## Bordeaux: Sauternes and Barsac

Sauternes and Barsac are Sémillon's most famous sweet-wine appellations. Botrytis cinerea infects the fruit on humid, foggy autumn mornings, and dry afternoons evaporate water, concentrating sugar and flavour into honey, dried apricot, ginger and saffron. Sémillon is the main variety, usually 70% to 80%, with Sauvignon Blanc adding acidity and citrus and a little Muscadelle. In the 1855 classification, Château d'Yquem was the only estate rated Premier Cru Supérieur, a status that still shapes prices and market perception.

## Dry Sémillon and blends

In Graves and Pessac-Léognan, Sémillon is blended with Sauvignon Blanc for dry white, Sémillon giving texture and waxiness and Sauvignon acidity and aroma. Proportions vary by estate and vintage, with Sémillon often 50% to 70%. Dry Sémillon is often barrel-fermented and aged on lees for roundness and toast; new oak must be restrained so as not to bury the beeswax and lemon. Single-varietal dry Sémillon is rare in Bordeaux but more common in Australia and South Africa.

## The Hunter Valley

Hunter Valley Sémillon is one of the world's most distinctive dry whites. Growers pick at very low sugar, with potential alcohol of only 10% to 11%, giving a lean, high-acid wine with lemon and green grass when young; after five to ten years in bottle it develops toast, honey, citrus marmalade and nuts, and the colour turns gold. This "early pick plus bottle age" style contrasts sharply with Bordeaux sweet wine and shows how high Sémillon's acidity can be when picked early.

## Other regions and the market

Chile, Argentina, South Africa and Washington also grow Sémillon, mostly for blends or single-varietal dry white. Chilean Sémillon is often blended with Sauvignon for a fresher style; South Africa has old-vine resources and makes both dry and sweet versions. In the market, Sauternes has long been the benchmark for fine sweet wine but volumes are limited; dry Sémillon draws more attention in Australia and the United Kingdom. Heat and drought under climate change are altering botrytis conditions, a long-term challenge for Sauternes.

## Winemaking and style

Winemaking depends on the target. Sweet wine relies on botrytis, with fruit picked berry by berry and aged in oak for one to several years; dry white is often fermented in stainless steel or oak, sometimes with lees stirring and malolactic fermentation for texture. Low acidity means Sémillon does not suit long hot fermentation or excessive oxidation. Typical aromas include lemon, beeswax, lanolin, honey and apricot, with toast and nuts after ageing.

## The difference from Chenin Blanc

Sémillon and Chenin are both prone to botrytis and both make sweet wine, but their logic differs. Chenin has high acid and low pH, can make dry, off-dry, sweet and sparkling wine, and tastes sharper; Sémillon has low acid and a waxier texture, sweeter wines that feel rounder, and dry wines often blended with Sauvignon Blanc. Chenin's home is the Loire, Sémillon's is Bordeaux; Chenin is Steen in South Africa, while Sémillon is known in Australia's Hunter Valley for early-picked dry white. Reducing both to "botrytis varieties" ignores their differences in acidity and blending.

## Producers and vintages

Sauternes names include Château d'Yquem, Château Climens, Château Coutet and Château Suduiraut; dry Graves is represented by Domaine de Chevalier and Château Smith Haut Lafitte. Tyrrell's and Mount Pleasant in the Hunter Valley are known for early-picked Sémillon. Vintage differences come mainly from autumn humidity: years with good botrytis give rich, balanced sweet wine; excessively wet years bring grey rot and losses in both yield and quality.

## Common misconceptions

First, that Sémillon is only for sweet wine; Graves and Australia both make fine dry white. Second, treating Sémillon as a Sauvignon Blanc substitute; acidity and aroma are completely different. Third, thinking sweeter is always better; acidity and the purity of botrytis matter as much. Fourth, that Sémillon cannot age; good Sauternes can last for decades. Fifth, taking Hunter Valley Sémillon for an unripe sweet wine; it is a deliberately early-picked dry wine. Sixth, that Sémillon has no aroma; its beeswax and lanolin need warmth and time to show.
