# New Zealand Winegrowers: Growers and Wineries in One Organisation

New Zealand produces a modest volume of wine but exports a high share of it. A small export-oriented region needs growers and wineries to act together: growers determine the raw material, wineries determine style and market. New Zealand Winegrowers (NZW) is the body that places these stages inside one national organisation, and that is also where its difficulty lies, since the two camps do not always want the same thing.

## From a merger in 2002

In March 2002 New Zealand's grower organisation and winery organisation were merged into New Zealand Winegrowers (NZW). Public material describes it as the only unified national body for the country's grape and wine sector, and as one of the few national wine organisations in the world to combine growers and wineries.

The background was export. A small region needs a unified image abroad and an organisation able to speak for both the raw-material end and the winemaking end. If growers and wineries each spoke separately, buyers would hear two different stories. After the merger, NZW became a platform for one external voice and internal coordination.

## One organisation, two camps

Members include grape growers and wineries. That structure shapes NZW's position: it cannot speak only for wineries, and it cannot speak only for growers. Purchase prices, quality requirements, sustainability standards and market promotion all have to be balanced between the two.

It is not a government body. Export and food-safety rules belong to government, while the association works on coordination, promotion, research and certification schemes. The boundary needs repeating because the standards and marks the association publishes look like official documents, while in fact they belong to industry self-regulation.

## Members, board and governance

The membership is usually given in public material as more than 600 growers and 700 wineries; these figures change with the structure of the trade and should not be treated as permanent statistics. Governance rests with a board chosen by the members, the chair speaks for the trade, and a secretariat and specialist staff run the programmes.

The head office is at Level 4, 5–7 Kingdon Street, Newmarket, Auckland 1023. The member list and the board change with elections, so the current notices and annual report are the place to look when a precise answer is needed.

## Promotion, research, data and SWNZ

First, promotion. NZW promotes New Zealand wine in overseas markets, presents a unified regional image and supports members' access to export markets. Second, research and technology: it supports work on growing, winemaking, quality and pest management, and passes technical information to members. Third, sustainability: it runs Sustainable Winegrowing New Zealand (SWNZ), which launched in 1995, established winery certification standards in 2002, and later covered vineyards, wineries, bottling and export. Fourth, data and coordination: it collects production and export figures, publishes industry reports and communicates between members and government.

SWNZ is among the association's most concrete outputs. It turns sustainability from a slogan into auditable items, so that a winery can show its practices against a shared standard. It remains a voluntary industry certification, not a mandatory government licence.

## Figures that can be checked

The checkable figures include: establishment in March 2002; more than 600 grower members and 700 winery members; SWNZ launched in 1995 and winery certification established in 2002; and, in the year to June 2024, exports of about NZ$2.1 billion, roughly 90% of production exported, sales to more than 100 countries, the sixth-largest export good and just over 1% of world wine.

These figures measure different things: a founding date, a membership scale, an annual export result. Each should travel with its year and unit rather than being compressed into “New Zealand exports a lot of wine”.

## Where the influence lies

New Zealand produces little wine by world standards but exports a high share of it, so an overseas buyer often meets the country before meeting an estate. NZW's contribution is to make the scattered trade legible: one narrative, one presence at fairs, one sustainability standard.

For small wineries, collective promotion lowers the cost of entering an overseas market; for large companies it supplies a national endorsement rather than a corporate one. The industry data the association assembles also give members a common reference for planning and negotiation.

## Limits and controversies

Representation is the first limit: growers and wineries do not always want the same thing, so the association's position can only be a compromise. Second, the membership figures move, and treating 600 and 700 as fixed facts invites error. Third, the boundary of certification: SWNZ is voluntary and no substitute for regulation, so treating the mark as a legal permit misreads its force. Fourth, exposure: climate, exchange rates, logistics and foreign trade measures are beyond the association's control. Fifth, scale: New Zealand accounts for a small share of world wine, and no amount of internal unity changes that basic magnitude.

## Common misreadings

Taking NZW for a government body; taking it for a winery association only; taking SWNZ certification for a legal requirement; taking New Zealand wine for a single style. All four share one habit: confusing an industry body and an industry standard with law. When buying, the legal information on the label still matters, as [how to start drinking wine](ref:first-wine) explains; to understand regional difference, start with [terroir](ref:terroir).
