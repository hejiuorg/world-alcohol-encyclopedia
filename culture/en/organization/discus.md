# DISCUS: The US Spirits Trade's Voice in Washington

Alcohol in the United States is governed in layers: the federal government handles tax, labelling and trade, states handle licensing, distribution and retail, and local authorities add their own operating rules. A single distillery can hardly answer every layer alone. The Distilled Spirits Council of the United States (DISCUS) therefore organises the leading producers and marketers so that the trade has one voice in policy.

## The 1973 merger and legal identity

DISCUS was founded in 1973 by the merger of three predecessor bodies: the Bourbon Institute, the Distilled Spirits Institute and Licensed Beverage Industries. The purpose was practical: to let the industry speak with one voice in Washington.

Its legal identity is that of a national trade association, not a government agency or a statutory alcohol authority. It is headquartered at 101 Constitution Avenue NW, Suite 350 West, Washington, D.C. 20001. The association may set out policy positions, testify in legislative hearings and file trade comments, but it cannot issue a liquor licence or approve a label.

## Who is on the member list, who decides

Members are mainly the leading producers and marketers of distilled spirits in the United States, with supply-chain companies also taking part. The association does not publish a fixed total, and consolidation changes the list, so pinning a number to the page would age badly.

In governance, the board of directors sets positions and priorities. In June 2026, Robinson Brown IV of Brown-Forman was elected chair and Joe Fortune of Mast-Jägermeister US vice chair; day-to-day work is led by President and CEO Chris Swonger. The advertising code has its own Code Review Board, kept separate from the policy team so that the body does not appear to write the rules and interpret them at the same time.

## From lobbying to advertising self-regulation: the output

The core output is policy advocacy. The association speaks at federal, state and local level on tax, licensing, distribution, advertising and trade rules, and files written comments on foreign trade barriers with the Office of the United States Trade Representative. For members, this work shapes the cost and speed of market access.

A second output is advertising self-regulation. The voluntary Code of Responsible Practices applies to members' spirits advertising and marketing in the United States and also covers alcohol advertising by non-member brands. It includes digital and social media guidelines, media-buying guidelines, a complaints route and published review decisions. It is not law, but regulators and platforms do refer to it.

A third output is data and research. The association publishes regular economic briefings that bring consumption, sales, export and employment figures into one house view for members and the press. A fourth is promotion and responsible drinking, including the Destination Distillery tourism platform and campaigns to reduce underage drinking and drink-driving.

## Figures that can be checked

In testimony submitted to the Office of the United States Trade Representative in May 2026, the association said the US spirits sector generates more than USD 250 billion in economic activity a year, supports about 1.7 million jobs and bought more than 2.7 billion pounds of grain from American farmers in 2024.

The same testimony gives the downturn: US domestic spirits sales fell 2.2% in 2025, the first annual decline in decades; exports fell nearly 4%; and between September 2024 and September 2025 distilleries shed 3.5% of their workforce, roughly 1000 jobs. The association's heritage page adds that more than 3,100 small distilleries have opened since the American Whiskey Trail began in 2004. These are industry estimates or member-reported figures, and citations should say so.

## Relationship with the American whiskey industry

DISCUS members cover whisky, gin, vodka, tequila and other categories, with American whiskey the most recognisable part. In trade negotiations the association pushes for Bourbon and Tennessee Whiskey to be recognised as distinctive products abroad; its testimony says such recognition now covers 45 countries. For readers, this connects to the cross-region frame of [whisky](ref:whisky), the grain and ageing rules of [bourbon](ref:bourbon), and the basic vocabulary in [Introduction to Whisky](ref:whisky-intro).

The association also treats distillery tourism as part of the industry's story. In 2004 it created the American Whiskey Trail with Mount Vernon and George Washington's historic distillery, later extended as Destination Distillery. Such projects do not change the legal definition of a drink, but they shape how consumers imagine where American whiskey comes from.

## The limits of self-regulation

However detailed, the DISCUS code is a voluntary commitment. It carries no administrative penalty, cannot suspend a licence and does not replace label approval by the federal Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau or licensing by state alcohol control boards. If a brand ignores the code, the association can mainly publish a review finding and apply industry pressure.

That leads to a long-running argument: whether advertising rules written by the regulated trade can protect the public interest. Public-health researchers generally argue that self-regulation tends to put commercial interests ahead of health goals, while the association points to its outside advisory board, public complaints and published decisions. Readers can hold both claims side by side without having to settle them. A second limit is representativeness: members are mainly large producers and marketers, and the interests of small distilleries are not always reflected equally.

## Common misreadings

- Taking DISCUS for a government regulator. It lobbies, coordinates and writes a self-regulatory code; licensing and tax remain with government.
- Taking its promotion for a quality guarantee. It speaks for a category, not for a particular bottle.
- Taking responsible-drinking work as the whole of its activity. Commercial promotion and policy advocacy are just as routine.
- Equating American spirits with bourbon. Bourbon is one type of American whiskey; gin, vodka and tequila also sit in the member map.
- Treating the association's economic figures as official statistics. Most are industry estimates, built on a different method from government data.
