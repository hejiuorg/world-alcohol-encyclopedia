# Tequila and Mezcal for Beginners: Two Agave Spirits

Tequila and mezcal both come from agave, but they are two different spirits. Tequila has a clear geographical indication and raw-material framework; mezcal covers a wider range of regions, agave species and roasting methods. Treating them as the same drink makes it easy to miss the most important flavour clues of each.

## Starting from zero

Agave is a succulent plant that stores sugar in its heart. After harvest, the heart is heated, crushed, fermented and distilled. [Tequila](ref:tequila) usually uses blue Weber agave and is produced in specific regions of Mexico; [mezcal](ref:mezcal) may use many agave species, covers more regions, and in traditional practice roasts the hearts in earthen pits, which brings smoky flavour.

"Smoky" is a common feature of mezcal but not the only one. Some mezcals are lightly smoked and show more of the agave's sweetness, herb and mineral notes; others are heavily smoky. Tequila can also show pepper, citrus and earth, but it is generally not defined by smoke. The two also differ in classification and ageing, so understanding raw material, region and process is more useful than deciding which is "better".

## Main types and differences

Tequila's common types include Blanco, Reposado, Añejo and Extra Añejo. Blanco is usually unaged or only very briefly aged, with a direct flavour suited to mixing and sipping; Reposado spends some time in oak and is rounder; Añejo spends longer and brings vanilla, caramel and wood; Extra Añejo spends longer still and is more concentrated.

Mezcal is often labelled by [agave](ref:agave) species, such as Espadín and Tobalá. Espadín is widely planted and high-yielding, a common entry point; other species may be rarer and more concentrated in flavour. Mezcal also divides into artisanal and industrial styles; artisanal bottles usually have more character, but also more batch variation.

The "worm" is not a requirement of mezcal. Some products put an agave worm in the bottle, a marketing and traditional practice, but it does not mean all mezcal has one. When buying, the agave species, region and distillation information on the label is more meaningful than what is at the bottom of the bottle.

## Choosing a first bottle

To understand tequila, start with a Blanco: clean flavour, with the agave's sweetness, pepper and citrus, and good for classics such as the Margarita. For something rounder, choose a Reposado; its wood and vanilla soften the alcohol and make sipping easier.

To try mezcal, start with an Espadín and see whether you like smoke. If you do, move to other agave species or regional styles, such as [Oaxaca](ref:oaxaca), which is well known for mezcal. Do not begin with the rarest or most expensive species; first build a reference for smoke, sweetness and minerality.

When reading the label, look at region, agave species, ageing type, alcohol content and whether it is artisanal. Tequila and mezcal both have geographical indications and official frameworks, and specific labels should be checked against the producer and local rules. If a bottle says only "agave spirit" without a type, confirm whether it is tequila or mezcal.

## How to drink and what to use

Both tequila and mezcal suit [slow sipping](ref:sipping). Room temperature, a small glass, nose first, then let the liquid cover the tongue. Blanco's pepper and citrus are more direct; Reposado and Añejo show more vanilla and caramel; mezcal's smoke and minerality need a little time to open.

Salt and lime are common rituals but not rules. Salt increases the sense of saltiness and lime adds acidity, and both change the flavour; if you do not like them, sip neat. Do not down a good agave spirit in one gulp; the heat of the alcohol will hide the detail.

Use a small [rocks glass](ref:rocks) or a narrow small glass, and pour about 30–45 millilitres for sipping. You can add one large ice cube, but ice dilutes and quietens the aroma; taste it neat first, then decide. When mixing, measure by recipe and pay attention to sourness, sweetness, ice and dilution.

## Price and buying

Agave spirit prices are affected by raw material, region, ageing, process, brand and channel; check local market and official channels for current prices. For everyday mixing, a moderately priced Blanco is enough; for sipping, choose a Reposado or Añejo with a clear ageing type and region, and do not pay too much for packaging.

Buy from reputable channels and check that the label is complete, the bottle is not leaking and the fill level is normal. Mezcal batches vary considerably; if possible, buy a small bottle or taste first before committing to a full one. After opening, seal it, keep it dark and store at room temperature; flavour will change gradually over months and years.

## Common misconceptions

First, that mezcal is a type of tequila. The two differ in raw material range, region and process, and mezcal is not a subcategory of tequila. Second, that all mezcal has a worm. The worm is an optional marketing and traditional practice, not a standard feature.

Third, that golden tequila must be aged. Colour can come from age or from caramel colouring; the ageing type should be read from the label. Fourth, that harsher and more fiery is better. A good agave spirit is judged by aroma, sweetness and [finish](ref:finish); aggression is not a quality standard.

Fifth, that salt and lime are compulsory. They are flavour choices, not drinking rules. Sixth, that alcohol strength equals quality. High-strength spirits concentrate flavour but also hide detail, and a beginner does not need to start at the highest proof.

## A path forward

First, use a Blanco to make a Margarita or Paloma and understand sour, sweet, bitter and bubbles. Second, sip Reposado and Añejo and note vanilla, caramel, wood and finish. Third, compare Blancos from different regions and feel the difference in pepper, citrus and minerality.

Fourth, try an Espadín mezcal and confirm how much smoke you enjoy. Fifth, try other agave species or artisanal batches and keep tasting notes: raw material, region, process, ageing and personal impression. Sixth, return to mixing and substitute different tequila styles in the same recipe to see how the drink changes. Progress is not buying more expensive bottles but being able to say what you like and why.
