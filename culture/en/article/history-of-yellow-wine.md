# A history of yellow wine: rice, qu, water and Shaoxing

Yellow wine is often described as “China's oldest wine”, and that is only half true. Grain-fermented wine is indeed very old, but “yellow wine” as a category name stabilised only in modern times. The ancient word “jiu” covered rice wine, cloudy wine and clear wine, and these are not the same as today's Shaoxing jiafan, Fujian laojiu or Shandong Jimo laojiu.

The problem to solve is the relation between continuity of craft and discontinuity of category. The underlying logic of brewing — rice, qu, water, fermentation — has lasted for millennia, but the scope, standards and products denoted by “yellow wine” took shape in the twentieth century. Confusing the two leads to the false conclusion that yellow wine was always as it is now.![A history of yellow wine: rice, qu, water and Shaoxing thematic illustration](/media/photos/theme-asian.jpg)

## Argument

The history of yellow wine should be read along two lines. The first is technical: from Neolithic grain fermentation to the qu-making and brewing of the Northern Wei Qimin Yaoshu, and then to the mature workshops of Ming and Qing Shaoxing, the line is continuous. The second is categorical: ancient wine names referred to raw material, colour, clarity and use, without a unified “yellow wine” class; modern yellow wine is classified by sugar content, process and alcohol strength, a product of industrialisation and standardisation.

Discussing the history therefore requires asking which technical step was inherited, which name was redefined, and which local condition cannot be reproduced. The answers lie in qu, standards and the landscape of Shaoxing.

## Evidence one: Jiahu and early fermentation

The earliest wine is grain-fermented. Residue analysis of pottery from the Jiahu site in Wuyang, Henan, shows a fermented drink of rice, honey and fruit about 9,000 years ago, with markers such as tartaric acid. This proves that early fermentation existed, but it cannot be equated directly with yellow wine: the Jiahu drink was a mixed fermentation without the later wheat qu and glutinous rice system.

The value of the evidence is as an upper limit: it shows that fermented drinks in China are very early and provides a technical background for later grain wine. It cannot prove that “yellow wine is nine thousand years old”, because the category came later. Connecting an archaeological find directly to a modern product is a common shortcut.

## Evidence two: qu and brewing in the Qimin Yaoshu

The key technology is qu. Qu saccharifies starch in grain and then lets yeast ferment it, producing a combined saccharification and fermentation. The Northern Wei Qimin Yaoshu, in chapter seven, records several qu types and matching brewing methods, one of the earliest systematic texts to survive. It repeatedly stresses the order of steeping the qu, adding rice and pitching the mash, and the choice of water, temperature and season.

“Adding rice” is the key to understanding Chinese yellow wine: steamed rice is added to the fermenting mash in stages, supplying sugar and controlling temperature and acidity. This differs from the single-charge methods of wine and beer and produces the high-gravity fermentation and ageing style of yellow wine. After the Song and Yuan, red yeast developed in Fujian and Zhejiang, providing another technical path for red yeast wine and some yellow wines.

## Evidence three: Shaoxing and the Ming-Qing market

The rise of Shaoxing wine is closely tied to local conditions. Water from Jian Lake, glutinous rice, wheat qu and wine starter form the basic combination; workshops reached scale in the Ming and Qing, and products travelled through the canal and commercial routes. Jiafan, yuanhong, shanniang and xiangxue differ by proportions and process: jiafan reduces water and adds rice for a fuller body; yuanhong is lighter in colour; shanniang uses wine instead of water; xiangxue uses distilled wine from lees instead of water for greater sweetness.

“Huadiao” is often mistaken for a type of wine. It is more about jar, painted decoration and ageing: the jar is carved and painted for weddings and gifts. The “daughter's red” story is folklore, not a production rule for all Shaoxing wine. The best way to understand Shaoxing wine is through four variables — raw material, qu, fermentation and age — rather than legend. In the Ming and Qing, Shaoxing wine enjoyed a high reputation among officials and literati, and shops specialising in it appeared in the capital and in Jiangnan cities.

## Evidence four: 1952 and the current standard

In 1952 the first national wine evaluation selected eight famous wines, and Shaoxing jiafan wine was among them. This placed a regional yellow wine in the national famous-wine system and pushed discussion of sensory evaluation and quality indicators. The national standard has since been revised, and the current [Yellow wine standard](ref:yellow-wine-standard) is GB/T 13662-2018, covering definitions, classification, technical requirements, test methods and labelling.

The standard classifies yellow wine by sugar content, process and alcohol strength, making it a testable category rather than a local reputation. For consumers it provides a minimum common language: dry, semi-dry, semi-sweet and sweet, alcohol ranges, and ingredient and origin labelling. It cannot guarantee that every bottle tastes good, but it reduces information asymmetry.

## Counterexamples and limits

First, “yellow wine equals rice wine” is too broad. Japanese sake is rice wine and Korean cloudy rice wine is rice wine, but neither falls within the Chinese yellow wine standard. Second, “the older the better” has limits. Jar storage, bottle storage, temperature and strength all affect ageing; low-strength yellow wine can sour in long storage, and heat makes aromas fade. Third, “traditional methods are safer” is false. Traditional fermentation can also face contamination, methanol and ethyl carbamate, and modern temperature control and testing are not superfluous.

Fourth, “all yellow wine comes from Shaoxing” is a misunderstanding. Fujian laojiu, Shandong Jimo laojiu and northern millet yellow wines have their own systems, and Shaoxing is only the most famous branch. Fifth, “older means more expensive” depends on labelling rules. Age-labelled wine, aged wine and caramel colouring are different things, and “aged” on a label does not mean the whole bottle spent the same number of years in storage. Sixth, treating yellow wine as a low-alcohol health drink is inaccurate: it is usually stronger than beer, and excess is harmful.
