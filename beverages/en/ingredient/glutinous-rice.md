# Glutinous rice

Glutinous rice is a type of rice that is extremely sticky because its starch is almost entirely amylopectin. In Chinese it is also called jiangmi, and in brewing it is often called wine rice. Steamed glutinous rice is soft and clumps together; it is used for foods such as rice dumplings and eight-treasure rice, and is also an important component of yellow wine, sweet rice drinks and multi-grain baijiu.

## Definition and forms

The difference between glutinous rice and japonica or indica rice lies mainly in starch structure. Glutinous rice has a very high proportion of amylopectin and little amylose, so it absorbs water quickly when cooked, is very sticky, and hardens on cooling. By grain shape it can be divided into long-grain and round-grain types; the former is common in some southern regions, the latter more common in the north and in some processing uses.

Glutinous rice also includes indica-glutinous and japonica-glutinous types, though everyday usage rarely separates them. Brewing glutinous rice is judged by grain integrity, starch content, impurities and freshness; over-milling removes outer material, but the stickiness itself comes from starch structure and does not change with milling.

## Main producing regions

Glutinous rice is grown mainly in Asia, including southern China, Thailand, Laos and Vietnam. Southern China has a rich tradition of glutinous rice varieties and cultivation, and some regions keep local landraces. Climate and rainfall affect grain filling and moisture, and therefore water uptake during steaming and fermentation behaviour.

Glutinous rice must be dried promptly after harvest or it spoils easily. It is especially sensitive to moisture during storage, because high moisture and heat accelerate quality loss. Large-scale production tests moisture, impurities and mouldy kernels, while household users should choose intact packaging and a recent production date.

## Role in yellow wine

[Yellow wine](ref:yellow-wine) is one of the most typical brewing uses of glutinous rice. Traditional yellow wine around Shaoxing is made mainly from glutinous rice through steeping, steaming, cooling, adding qu and [fermentation](ref:fermentation). The stickiness helps form a stable mash structure and gives the wine more fullness, but excessive stickiness can cause clumping, uneven heat removal and difficult pressing.

Yellow wine does not use only glutinous rice. Different regions and products use japonica rice, ordinary rice or mixed rice, and the glutinous proportion affects sweetness, thickness and yield. Treating "yellow wine must use glutinous rice" as a fixed rule ignores regional and product differences; more accurately, glutinous rice is one important raw material in traditional yellow wine.

## In baijiu and sweet rice drinks

In [baijiu](ref:baijiu), glutinous rice usually appears in multi-grain recipes. [Strong-aroma baijiu](ref:strong-aroma) commonly combines sorghum, rice, glutinous rice, wheat and maize; glutinous rice adds softness and a sweet, rounded quality. In solid-state fermentation it must be paired with sorghum, rice husks and other materials so that excessive stickiness does not harm air permeability and distillation.

Sweet rice wine and fermented rice (jiuniang) use glutinous rice as the main raw material, relying on qu to saccharify the starch before fermentation; alcohol content is usually low and sweetness is pronounced. These products and distilled baijiu belong to different categories with different goals: the former retains sugar and rice aroma, the latter pursues distillation and ageing after alcoholic fermentation.

## Raw material differences from sake

[Sake](ref:sake) also uses rice, but usually dedicated sake rice varieties and controls milling through the polishing ratio. The main sake base is japonica rice rather than glutinous rice; koji converts steamed rice starch into sugar, which then ferments in parallel with yeast. The stickiness of glutinous rice does not suit the sake process, and the two cannot simply be exchanged.

This comparison shows that "rice wine" is a broad term; the actual drink depends on raw material, qu type, fermentation method and whether it is distilled. Glutinous rice suits products seeking stickiness, sweetness and fullness, while japonica rice better suits the polishing, koji and parallel fermentation system of sake.

## Stickiness, steaming and process fit

The main process difficulty with glutinous rice is stickiness. Too little steaming leaves starch insufficiently gelatinised and saccharification inefficient; too much makes the grains clump, reduces air permeability in the mash and causes uneven fermentation. Producers control water uptake and temperature through steeping time, steaming method and cooling.

The glutinous proportion must also match the target style. A high proportion gives a softer, sweeter body; a low proportion makes fermentation easier to control and yield more stable. Multi-grain recipes are not fixed formulas but adjustments based on raw material traits, qu type, season and equipment.

## Buying and storage

Start with the form and provenance. From there, consider the preparation, then how it behaves in the drink. That is how a practical judgment about Glutinous rice is built.

Home brewers should use glutinous rice of clear origin and free of mould, and pay attention to equipment and water hygiene. Mouldy rice may contain mycotoxins that cannot be fully removed by washing or steaming, so it should not enter brewing.

## Common misconceptions

First, that glutinous rice and ordinary rice can be swapped freely. Stickiness and starch structure differ, so the process must be adjusted.

Second, that yellow wine can only use glutinous rice. Different regions and products use japonica rice, ordinary rice or mixtures.

Third, that the stickier the glutinous rice, the better. Too much stickiness causes clumping and pressing difficulties.

Fourth, that sake is also a glutinous rice drink. Sake mainly uses japonica rice and koji in a different process system.

Fifth, that sweet rice wine and baijiu differ only in alcohol content. Raw material, qu, fermentation and distillation goals all differ.
