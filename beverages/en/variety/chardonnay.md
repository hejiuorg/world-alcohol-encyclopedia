# Chardonnay

Chardonnay is a major white grape variety, botanically Vitis vinifera. Genetic research shows that its parents are Pinot Noir and Gouais Blanc. Chardonnay has a fairly neutral aroma and high adaptability, which is why it is often called the winemaker's canvas: climate, soil, fermentation temperature, lees contact, malolactic fermentation and oak use all change the final style considerably.![Chardonnay image](/media/photos/variety-chardonnay-efde6838.jpg)

Figure: Chardonnay. Image: Jenavive at English Wikipedia, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons ([source page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chardonnay.jpg)).

## Definition and identity

Chardonnay is the name of a variety, not a fixed style of wine. The same variety can be lean and mineral in Chablis, ripe and oak-scented in California, or a sparkling base in Champagne. A variety page describes botanical traits, ripening patterns and winemaking potential; a wine page describes a particular bottle's origin, vintage and process. The two cannot replace each other.

Keeping variety and wine separate prevents the name "Chardonnay" from standing in for every difference. On a shelf the consumer sees a wine; on the label they see a combination of variety, region and vintage. The variety supplies possibilities, while region and process decide which possibility is chosen.

## Botanical characteristics and parentage

Chardonnay has relatively small clusters, medium berries and thin skins, coloured from yellow-green to golden. It buds early and ripens early to mid-season, with high yield potential. Thin skins mean less pigment and tannin, which suits white winemaking; they also make the variety more sensitive to grey rot, powdery mildew and similar problems, which must be managed through canopy work, airflow and harvest timing.

The parentage explains some of its traits: Pinot Noir contributes a fairly fine structure and aromatic base, while Gouais Blanc contributes vigour and adaptability. Genetic evidence does not mean that Chardonnay is a simple mixture of the two; long vegetative propagation and vineyard selection have produced a stable combination of characteristics.

## Main regions

Chardonnay is generally considered to originate in Burgundy and its surroundings in France. Chablis is known for lean, high-acid dry whites, while the Côte de Beaune and Mâcon tend towards fuller styles; Champagne uses it as one of the three main traditional-method varieties, and there are also "blanc de blancs" wines made only from Chardonnay.

Outside Burgundy, it is planted on a commercial scale in California and Oregon, Australia, New Zealand, Chile, Argentina and South Africa, and in China it is used for dry whites and sparkling wines in Ningxia, Shandong, Hebei and Yunnan. Cool regions usually bring higher acidity with citrus and green-apple notes; warm regions lean towards riper fruit such as melon and yellow peach. Under different heat conditions the ripening curve and aroma expression of the same variety differ greatly.

## Climate and ripeness

Harvest decisions for Chardonnay consider sugar, acidity, aroma precursors and phenolic ripeness at the same time. Picking too early gives high acidity but insufficient aroma and possible grassy notes; picking too late gives falling acidity and overripe fruit, and the wine can feel heavy. Regions with large day-night temperature swings help accumulate sugar while retaining acidity, a climatic feature shared by many quality white-wine regions.

Chardonnay for sparkling base wines is usually picked earlier to keep higher acidity and lower alcohol, leaving room for the second fermentation and bubble structure. Dry whites fermented in oak may be picked slightly later for riper fruit and fuller body. There is no single answer on harvest timing; it depends on the target wine.

## Typical flavours and structure

Unoaked Chardonnay often shows green apple, lemon, pear and white-flower aromas with high acidity, a fresh palate and light to medium body. Chardonnay fermented or aged in oak gains vanilla, cream, toasted bread and nut notes; lees contact adds roundness and yeast-like flavours; malolactic fermentation converts sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid and produces buttery, creamy notes.

These treatments can be combined or deliberately avoided. Stainless-steel-only fermentation with malolactic fermentation blocked emphasises pure fruit and acidity; new oak and long lees stirring emphasise texture and complexity. Judgement should first establish the process route, then decide whether the style meets expectations.

## Winemaking choices

The first choice is whether to use oak. Barrel size, the proportion of new wood and the toast level all change aroma and tannin feel; a high proportion of new barrels makes vanilla and spice notes more obvious. The second choice is the length of lees contact and whether to stir the lees; long contact adds roundness and bread-like aroma but demands more careful hygiene. The third choice is whether to run malolactic fermentation, which lowers acidity and changes the flavour direction.

Fermentation temperature also matters: lower temperatures help preserve delicate aromas, while higher temperatures may strengthen fruit and body. Pressing intensity, whole-cluster pressing and the degree of clarification also affect mouthfeel. Sparkling base wines must additionally consider the second fermentation and ageing, so the base usually needs acidity and structure in reserve.

## Representative wines and relationships

In Burgundy, Chardonnay is used for dry whites from village level to grand cru, and differences in soil drainage, slope and aspect are clearly visible. In Champagne it is an important component of sparkling wines such as [Dom Pérignon](ref:dom-perignon), and is also used for blanc de blancs. Producers in the United States and Australia often use oak and lees to pursue a fuller style.

For Chardonnay, begin with where it is grown. Then look at how it is labelled, and finally ask whether the style is dry or sweet. The answers point to different choices, not to a single score.

## Common misconceptions

First, all Chardonnay tastes of butter. Buttery notes come from malolactic fermentation and oak treatment, not from the variety itself.

Second, Chardonnay can only make dry white wine. It is also widely used for [sparkling wine](ref:sparkling) and appears in sweet wines and some fortified wines.

Third, Chardonnay has no character. Its neutrality is exactly what makes it adaptable; style comes mainly from origin and process choices.

Fourth, "blanc de blancs" means Chardonnay. Blanc de blancs means only white varieties are used; in Champagne this usually means Chardonnay, but other regions may use other white varieties.

Fifth, the more oak the better. Oak is a seasoning and structural tool; too much masks fruit and brings a drying sensation.

**At a glance:** A major white grape variety with a neutral aroma and high adaptability; used from Burgundy dry whites to Champagne sparkling wines, with style largely determined by region and winemaking.
